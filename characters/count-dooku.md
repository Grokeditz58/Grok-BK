# Count Dooku / Darth Tyranus

Evidence file for Count Dooku (Darth Tyranus), **Star Wars Legends** continuity. Each entry is a short description of what he does or what is said about him, followed by the full quote in a code block with its source. Anti-feats and limitations are in their own section at the end, each backed by quotes.

**How to read this file**

- Quotes are copied word for word from the sources, with whitespace and curly quotes normalized. `[…]` marks an omission inside a quote.

- The source line is the last line inside each code block, so one copy gets the quote and its citation. Novels are cited with the chapter where we could find it.

- **†** after a source means the quote was transcribed from a published source that was not re-checked against our own copy of the text (film dialogue, DVD commentary, guides, comics). Everything else was matched against the text on file.

## Fights and Feats

### 17. Dooku's spinning side-stamp folds Anakin in half and he follows with a blindingly fast wheel-kick that cracks against the point of Obi-Wan's chin like a slugthrower report.

```
Kenobi reached the top of the stairs and a single slash of his lightsaber dismantled both droids. Before their pieces could even hit the floor Dooku was in motion, landing a spinning side-stamp that folded Skywalker in half; he used his last burst of dark power to continue his spin into a blindingly fast wheel-kick that brought his heel against the point of Kenobi’s chin with a crack like the report of a huge-bore slugthrower, knocking the Jedi Master back down the stairs. Sounded like he’d broken his neck.

— Revenge of the Sith novelization (Matthew Stover, 2005), ch. 3
```

### 18. Dooku meets Anakin's charge toe-to-toe, noting Anakin has lost his edge because a simple taunt shifted his attention from winning to controlling his emotions; the angrier and more afraid he gets, the more the fear feeds his anger.

```
Skywalker leapt for him again, and this time Dooku met the boy’s charge easily. They stood nearly toe-to-toe, blades flashing faster than the eye could see, but Skywalker had lost his edge: a simple taunt was all that had been required to shift the focus of his attention from winning the fight to controlling his own emotions. The angrier he got, the more afraid he became, and the fear fed his anger in turn; like the proverbial Corellian multipede, now that he had started thinking about what he was doing, he could no longer walk.

— Revenge of the Sith novelization (Matthew Stover, 2005), ch. 3
```

## Sith Power, Scheming and Leadership

### 21. In Labyrinth of Evil, Dooku on Geonosis deliberately allowed Obi-Wan and Anakin to pursue him as Sidious instructed, and told Obi-Wan of Sidious's existence as a way of confusing the Jedi Order by telling them the truth.

```
Once Master and Padawan, Kenobi and Skywalker had become the scourge of Dooku’s existence. On Geonosis he had deliberately allowed them to pursue him—just as Sidious had instructed him to do. Also as instructed, Dooku had made Kenobi aware of the existence of Darth Sidious, as a means of confusing the Jedi Order by telling them the truth. In the sloop’s docking bay he had demonstrated his mastery to Kenobi and Skywalker—although Skywalker hadn’t been as easily defeated the second time they had dueled. Enraged, the young Jedi had proved a powerful opponent, and Dooku suspected that he had grown only more powerful since Geonosis.

— Labyrinth of Evil (James Luceno, 2005), ch. 26
```

### 22. As a Master, Dooku chose the most promising Padawan, Qui-Gon Jinn, maneuvering to get him the first time he saw him in lightsaber training at ten years old, because a Master is judged by the prowess of his Padawan.

```
Dooku had learned much from Thame Cerulian. Now he was a Master with an apprentice. Qui-Gon Jinn had been the most promising of the Padawans, and Dooku had maneuvered to get him the first time he saw him in lightsaber training, at ten years old. Dooku knew that a Master would be judged by the prowess of his Padawan, and he wanted the best of the best. When Yoda had given his approval of the match, Dooku had been satisfied. Another step had been taken toward his goal — to surpass Yoda as the greatest Jedi ever.

— Legacy of the Jedi (зв) (Джуд Уотсон, 2003), ch. 7
```

### 24. Dooku taught Grievous the seven classic forms of lightsaber dueling, which made Grievous and his elite guards lethal, though not invincible because they lack finesse and can be confused by unpredictability.

But they were not invincible, not even Grievous, because they could be confused by unpredictability, and they had no understanding of finesse. A player of dejarik could memorize all the classic openings and countermoves, and still not be a master of the game. Defeat often came at the hands of less experienced players who knew nothing about the traditional strategies. A professional fighter, a combat artist, could be defeated by a cantina brawler who knew nothing about form but everything about ending a conflict quickly, without a thought to winning gracefully or elegantly.

```
— Labyrinth of Evil (James Luceno, 2005), ch. 32
```
