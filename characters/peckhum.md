## Background and Ship

### 1. Peckhum is the old man who took in Zekk and brings supplies to the Jedi academy.

```
Jaina smiled at the mention of their mischievous friend, a street urchin who had been taken in and cared for by old Peckhum, the man who brought supplies to the Jedi academy A couple of years older than the twins, Zekk had proven to be a resourceful scamp, despite his disadvantaged life.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 1
```

### 2. Peckhum has shared quarters with Zekk for years; he does odd jobs in his ship, the Lightning Rod.

```
For years, Zekk had shared quarters with old Peckhum. The thin, gray-haired man had no particular career, but spent his days doing odd jobs such as transporting cargo in his battered ship, the Lightning Rod, or performing whatever duties the New Republic required. Zekk and the old supply runner got along well and helped each other as if they were family, providing mutual support, company, and a place to stay.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 4
```

### 3. Peckhum's appearance and his junk-filled apartment.

```
The metal door slid aside to reveal a paradise of junk-rooms stacked high with salvaged items, partially restored antiques, and strange gadgets whose original use had long since been forgotten.

A grizzled old man stood up from a rickety table where he had been poring over manifest files on a scuffed datapad. He had lank gray hair, a leathery face, and a broad smile-and he very much needed a shave. “Ah, Zekk, you’re back.” He looked past the teenager. “And you’ve brought guests. Hello, my young Jedi friends.”

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 4
```

### 4. Peckhum flies the Lightning Rod up to the mirror station with Jaina, Lowie and Chewbacca.

```
STILL DEEPLY WORRIED about Zekk, old Peckhum piloted his battered supply ship, the Lightning Rod, out of its sheltered hangar. The New Republic would have provided him transportation if he’d requested it, but Peckhum liked to take his own ship, though even on its best days it functioned less reliably than the Millennium Falcon. And it had never been made to carry so many passengers.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 16
```

### 6. Peckhum rides the orbital mirrors: a lonely job that once punished disobedient stormtroopers.

```
“No, I’ve been assigned to riding the mirrors up in Coruscant orbit,”

Peckhum said. “It’s a lonely job, but the pay is good-and somebody’s got to do it. Besides, it’s relaxing … if you look at it that way.”

Peckhum snorted. “According to the design, yes. But they’re all malfunctioning. The mirror stations were set up long ago, even before the Emperor took over. During the Imperial years, riding the mirror station was a punishment assigned to stormtroopers who had disobeyed orders.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 4
```

### 7. Peckhum turns the mirrors past their maximums to expose the Shadow Academy.

```
Old Peckhum sat strapped into the command chair in the monitoring station as it dangled beneath the giant solar reflectors, working the outdated attitude adjustment controls. Jaina crouched over the chair, whispering excitedly into his ear. “Turn the mirrors,” she said. “Turn, turn, turn!”

“I’m already beyond the maximums,” Peckhum said in despair. His jaw was clenched, his neck muscles taut, and beads of sweat glistened on his brow.

“These are delicate sheets of reflective material. Well tear the solar mirrors if we whip ‘em around too fast.”

Hanging in orbit over Coruscant, the big mirrors finally swung into position, focusing their bright beam of condensed sunlight into the empty void. The mirror beam cut a swath through space like a searchlight.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 20
```

## Statements and Beliefs

### 8. Peckhum on the New Republic turning down his requests for a new multitasking unit.

```
“Don’t I know it! But those units are expensive and hard to come by I’ve requested a new system five times, and it’s always been turned down.

‘The resources of the New Republic are allocated according to greatest need,”’ he said, as if quoting from a report. “My comfort isn’t a great enough need.” He scratched his stubbled chin. “Oh well, I’ll survive. It’s a job. Last month I used some of my own credits to get a hand-held holoplayer to take up with me. It’ll do.”

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 4
```

### 9. Peckhum rants about bureaucrats and doing things for yourself with help from friends.

```
“‘You’ll just have to make do, Peckhum.’ ‘We’ve got procurement problems, Peckhum. `New equipment is expensive, multitasking units don’t grow on starflower vines, Peckhum.”’ Scratching at his chin stubble with one hand, he continued to rant, as used to talking to himself as he was to talking to Zekk.

“If I waited for those bureaucrats to get something done, why, the whole station would fall apart.” Then he smiled at the thought of Zekk’s promise of a new central multitasking unit. “Sometimes you just gotta do things for yourself … with a little help from your friends.”

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 13
```

### 10. Peckhum on Zekk's reliability.

```
That was unlike Zekk. He was more reliable than that. Others might brush him off as a young scoundrel or a street urchin, but Zekk knew his responsibilities well and always met them. He had promised Peckhum a new central multitasking unit, knowing how important it was to the mirror station. If Zekk told him he was going to do something, the boy did it.

Always.

Sure, Zekk was an orphan, a joker, a teller of tall tales, an adventurer-but he had always been a good friend, and he had always been completely reliable.

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 13
```

### 14. Turning the mirror to follow the moving station tears it; Peckhum cannot hold it.

```
Peckhum punched new coordinates into the mirror directional systems, but the sudden acceleration and the change in direction proved too much for the already-stressed silvery sheeting. The long webs of cables that held the great mirror in position tore free, and a wide gash began to open up, spilling a seam of stars and black night through the glittering reflector.

‘We can’t hold it,” Peckhum shouted. “It’s too much!” He shook his head. “We could never target a moving object anyway.” Then he looked up and moaned.

“My mirrors!”

— The Lost Ones (Kevin J. Anderson and Rebecca Moesta, 1995), ch. 20
```
