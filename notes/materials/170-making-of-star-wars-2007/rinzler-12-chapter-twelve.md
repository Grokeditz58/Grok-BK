# 170 — Chapter Twelve: Fairy-Tale Cinema (April 1977 to December 1977)

Print pp. 284–end of narrative (TOC: chapter starts p. 284). Typography from the Enhanced Edition XHTML. Quotes circa 1975–1978 unless flagged later reminiscence. Rinzler glosses flagged.

As the 25 May release nears, **Hirsch**: “George is the iron man… indefatigable… The picture is an enormous accomplishment.” Final cut simply not final until weeks before release; per Lippincott, Ladd agreed informally in early 1977 that no one at Fox would touch a frame. **Wigan**: “it was George’s idea to open the film in May. Nobody had ever opened a summer film *before* school was out… Memorial Day, that three-day weekend holiday.” **Lucas**: traditional big weekends were Christmas and the Fourth of July. “I said I want my film to be released in May for Memorial Day weekend… ‘Well, I don’t want the kids out of school; I want the kids to be able to see the movie and then talk about it so we can build word of mouth.’ They thought I was out of my mind.”

## PARTING SHOTS

End of April / early May 1977: ILM’s last photography. Shot 114, the jump to hyperspace: months in the works. **Muren** began it — a “streak thing,” shutter open, start/end camera positions for a “punch”; left production before Lucas asked the length changed; **Edlund** completes it, adding the pirate ship to the second half. “When the ship zips away into infinity… the only way that I could get it that small that fast was to use a Polaroid shot of the ship with a zoomback on it.” Peterson: *Falcon* returning from hyperspace used “Mylar junk.” Storyboard gag: YOU ARE NOW LEAVING HYPERSPACE. COME AGAIN! Luke’s exhaust-port blast: **Johnston**’s last sketch and last model built — a re-dress of the Death Star laser-tunnel model.

Title crawl massaged after De Palma’s rewrite to coincide more exactly with the opening (typography locked as printed):

> It is a period of civil war. Rebel spaceships, striking from a hidden base, have won their first victory over the evil Galactic Empire.
>
> During the battle, Rebel spies managed to steal secret plans to the Empire’s ultimate weapon, the DEATH STAR, an armored space station with enough power to destroy an entire planet.
>
> Pursued by the Empire’s sinister agents, Princess Leia races home aboard her starship, custodian of the stolen plans that can save her people and restore freedom to the galaxy.

McQuarrie one-sheet concepts circa March 1977, neither chosen. **Ellenshaw**: “The greatest sales tool this film ever had was Ralph McQuarrie’s production paintings, in my mind.” Fox pushes to replace the roll-up with a narrator reading it aloud; Lucas insists on keeping it. “I suppose there are kids that aren’t going to be able to read it… But they’re going to have to learn to read sooner or later. Maybe *Star Wars* will give them some incentive.” **Blalack**: crawl one of the last shots, “about three weeks before release”; tilting lens board and wide angle for receding STAR WARS; “the image begins to break up” plus anamorphic compression; shot “possibly six times”; “the longest single shot that we ever dealt with.”

Eleventh-hour Dia Noga head: England had filmed only the tentacle; Lucas wants more personality. **Tippett**, called by Jim Nelson: “We need another monster—it’s kind of an eyeball, with tentacles and things.” He and Berg sculpt a blinking eye; miniature trash-masher pond of Death Star/starfighter leftovers; **Berg** sits under a hole in the floor, “stuff leaking all over me, and just jam the puppet up.” Cantina exterior POV unfinished; Ellenshaw, already “finished,” is rushed a negative Tuesday for Thursday (they’re cutting negative). “I can have it about three months from Thursday”; they agree a week from Thursday; “probably the fastest matte shot I’ve ever done… probably the last bit of film to go in the picture.” Van Der Veer had tried masking Guinness and Hamill out of a side-angle (can’t be their POV with them in the shot); George unhappy.

## YOU CAN HISS THE VILLAIN, BUT THE FILM CAN’T HISS

Dolby Labs founded by Ray Dolby in England 1965; moved to the US in 1976, “right on time.” Noise reduction; dialogue easier; more complex sound design. **Lucas**: chose Dolby from the start over DBX; “the only really good stereo optical setup, and we really wanted to do the film in stereo… just a little box that they clipped onto the Nagra.” First used on *A Clockwork Orange* (1971), middling success since. **Ladd** concerned: *The Rocky Horror Picture Show* (1975) at UA Westwood, “the Dolby fell apart on opening day”; *Mr. Billion* (March preview, Tucson) “blew out the whole system.” “Don’t use Dolby!” “But… George knew how to use Dolby… designed shots that would work specifically for the sound system, which is, I think, the first time that’s ever been done.” **Burtt** satisfied; consultant Steve Katz valuable. **Shaw**: Dolby excellent for pre-dubbing hiss. Still need monaural and other stereo tracks for foreign markets and much of America.

Caption (landspeeder shadow): George wanted a shadow; Beckett tried; “immensity of trying to generate a shadow where none existed”; passed to Ellenshaw/Disney, then Van Der Veer finished it; Blalack: “Our prediction was that it would never work.” Foley cue sheets reel #1, March–April 1977, prominent DOLBY sticker.

## THE LEGENDARY SHOW—AND SECRET DUDS

Sound mix incomplete; Lucas previews for the general public **1 May** at San Francisco’s **Northpoint Theatre**. The opening Star Destroyer shot, Williams, Burtt — “the first modern fairy tale since the Western.” **Ladd**: “the best, was the strangest experience I have ever had… they just started applauding, the tears started rolling out of my eyes… at the end of the picture, it kept going on, it wasn’t stopping… I had to get up and walk outside because of the tears.” **Howie Hammerman**: “the audience just went *nuts.* They stood screamin’ and yellin’!” Regular show that night: *Slap Shot* (bowed 25 February). Afterward, Park Way table tallies comment cards (Lippincott, Alsup, Burtt, Colin Kitchens, Marcia Lucas, Pamela Malouf, et al.). **Chew** in the lobby: expected George exhilarated; first words: “Well, as you can see things haven’t changed much since you last saw the picture, and we’re still trying to make it work.” George’s father pumping hands: “Thank you… for helping out George.”

Ladd apprehensive: younger kids scared by the noise; “I sat behind a little girl and it moved too fast for her.” Lippincott: several people “walked into the lobby, gray in the face.” Rating: *Daily Variety* reports MPAA vote “split evenly between G and PG”; in an unusual move, Fox “asked for a stricter rating” — some scenes arguably scary, and concern of “backlash… from teenagers if the film were to receive a G, which is sometimes considered an ‘uncool’ rating.” **Lippincott**, typography locked: “I had a kid about five years old sitting in front of me. And when Darth Vader appears on the bridge of the Princess’s ship at the beginning and grabs the guy and chokes him, the kid began crying, and just really broke down. So I said to George, ‘This film is a PG.’ ” A friend on the board (one of two females) said the men were “absolutely bored… a couple of them fell asleep, and they all voted it a G… The only one who was concerned… was a mother… a little too intense for kids. But all the guys dismissed her… the worst thing we could possibly get.” They go back; “They had never had this done to them before by anybody… the board reneged and gave it a PG because we wanted that.”

**2 May**, Metro Theatre: “People don’t know about the next night at the Metro” (Ladd) — “not the same kind of reaction *at all.*” Dinner afterward, depressed. Next reaction: Fox board screening. **Warren Hellman**: little paper — “*Star Wars* will 1) Be a breakout film, 2) Return our money, 3) Lose all of our money.” He was going to check the center; wife Christina: “Are you crazy?! This is the best movie I’ve ever seen!” He checks column one. **Wigan**: sixteen board members; “three of them really loved it; three… maybe it would work, two of them fell asleep… and the rest of them really hated it… very distressed indeed.” Hellman: “Supposedly only two directors voted for ‘breakout.’ Most… neutral to negative.” Radio and record screening: “about ten people walked out…” (Ladd).

## THE LAST AUDIBLE GASP

Mid-May, ten days from release, final mix still incomplete. Foley at Glen Glenn, Stage A, March (hundreds of legal-pad pages). ADR April, Producers’ Sound Services, North Highland. Pre-dubs at Burbank Studios; also Warner Bros. Stage 5; final mixing at Goldwyn. **Shaw**: Foley per reel “the equivalent to an entire normal feature film”; eight or nine mixers instead of the usual three. **Burtt**: blending the biggest frustration; mixers “weren’t the filmmakers that had been on it for a couple of years”; “We thought the sound was about 30 percent successful in view of what we intended to have.” First mix out at the last second: six-track Dolby stereo, “the most errors”; two-track derived from it, no time to change. Burtt asks Katz; “too afraid to mess with it… kind of an experiment.” A week before opening, still no answer print; by **24 May**, “just in time—as Lucas says, the film wasn’t finished, it was ‘abandoned.’ ”

## SWR 05.25.77

**Wednesday, 25 May 1977**: theatrical run begins in **thirty-two theaters**, including the Coronet, San Francisco. 26 May: Glenwood, Kansas City, Kansas, added. 27 May: ten more. In each of fewer than fifty houses: “Joy.” Coronet: applause as soon as the spaceships arrive; cheering never stops “except at times to boo the villain, until halfway through the end credits.”

Lucas still on the **fourth final mix, the monaural**. “We’d finished the 70mm eight-track stereo mix, which Fox had resisted, and we were working on the monaural version for the wide release, so I was mixing at night… sleeping all day in a little house that Marcia had on the flats of Hollywood. She was working on *New York, New York.*” The night before they finish the mono mix, dinner at a **Hamburger Hamlet on Hollywood Boulevard**. “I could see this huge crowd in front of Grauman’s Chinese—limos—and I thought someone must be premiering a movie. It never occurred to me that my movie was out, because *I was still working on it.* … I looked up at the marquee and said, ‘Oh my God, it’s *Star Wars*! I forgot the film was going to be released today! Holy moly!’ But it was like six o’clock, so I had to go back to the studio to finish the mix.” Never supposed to play Grauman’s; **William Friedkin’s *Sorcerer*** (due 25 May, bowed 24 June) wasn’t finished. (Grauman’s, built 1927, called Mann’s Chinese 1973–2001.)

Back at Goldwyn, *Los Angeles Times* staff writer **Gregg Kilday** observes a call to Ladd: “Wow … wow … gee … that’s pretty amazing,” while offering instructions to engineers. **Lucas**: “Hey, I forgot the movie was going to be released today… there were people around the block…” Ladd: “It’s a giant hit everywhere…” Lucas: “Wait—calm down. Remember, science-fiction films do really great the first week, then they drop off to nothing… Let’s wait a couple of weeks.” Ladd keeps calling all night. To Kilday: “I’m still going to hold my breath for a few weeks… The movie’s only been released for five hours. I don’t want to count my chickens before they hatch.” Limos were **Hugh Hefner** and entourage, who watched twice in a row. **Burtt**: George, Hirsch, and crew list stereo-mix dislikes and try to fix them on the mono — “different voices for some of the stormtroopers, some new loop lines for Luke, minor changes.” **Lucas** calls **Hamill** that very night. Hamill also stupefied by the Grauman’s lines: “Imagine—the ultimate Hollywood theater… But believe it or not, the night the picture opened, George called and said, ‘Hey, kid, do you want to come down and loop?’ … ‘What are you talking about? It’s playing. There are lines around the block!’ … the print being shown was the 70mm stereo mix, and now for the monaural mix… he wanted me to add a few things. Can you believe it? The day it opened…” Hamill presumably loops another night. Lucas to Kilday: “I expect it all to fall apart next week… I start out by expecting things to be the worst they can be…” Marketing studies: women generally ignore SF; lobbying for a campaign to capture “female fancies”; confers on a new *Gone with the Wind*–style poster. Visitors: Marcia with two bottles of champagne; Jay Cocks; **De Palma** congratulates and jokes, “Shall we call Francis and spread the gloom?” **Lucas**, typography locked: *Star Wars* was a disappointment. “I expected more than any human being could possibly deliver. A Wookiee could have delivered it, but not a human being. I needed a crew of Wookiees… I felt the same about *Graffiti*… Obviously, from everyone’s reaction, it was good enough… Pretty hard to believe, though, definitely beyond my capacity to understand.” Mixes until dawn.

Publicity: Howard Chaykin poster sold at cons, later modified for the first Marvel *Star Wars* cover (March 1977, six-part series). Novelization “the fastest sellout of a first edition for a sci-fi movie novelization [Ballantine] had ever had” (Lippincott); sold out by February after December 1976 publication, edited by Judy Lynn. *NYT* newspaper ad 15 May 1977; Tommy Jung artwork. Teasers in Steranko’s *Mediascene* (Nov–Dec 1976), *American Film* (April 1977), *Rolling Stone* (May 1977). *Variety* Friday 27 May.

## HAUNTING HAWAII

Grauman’s biggest opening day in fifty years: **$19,358** (*Variety*); ~$4 a ticket ≈ 4,800 viewers (tickets $2.00–$4.50). Eight records in the other thirty-one theaters; grand single-day total **$254,809**. Lucas works the mix that night and the next, then leaves for Hawaii with Bill and Gloria Huyck and Marcia. Sees Walter Cronkite talking about *Star Wars*. Told Laddie he didn’t want to hear; Ladd calls every three or four days. Papers after about a week, just as the Huycks leave and **Steven arrives**. Six-day box office $2.5 million; 2 June, $3 million (*Variety*). On the beach, building a sand castle: Spielberg wants to do a James Bond film; Lucas has “something better”: **Indiana Jones**. **Spielberg**: George called away about Friday/Saturday grosses; “The 9:30 AM shows were sold out to a seat.”

Reviews (selected, as quoted): Art Murphy / *Variety*: “a magnificent film… equal the genius of Walt Disney, Willis O’Brien… sweeps away the cynicism that has in recent years obscured the concepts of valor, dedication, and honor.” Ron Pennington, *Hollywood Reporter*: “thrilling audiences of all ages for a long time to come.” Siskel (*Chicago Tribune*) good; Champlin, *LA Times*: “Buck Rogers with a doctoral degree but not a trace of neuroticism or cynicism…” Actors less noticed than FX, all praised, especially Guinness. Several liken C-3PO to Edward Everett Horton; Gary Arnold, *Washington Post*, compares R2-D2 to Harpo Marx; “jaunty rather than portentous… Lucas doesn’t linger” as Kubrick did over the *Clockwork Orange* nightclub. Vincent Canby, *NYT*, day after opening: “an apotheosis of Flash Gordon serials and a witty critique… *Quo Vadis?*, Buck Rogers, *Ivanhoe*, Superman, *The Wizard of Oz*, the *Gospel According to Saint Matthew*, the legend of King Arthur…” *Time*: “The Year’s Best Movie.” *Newsweek* positive. Positive papers in many cities; negatives in Augusta, Maine; Pascagoula, Mississippi; Statesville, North Carolina; *The Harvard Crimson*; John Simon in *New York*. *The New Yorker* favorable, then negative months later. Stanley Kauffmann, *New Republic*, 18 June: “made for those (particularly males) who carry a portable shrine within them of their adolescence…”

**Racism accusation**: “it didn’t have any African American actors in it, with the exception of the ‘black’-robed villain Darth Vader, who, either conveniently or paradoxically, was voiced by a prominent black actor.” (Rinzler paraphrase of the press charge — flag as such, do not collapse.)

**Triumph of the Will** rumor (throne room). **Lucas**, typography locked: “The truth of that particular situation is that I hadn’t seen it for about fifteen years. I had wanted to see it again because, early in the writing process, I was thinking of doing a scene with the Emperor on the Empire planet, and I wanted to do that like *Triumph of the Will*. But it unfortunately got published that I was going to try to see *Triumph of the Will* and use it in *Star Wars*; evidently somebody read that somewhere and then looked at the end of the movie and thought that looked just like *Triumph of the Will*. But the end of the movie is just what happens when you put a large military group together and give out an award.” Canby later defends his positive review (*Star Wars* and the musical *Annie*): “This is not a film of the Non-Think Age.”

## WORD OF MOUTH

*Variety* 22 June 1977: LA telephone operators “avalanched” for Avco and Mann’s Chinese numbers — about 100 calls an hour, most in ten years. 29 June: accumulated $8,780,435, knocked from #1 by *The Deep* and *Exorcist II—The Heretic*. 27 July: 48 theatres, recaptures the pole after eight weeks, $18,220,716. Limited publicity; gigantic lines fueled by person-to-person talk. Audience quotes: Ronnie Reed, Oskaloosa, Iowa; five-year-old Darren Pierce (“the monsters the best”); 23 June, Chico, CA, 500 turned away from the 900-seat El Rey, line started five hours early (Richard Gorman); Monika Caldwell, 11, Clarksville, TN; Dennis Moore, 14; Harry Stenzhorn, San Bernardino: “They didn’t give us any money for advertising… People really like the good guy/bad guy thing, kind of like a Western.” All ages; in Saint Louis, more fathers accompanying children than usual. Magazine covers: *American Cinematographer* (July 1977 — “a unique, composited image shows Darth Vader’s ship actually being fired on by a Rebel X-wing”), *People* (18 July), *Famous Monsters* (October), *Newsweek* (26 December), *Cinefantastique* (1978), *Mad* (January 1978). Shortly after release, Meco Monardo’s 45 disco version becomes a Top 40 hit.

## DIRECTORS ON *STAR WARS*

**Coppola**: finished form “very compelling… the inspiration from Flash Gordon, with a little bit of *Hidden Fortress.* … all art… comes from something before… which you borrow and make your own.” **Kurtz**: sent Kubrick a 35mm print; “he said he liked the movie… His children really enjoyed it.” **John Korty**: “the mythic dimension… grand myths of the past… recasting them… especially kids.” **Saul Zaentz** open letter in *Variety* 6 June, Fantasy Film stationery: “You have given birth to a perfect film…” **Ballard**, back in town: “a transformation had taken place.” **Spielberg**: “I had never experienced special effects that were so real… amazing and depressing at the same time,” in post on *Close Encounters*; “I thought my movie just paled… depressed for two weeks. I didn’t even want to release my movie in the same *year*… *Star Wars* was the star of 1977… a deeply spiritual story, yet somehow he made a war movie, too… he touched something that needed touching in everybody.” In Hawaii they **swapped profit points** on each other’s films, each sure the other would make more; “I’m very happy to say that I won the bet” (Spielberg). **Ridley Scott** (*The Duellists* just finished) at Grauman’s: “I don’t know what we’re doing—this guy’s making *Star Wars*? I’m not even in the same universe…” Next: *Alien* (1979), *Blade Runner* (1982). New generation: **James Cameron** “quit my job as a truck driver”; **Frank Darabont** “blew my socks off”; **Peter Jackson**: “one of the most exciting experiences that I ever had in my life… the first time that I saw science fiction presented in a way where everything was beaten up and oily and greasy and grimy—and you just believed it.” **John Singleton**, nine years old: “Anyone who knows me knows that my life was changed at that moment.” **Scorsese**: “the classic retelling of man’s mythology.”

## World Comedy

By 13 October 1977, opened in **910 theaters** in Canada and the US (last three: Rock Rapids, Iowa; Cordele and Douglas, Georgia). Foreign dubs: French, German, Italian, Spanish; subtitles Chinese, Arabic, Japanese. Most complete FX mixes end up overseas (mono improvements remixed in stereo); twenty overseas mixes. **Burtt**, not speaking the languages, “became somewhat of a mime… scribbling little pictures and dancing around in front of the mixing console.” European debut October. **Belgium**: “incredibly violent”; first day adults only; second day, new prints with cuts **authorized by Lucas** — “the severed arm, the sound of the Rebel officer’s neck cracking, the two skeletons.” 23–26 October, Lippincott and Hamill in Hong Kong for the premiere. Coda: after seeing *Star Wars*, Dino De Laurentiis buys Flash Gordon rights, offers the movie to Fellini, who turns it down, saying “George Lucas had already done it” (Lippincott).

## CAST AND CREW TAKES

**Cushing**: “I was absolutely knocked for six… ‘Never work with children or animals.’ Now we have to add to that, ‘or special effects.’ … My only real disappointment was that poor old Tarkin was blown up at the end, which meant I couldn’t appear in any sequels.” **Guinness**, with nine-year-old grandson at a trade screening: “He was a little scared and wanted to know if I was going to be killed. I wouldn’t tell him. The dialogue is pretty childish but the film has a lovely, fresh, innocent quality.” To *Seattle Post-Intelligencer*, **25 September 1977**: “My life is structured around making movies so I can do a play. I don’t imagine things will change very much even now.” **Ford**, typography locked: thanks to the film, anticipates stopping carpentry. “All of the characters I’ve played up until now have not been audience-identification type characters. They have been plot devices… It’s playing the kind of charm [of Han Solo] that wins people in casting offices. It’s certainly better career-wise to have done something that had an element of romance in it. I told George: ‘You can’t say that stuff. You can only type it.’ But I was wrong. It worked.” Spielberg ad in *Variety* 2 December 1977 congratulating Lucas that *Star Wars* has surpassed *Jaws*, becoming number one of all time. **Fisher**: “I used to drive by and look at the lines and think, *What?!* I haven’t had a chance to absorb the madness.” **Dianne Crittenden** (1978): actors who auditioned in 1975 now say they didn’t realize how important it was. **Kurtz** to *The Washington Star*, May 1977 press trip: “We’re really surprised it’s doing so well… a hard-core science-fiction-buff audience… surprised at how big it has turned out to be.” **Burtt** sees it in a hometown shopping-center theater: “miserable… Weak and dead, like an old radio broadcast.” **Barry**: “There is really only one sort of movie magic, and it’s called *directing*… The jump to lightspeed is an example of conjuring…” **McQuarrie**: first picture he saw through to completion; didn’t realize the value of the production illustrations “until I saw them on the screen.” **Ladd**, elected to Fox’s board late July 1977: “George’s concept was to really just throw those things away, not dwell upon them… The whole thrust of the film was movement… All of our concerns evaporated once we saw the final picture.” **Lucy Wilson**: restaurants talking about the person you work for; “in two years, you know, is there a future?”

## ILM FINALS

**Blalack**: “fourteen thousand pieces of film… The final count is 560 bluescreen shots compressed into 365 total shots, and it’s all one generation removed, except for six shots.” Dykstraflex: less than twenty-four hours of downtime for the whole production. ILM spent **$3,619,601**, $2 million over the studio-imposed ceiling, $1 million over the original estimate; revised final outside opticals $273,369. Fox *Variety* ad 16 September 1977: studio’s number one of all time. People’s Choice, Favorite Motion Picture of 1977, awarded 1978. **Lucas**: first “real special effects… I don’t think we took them as far as they can go.” **Dykstra**: “fabulously, considering… I really respect all of the stuff that George did.” **Edlund** quotes George: “I wore my favorite clothes, and never has so much money and time been spent on fun.” **Muren**: “difficult for me to accept George’s idea of having the starfighters move at a tremendous speed and, of course seeing the film now, I agree with him completely.” Johnston at the DMV: someone sees the line, “Shit, they ain’t playin’ *Star Wars* here, man!” Kids ask model builders for autographs. **Lind**: once people knew it was “a really good film, things just started disappearing… everybody put locks on the doors.” She locates the negative at Hollywood Film Company, Seward Street: B&W opticals vaults 203 and 204; entire-film negatives vault 356. Last duty done, taxi to the airport in a bathing suit and sandals, bound for Hawaii.

## SEVEN STORIES FOR ONE DEAL

**Hellman**: Fox, in a very few years, from “right on the edge” to very profitable. Stock from a low of **$6** a share in June 1976 to nearly **$27** immediately after release (Aubrey Solomon, *Twentieth Century-Fox: A Corporate and Financial History*). *Variety* 22 June 1977: 7,808,400 shares traded since 23 May, exceeding 100 percent of common stock. Insider-trading rumor. **Wigan**: almost no one at Fox who believed it would be huge had any stock; he thinks the move was people expecting a *catastrophe* and a management change — “a move to acquire stock with a view to a takeover.” Fox penny-pinches to the end. Final budget **$11,293,151**, $3 million over the imposed budget (fairly close to at least one early projection); studio took part of Lucas’s director fee. **Kurtz**: “George was committed personally for $15,000 of his salary, as a penalty against the budget overage… they didn’t reimburse him until after the movie came out.” Sequel and merch rights grow in importance. **Jake Bloom**: George determined products and had “complete creative control on how the artwork looks, how the packaging looks, what ads would be used” — not true under traditional pre-picture Hollywood deals. Marvel deal (Lippincott): Roy Thomas / Howard Chaykin “legitimacy in certain circles.” Bloom: “Studios minimized the comic fan, but George felt they were important in creating a base of hard-core fandom.” **Tom Pollock**, 10 August 1977, after talking to Fox business affairs: “they told me that there are seven different stories circulating about why those rights were given to George—when they had never been given to anybody else before, have never been given to anybody else since, and will never be given to anyone in the future… they gave us half the merchandising. It was George’s idea and I give it all to George.” Bloom at San Diego Comic-Con, summer 1977: already “avid collectors who will be there forever.” Lippincott: first completed-film screening, the big theater on the Fox lot, Friday before opening, daytime, for the *Variety* critic who “blew his top” because Lippincott invited 20 people from the sci-fi and comic-book world; they called friends over the weekend, “the spark really took off.” Soundtrack ~$9, platinum, 650,000 copies by mid-July. General Mills (Kenner) stock up on the rumor of the toy license (toys not until after holiday 1977). Lucas gives points or percentages: “the actors, composer, and crew should share in the rewards” — among them Kurtz; the Huycks; Pollock, Rigrod, and Bloom; Guinness, Fisher, Hamill, Ford; Burtt, Fred Roos, John Williams.

News of a sequel reverses the table. **Hellman**: “when George’s agent came back for *Star Wars* two, he said, ‘Okay, one: The profit split is being reversed. Two: You’re going to donate the product rights to us. And three: You’re still not getting any sequel rights.’ … a great board meeting… ‘We have to give George the rights to his characters.’ But we had a bunch of lawyers who said, ‘You can’t simply give away property rights!’ We voted, and I think it was a six to five vote, because somebody had made the point that you can’t own his children.”

1978 Oscars and parties. Special Achievement, Best Sound Effects: Burtt (flanked by C-3PO and R2-D2). Williams, Best Original Score. Also: Barry, Reynolds, Dilley, Christian, Best Art Direction/Set Decoration; Stears, Dykstra, Edlund, McCune, Blalack, Best Special Effects; Hirsch, Marcia Lucas, Chew, Best Editing; MacDougall, West, Minkler, Ball, Best Sound. Nominations: Lucas, Best Director and Best Screenplay; Kurtz, Best Picture; Guinness, Best Supporting Actor. Imitations: Disney *Space Station One* ($10 million, teens) and *The Cat from Outer Space*; Universal/Paramount new *When Worlds Collide*; Universal remake of *The Thing*; Future General inundated. Rinzler: “To this day, Leonard Nimoy credits *Star Wars* with getting the first *Star Trek* film made”; in 1977 a new *Star Trek* TV series announced at an LA fan convention.

## NO BUSINESS LIKE SHOW BUSINESS

Repeat viewers key to first-run longevity; some attend more than fifty times; before consumer video, only in theaters. By 13 September, Bend, Oregon, an estimated 50 percent of each audience repeat. Fox terms still thin for exhibitors: guaranteed 12-week run, 90 percent after overhead, $65,000 guarantee. April 1977 Justice Department ruling requires theaters to bid (no more “split agreements”). Town Theatre, “Rocklander, New York” [as printed], opens 5 August; figures as reported by Jan Herman, *Journal-News*. By 27 November, 90,000 people at 240 showings — roughly one-third of the city’s population. Half of Benton County, Oregon (~6,750) by 29 July. Cinema II, Danbury, CT, at least 18 weeks. Hamilton, Texas, 10 November: Texan Theatre manager Lem Guthrie, *second* week — “which had never *ever* happened there before” (one showing a night, $2). Prints worn out; popcorn “like never before.” Eventually yields to new films, e.g. *Close Encounters*. 20 October: leaves Waterloo’s Strand, Cedar Falls, Iowa, after record 18 weeks, replaced by *The Last Remake of Beau Geste*; Mentor, Ohio, 22 December, *The Goodbye Girl*. November, San Francisco: Fox and Columbia bicker over the Coronet; **Kurtz**: “still doing $55,000 a week”; *CE3K* goes to the Northpoint. Denver mid-December: *Star Wars* moves from the Cooper to the Continental for *Close Encounters*.

## THE FIRST TRILOGY:*THX 1138, AMERICAN GRAFFITI*, AND *STAR WARS*

[Heading typography as printed, no space after the colon.] Rinzler gloss (flag): at least one critic per hundred fans; Lucas at the 2005 AFI Life Achievement Award joked he was the “King of Wooden Dialogue”; many consider him only a master of (now) visual effects. “while Lucas was amazed that no one had done a film like *Star Wars* before him, only a few have done one since.” ILM techniques “not completely groundbreaking, or all that effective”: Trumbullflex before Dykstraflex; Muren thinks they could’ve done better; Lucas found the effects disappointing; some release-era experts saw nothing revolutionary at that level. Contrast: Disney’s *The Black Hole* (formerly *Space Station One*, 1979), also with Ellenshaw mattes — “the film Twentieth Century-Fox was afraid they were getting”: “robots with eyes painted on them, cardboard characters, slight themes, and corny special effects,” including a copy of the opening shot. Johnston impressed by exactness of each ship’s pan, rotation, bank. “mainstream simplified cinema was actually an amalgam of abstract and cinema verité filmmaking with aspects taken from… Michael Curtiz and John Ford—all enhanced by Lucas’s editorial artistry.”

**Lucas**, typography locked: “THX and Luke are both Buck Rogers. One is Buck Rogers today and the other is Buck Rogers in fantasy… Just two sides of the same coin. It’s the same heroism… accepting responsibility for one’s actions. That’s the one thing I think that ties them together, and which ties them to *American Graffiti*. In a way all three of my movies have been about the same theme. *THX 1138* looked at it from the point of view of a twenty-year-old. *American Graffiti* moved the perspective back to that of a sixteen-year-old. And now *Star Wars* tells the story from a twelve-year-old’s point of view… I made seven movies in film school while everybody else was complaining… Well, those people are still stuck. They didn’t realize that all you have to do is just do it.”

Rinzler gloss (flag, do not collapse into Lucas): *Star Wars* develops a “more tangible mystical side.” Droids’ arrival and Owen’s purchase start Luke “toward his highest dreams of himself.” “Where he is drifting in the beginning, he is awakened by the end. As is Han Solo, although his transition is often overlooked—but he, too, goes from an ambiguous materialism to an acceptance of responsibility. It’s not an accident that Lucas dressed them similarly for the end ceremony.” “Psychologically speaking, Luke has to rid himself of his surrogate fathers—Uncle Owen, Darth Vader, and even Obi-Wan—before he can be truly independent.” Curt / THX / Luke all independent at the end, looking out on the future. “While Lucas would go on to add many shades of gray to the future saga, *Star Wars* is really the third chapter in the trilogy of his youth… Luke is the future and mystical knight-king who leaves his farm behind—but gains a new family in Solo, Leia, Chewbacca, and the droids. Not to mention the Rebel Alliance. He is happily in the world.”

## MANY FUTURES FOR THE SHOGUN

**Lucas**: “Making a movie is very much like constructing a house… I ran the race and I didn’t do as good as I should have by a long shot… I certainly fell far short of what I wanted. And nothing will change that… when I saw the first cut, my only opinion was that I did a terrible job, but it works. It doesn’t work very well, but it works. That was reconfirmed when I saw the trailer… *This is the movie I set out to make.* Spaceships flying around and monsters and craziness. And then when I finally saw it with an audience for the first time, I realized that no matter how far short I fell… the film did work for an audience… I feel a movie is very binary: It either works or it doesn’t work. And it did work.”

Summer and fall 1977: time off; celebratory dinner with Fox board on a sound stage, seated next to Princess Grace’s daughter Caroline; more Lippincott interviews. Future plans: “More than anything else, I am interested in seeing the genre of science-fiction adventure become strong, like the Western… I don’t want to be a slave of technology… as free as the literary side of the genre, which is asking an awful lot. I think science fiction still has a tendency to make itself so pious and serious, which is what I tried to knock out in making *Star Wars*. Kubrick did the strongest thing in film in terms of the rational side of things, and I’ve tried to do the most in the irrational side of things because I think we need it. I would feel very good if someday they colonize Mars, when I am ninety-three years old or whatever, and the leader of the first colony says: ‘I really did it because I was hoping there would be a Wookiee up here.’ ”

Tells the press several times he will retire from directing; exhausted; friends not surprised. **Ford**: “I don’t think you’ll see any change… I don’t see any change in George after his success.” USC *Daily Trojan*, **9 December 1977**, Rori Benka, closed-door visit: still disappointed in the film; “I’m retired now, I’ve done it… I’ve decided to set a year or so aside to enjoy those distractions. Plus, I’m setting up a company and getting the sequels off on the right track.” Sequels’ ILM closer to home, San Rafael; Van Nuys stay-behinds become **Apogee** (Shepherd, Dykstra, Alexander, Alvah Miller, McCune, Peterson, Edlund — latter two rejoin ILM about a year later). Apogee: intended a feature (Shepherd) that never happened; *Caddyshack* (1980), *Firefox* (1982), *Battlestar Galactica*, commercials. **Muren** north: “There’s no way at the moment that this equipment can do a stop-motion dinosaur…” Rinzler: later *Jurassic Park* (1993), one of his seven Oscars. **Shepherd** on Johnston: “going to be known… He might art-direct an important movie…” Later directs *Honey, I Shrunk the Kids* (1989), *Jurassic Park III* (2001), *Hidalgo* (2004). Later careers compressed (Rinzler later-gloss, flagged as such): Ford / Indiana Jones / AFI 2000; Williams *Schindler’s List* 1994; Hirsch *Ray* 2004 nomination; Mollo *Gandhi* 1983 after *Alien*; Baker six Oscars; McQuarrie *Cocoon* 1986; Ralston five others; Burtt *E.T.* 1982; Dykstra *Spider-Man 2* 2004; Ladd *Braveheart* 1996; Lucas Irving G. Thalberg, 1992. **John Barry** dies 1 June 1979, “shortly after making *Star Wars*, while at work on the first of the sequels.” **Adam Beckett** dies in a fire, 1978. Dykstra 1978 prediction: “The next step is digital, because then you can do anything you want.” Lucas wouldn’t direct again for two decades; dedicated years and millions to digital editing, sound, cameras, “as well as what has become Pixar Animation Studios.” **Scorsese**: “If one wants to know the future of the film industry, one goes up to San Francisco and visits George. It’s as simple as that.”

Closing Lucas quote (typography locked, mid-sentence italics as printed): *“I’m interested in cinema, the psychological process, why certain films work and others don’t.* Film is magic. *That’s all it is. You’re sitting there with one hand doing one thing, while you’re fooling the audience with the other hand.”* —George Lucas
