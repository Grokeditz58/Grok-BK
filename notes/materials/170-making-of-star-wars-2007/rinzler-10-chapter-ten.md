# 170 — Chapter Ten: Ace-People and the Wizards (July 1976 to December 1976)

Print pp. 218–247 (TOC: chapter starts p. 218; Chapter Eleven starts p. 248). Typography from the Enhanced Edition XHTML. Quotes circa 1975–1978 unless flagged. Rinzler glosses flagged.

A shattered Lucas stops in Mobile, Alabama, to visit Spielberg on *Close Encounters*, and in New York to see Brian De Palma; he gives black-and-white production-still booklets to friends including Jay Cocks. **Spielberg**: “he was so depressed… He didn’t like the lighting; he didn’t like what his cameraman, Gil Taylor, had done for him.” In LA he screens *Carrie* with Paul Hirsch and De Palma. Returns to ILM **1 August 1976**. Rinzler flags a 2005 speech at ILM’s 30th anniversary (Presidio / Letterman Digital Arts Center): “When I got back, they’d spent half their budget, and had only one shot in the can. There were 45 people and about 360 shots left—and they thought they’d be able to finish the film easily in only eight months.” **Lucas**, typography locked: “On my way back from ILM, I started getting severe chest pains, since my life was collapsing around me. I had to recut the movie from scratch and ILM hadn’t created a single shot that was viable… I went straight from the airport to the hospital.” Overnight; not a heart attack; discharged.

McQuarrie: 19–22 July 1976, “roughs for S.W. book cover”; finishes the illustration **11 August 1976**, then “I.L.M. *Star Wars* T-shirt art.” By this point Lucas has dropped *The* from the title; it is *Star Wars*.

Lucas’s first ILM conclusion: it is being run as a research facility, not a production unit. Equipment late; explosion tests bad; they wait for rushes before breaking a set. **Kurtz**: “George ended that as soon as we got back. No holding any shots… We can’t worry about the rushes.” **Edlund**: “We didn’t even know how to do a bluescreen shot at the beginning of *Star Wars*.” He brings in Bill Reinhold (*It’s a Mad Mad Mad Mad World*, 1963) to teach Blalack bluescreen. Original ILM plan (abandoned in principal) was to finish the Death Star escape first; now they must cut a work print of the bluescreen elements already shot so ILM can work backward. **Lucas**: “I really enjoy editing the most… Everybody has their ace in the hole—mine’s editing.” **Ford** (having seen *American Graffiti* before and after): “That’s one of George’s great virtues: putting a movie together to create an experience for the audience… It’s not fully resolved until it’s done by him.” No one has been editing for several months; they reconstitute the English cut into dailies and start from scratch. **Richard Chew** is hired; he and **Marcia Lucas** race for a first rough cut by Thanksgiving.

## CUTTING GUNS

Chew met Lucas through John Korty; he had been Lucas’s first editorial choice the previous year; had offers after *Cuckoo’s Nest*. “George wanted the whole thing redone. He asked Marcia to work on the final battle sequence, so ILM could start, and he needed someone else to start at the beginning.” Cut tightly so no FX footage is wasted. **Mary Lind** (Film Control, Van Nuys, started August 1975 via Jim Nelson at Schwab’s): eventually 23 cross-referenced notebooks; “about 60,000 feet of live-action VistaVision out of England… reduced from 8-perf to 4-perf.” Mechanical work ~four weeks, plus two weeks coding (code 400 in 4-perf = 800 in VistaVision). English assembly damaged: “As though people hadn’t cared… slamming it all together and saying, ‘Screw this job!’ ” (Chew). Waiting, Chew starts on the Rebel ship and droids: no lip-sync on robots; for Artoo, “either George or I would say, ‘Phoove-orvr-ovrrrrrr!’ ” George: lock the escape-from-Death-Star sequence by **15 September**; “don’t look at the English cut.” Guide: a black-and-white dupe of World War II dogfight newsreel. First three days, Chew grease-pencils TIE paths, explosions, laser fire. Wild-card lines (“Here they come,” “Do you hear me, baby, hold together”) can be shifted. After a rough cut, Lucas takes over the gunport on his upstairs Steenbeck, weekends, locks on schedule. **Lucas**: “It was too hard to explain what I wanted, so I just cut the whole thing.” Lind: when those rolls hit Film Control, “18 hours a day just figuring out numbers. There were people sleeping here.” Kit-bashing: ILM spent about $1,700 on art supplies and $2,900 on model kits.

## CONTRACT FOR VICTORY

**Tom Pollock**: “Kissinger always said that the essence of negotiating is having an ass that doesn’t get tired.” After years of talks (Andy Rigrod and Pollock vs. Mort Smithlise, Donald Loze, Lyman S. Gronemeyer, William Immerman, and production people), the seventy-four-page “long-form” contract is signed **27 August 1976** — more than a month after principal photography (they had raced to see which would finish first). **Rigrod**: endless sessions in a huge paneled room, four or five revisions; “the most lengthy, complicated, and difficult contract negotiation I’ve ever seen.” Thrust: Lucas controls all versions “with as little interference by the studio as possible.” Most important: **Exhibit D** begins, “[The Star Wars Corporation] owns all Sequel Motion Picture rights in and to the picture.” Lucas also maintains control over licensing of the movie and sequels; merchandising profits (historically T-shirts and posters) split fifty-fifty for *Star Wars.* In-contract glossary required.

## BATTLE STATIONS

**Burtt** had had little feedback; still thought Walter Murch would take over. Kurtz would say, “Now a real sound effects editor is going to run the picture…” In August they ask him to San Anselmo for a week or two of temp effects; “I went to San Anselmo and stayed for seven months instead of three weeks.” September ILM changes: Shepherd gone since March; Nelson + Dykstra “didn’t work out well” (Kurtz). **Kurtz** on Dykstra: “He didn’t like yelling at people… technical… the broadest range… but he didn’t like to push people… All of ILM had that college fraternity feel.” **Dykstra**: “about 125 people passed through here… we didn’t fire many… we did lay a lot of people off… Someone would come downstairs and say, ‘Up in roto—they’re killing each other.’ ” Fox pressure eased slightly by *The Omen* (released **25 June 1976**) but not much. **Kurtz**: “The only way we could placate Fox was to invent paperwork.” Average five shots a day; shots categorized complicated/medium/simple; by **18 September** a “somewhat fictional 10-percent-complete mark.” Late September, Lucas hires **George Mather** as production supervisor. **Kurtz**: technical side now “fine… bluescreen process had begun well, both optical printers worked like gangbusters… they should have been in that spot *three months* earlier.” **Dykstra**: Mather “brought in by Twentieth or whatever combination of powers… an unnecessary hindrance to my work and my communication with George.”

## BLOWING THINGS UP AT NINE-TEN

**Joe Viskocil** and **Bruce Logan** form Nine-Ten; hired mid-September 1976 after ILM explosion tests fail. Logan from the *2001* class (animation cameraman, artist, model builder, shot designer, two-plus years). Experimental short cuts (nine- and eleven-frame flashes) plus explosions, which become “the main contribution.” Borendo’s stage too small; they move to Producers’ Studio, 100 fps instead of 300, models from ~3 feet to ~6 feet. **Viskocil**: ILM acetylene bombs “mainly sparks and the disintegration of the model. It looked very cartoonish.” Via Greg Hauer’s powder card, legal. George wanted “the outline of the ship to be on fire… then it would blow… the World War II type of footage where the ship just disintegrates and you see this big ball of gasoline flame.” Paste applied to the model, then the blast. Silt, magnesium, gasoline-based, electronic squibs. One Saturday: eight or nine X-wings and Y-wings; no tests; “we went for the shot—and we were lucky.” Pieces through the ceiling; Viskocil burns a hand; Logan has “a little flaming debris on my arm.” Seventeen days (seven Borendo, ten Producers’), crew of eight; over budget: over $65,000 vs. $35,000 projected. Later they work at ILM on more explosions. Viskocil: the long-shot TIE explosion in the Death Star escape is “two separate explosions shot off at the same time, so it looked like one was coming through the other”; Alderaan “a gas explosion that consisted of wood fibers” silhouetted as the core. Other houses: DePatie-Freleng, Ray Mercer & Company; Van Der Veer Photo Effects (laser bolts and lightsabers, about thirty shots, Blalack); Modern Film Effects composites English 35mm anamorphic with ILM VistaVision.

## STATE OF THE PRODUCTION

**14 September 1976**, Lucas’s first extended Lippincott interview since before principal (Rinzler: possibly tied to the contract). Exhausted, disappointed.

The studio: “Fox still doesn’t know what the film is. They’re not a model of confidence. They’re still worried that they’ve got a big and expensive ‘something’ on their hands, which they can’t describe or understand.”

ILM: “Given another five years and another $8 million, we could get some pretty spectacular effects out of there. But we don’t have the time or the money… That’s the biggest problem.”

Principal photography: “After trying to improve the script and trying to find the right story, the movie became itself, it took over. It was very hard. It found itself and became the movie that we were making. It wasn’t particularly the movie I started out to make.”

Concept: like *Graffiti* and cruising — “It’s inconceivable that they aren’t making hundreds of these adventure movies. It’s part of American culture.”

Visuals: “written and designed as a movie… cinematic elements, not literary elements… an emotional, visual exercise, not as an intellectual exercise. I’m sure we are going to get a lot of backlash… but I’m not afraid of it being called a comic-book movie.”

Predictions: “could be a type of Davy Crockett phenomenon… I know I’ve got a better one in me, one that is more refined.” Cites Gene Roddenberry: *Star Trek* didn’t pull together until “the tenth or fifteenth episode.” “Someday I’ll do another movie like this, maybe, which will be much closer to my original plan. I didn’t get the illusion that I’d seen in my mind… Whenever you make a movie, a force takes over and directs the movie… it all has a life of its own.” “The film’s only hope is that it is *Jaws* and not Godzilla versus the Gila Monster.”

Caption typo in EE: “Hirsh also attended a discussion with Steven Spielberg…”

## A BLACK MOOD

Lucas shuttles Park Way / ILM three days a week. **Chew**: George “admitted… that all he could see were his mistakes… free association… the day so-and-so had a cold, or someone had a sore throat… that lens broke… ‘Oh, this doesn’t work—did I mess up in directing this?’ ” **Hal Barwood**, with Matthew Robbins: lunchtime screening of Leia with “this sort of cardboard gun,” guys in “plastic suits that looked silly” saying “Set for stun”; they joke, “Oh my God, it’s a disaster!” Lucas’s memory of line readings usually spares going through the outs; half hour to a couple of hours per scene. Still more than a month behind the contract calendar. De Palma recommends **Paul Hirsch**. **Marcia Lucas** calls; Hirsch’s wife Jane is expecting; “Go do it.” Hirsch arrives San Anselmo **16 October** after a week finishing *Carrie*; intimidated (Marcia and Chew have Oscar nominations). Switches from Moviola to George’s Steenbeck after being “all thumbs.” First assignment: a Jympson assembly that already seemed to be what George wanted — “This is your test.” Cut from about four minutes to three, “but there was more in it.”

**Burtt** takes a basement room: “What I want you to do is just start cutting in sound effects… so I can see whether I like them or not.” First look at reels one and two: “overwhelmed.” Starts on the opening fight (no spaceship shot, no titles) and Artoo; works basic laser-gun sounds about a month.

**Johnston** on Vader’s TIE: “another quickie design, based on a two-by-two-inch sketch.” George: “Make it look like a faster, newer TIE model. It’s the general’s ship, the command car.” A dozen sketches; Steve Gawley builds it. Starfields: Johnston, two 4×8-foot Plexiglas sheets, semi-circular light box, painted silver then black; holes poked in the black paint; first a “polka-dot look,” then nebulae and constellations.

## FINALLY MAGIC

Lucas at ILM Monday–Wednesday evening. Kurtz/Mather “fictitious but… strangely accurate” estimate: **18.1 percent** complete by 16 October. **Edlund** (CBS News, Seattle; UCLA philosophy; dropped out of Harvard Law): programs shots, shoots B&W, develops in 15–20 minutes, Moviola review; sometimes ten takes; then he can phone San Anselmo and be understood. Optical: sixteen people, two shifts, eleven hours, six days; after about six months of testing, bluescreen printing “fairly good.” Blue pylons — “one of Dykstra’s innovations.” Stages: Dykstraflex on a 40-foot track; second stage a 14-foot track with a Technorama camera; turntables, motorized trolleys, bluescreens. Vacuum for shoes to keep the printing room clean. **Bob Shourt** fixes R2-D2 in two weeks — something Stears’s department hadn’t in six months. Mather, per Lorne Peterson, speeds VistaVision processing (houses had been ignoring the low-level production). **Muren** nights, Edlund days on the main camera, from August or September, about five months. **Ken Ralston**: “George was our general… We were his soldiers.” Enthusiasm rises as product appears (Dykstra). “Renaissance people”; then arguments *to take more work*. No internal memo pads; word of mouth. “Country Club”: no AC, stages over 100 degrees, cold tub eyed “with horror” by studio execs; Johnston airplane-escape-chute water slide; model-shop dances, pizza, “the occasional porno film snuck in”; Friday “launching ship night” (model rockets, broomsticks, old X-wings, once an old car). Daytime plants hog electricity; shots often after 5 PM; people sleep on cots. McQuarrie Death Star matte/notes: **92 miles in diameter**, equatorial trench **2 miles wide**. **30 October**: “25.8 percent complete.” **Scorsese**, later reminiscence: George visiting his LA house, “complaining about the special effects and how he never wanted to direct again… But he was thrilled with certain shots, like a spacecraft crossing the frame and the camera panning with it.”

First ILM shot officially approved **11 October 1976** (Death Star trench cannon note). *Falcon* pulled into the Death Star: matte with several elements; **Joe Johnston *and* Joe Johnston** — only one Imperial suit left (“the other had been stolen”), split-screen; only one canon, needed two (Edlund). Lucas also shoots Death Star laser-tunnel inserts (Johnston and Jon Erland); **Adam Beckett** on the energy tunnel: Pete Kuran platform and wall lights with Jonathan Seay; ~160 pieces of artwork in 42 frames, mostly cycles; infinite-tunnel miniature via a hole in a front-surface mirror, “just like at the funhouse.”

## SPLICING THROUGH SPACE

**Hirsch** has not seen the whole picture; aims for about a reel a week. Reel nine (Ben in the power trench, “Tarzan scene,” swordfight, shootout): “*My God, this is going to be a lot of work.*” **Lucas**: three editors at once; half his time in LA; three or four days a week with them; “editor to editor to editor, all day long.” **Hirsch**: “George likes to keep things simple and gets his energy from the cutting.” **Chew**, coming off *Cuckoo’s Nest*: Forman printed four or five takes of masters and singles; “George, either for aesthetic or budgetary reasons, was much more controlled.” Just before the first cut, a “tag team” of George, Richard, Marcia, and Paul, a couple of hours each; last day about twelve hours.

## PRIVATE PREMIERE

First cut probably late October or early November (Rinzler: aggregate interviews not exact). Scene chronology almost exactly the shooting script: Biggs, Luke’s friends, Jabba the Hutt, two trench runs, etc. Viewers: Lucas, three editors, Kurtz, Burtt (“scratch mix”). Few end-battle opticals; work print based on WWII footage. **Burtt**: “Spitfires going by that sounded like spaceships; we had lasers being fired from Messerschmitts. It was relatively insane.” **Chew**, typography locked: “astounded by the *entirety of George’s vision*… if you know George in any way, you had to realize that all of this came as a result of this *one man*… this basic seed that he had grown into a thousand trees.”

Two substantial cuts follow. First: start as in the **second draft** — do not intercut Luke and friends on Tatooine with the robots, Vader, and Leia in space. **Hirsch**: first five minutes “too many story lines… the robots and the Princess, Vader, Luke.” Cut Luke and Biggs; present Princess and Vader; plans to robots to planet to Luke. “But it also made the picture a lot weirder… the main characters became the robots, which is a wonderful idea. It’s very George… And the reason it works is that George invested the characters with a human sense of humor.” Planet now mysterious (Jawas first). George also felt Luke should not appear until an active participant. Marcia fought to keep the four friend scenes; tried in different places; arguments for cutting: George didn’t like the performances; later relationships are stronger. **Chew**: “how do we speed up getting to the cantina… stay with the story of the robots… That’s when George told Paul and me for the first time that that was initially how he had written the story.”

Second major cut: **Jabba the Hutt spars with Han Solo**. ILM cannot complete the complicated stop-motion Jabba in time. Marcia lobbies to keep it; **Harrison Ford joins her**. Problems again: pacing and performance. **Hirsch**: “George also thought there were too many phony-looking green Martians that looked like Greedo in the background.” Salvage: **Chew**: “George considered putting subtitles under Jabba, and I think that idea got transferred over to Greedo.” **Hirsch**: exposition (Han a smuggler, wanted, needs the money) incorporated into Greedo’s sequence.

First cut ~117 minutes; dropping scenes won’t shrink it much (missing personality/establishing shots, roll-up, credits). Ladd pessimistic about the date (already slipped from April 1977). **Lippincott**: September meeting with Laddie, Jay Kanter, Ashley Boone — “ready at the end of May”; they agreed after a few days. **Ladd**: contemplation of an October opening “because there were hardly any effects”; “George said, ‘I will be ready.’ ” Lucas also asks Ladd for funds to **reshoot parts of the cantina** (Greedo’s new dialogue; more aliens); no immediate decision.

## Chew-ing Editorial

Lucas revives serial/swashbuckler **editorial wipes**. Chew anecdotes:

Rebel ship shootout: staged twice, three cameras each time, behind Rebels then behind stormtroopers; ~twenty seconds on set becomes two minutes. Grease-pencil every laser path (bolts usually no more than three frames); George spots gaps “with one look.”

Luke in garage with robots: not enough Artoo coverage (ran out of time, no close-ups for the hologram). Chew finds a couple of feet before the slate and after “cut”; first time George “got excited about something,” especially as they were “cheating new dialogue for Artoo, which George had yet to make up.”

Dinner with Owen and Beru: usually seldom beyond take three; this scene takes seven, nine, even eleven — “He knew that he was in trouble with the actor playing the uncle.”

Rebel hangar: few baroque moves (contrast Francis); “the most interesting camera moves George did were in the hangar, in scenes that got dropped… Luke getting ready to go to battle, stopping to talk to Han, going over to Leia, and then coming to see Biggs.”

Division (with occasional overlap): Chew also cantina; Hirsch the droid sale, Ben’s cave, and everything from blasting out of Mos Eisley up to the Death Star escape (which Chew and Lucas cut); **Marcia** primarily the deleted Luke-and-friends Tatooine scenes, and everything from X-wing canopies closing to the end of the film.

## ELECTRONIC BABY TALK

Scratch track lacks finished robot sounds. **Burtt**, typography locked: George: “I want an organic sound, like it was a five-year-old kid, but he’s got to sound electronic.” First tries “too electronic, pure synthesizer, too impersonal and cold.” Must stand up beside Guinness. “We had to achieve a performance through sound… many times I think I stopped too early… He’d never be harsh… ‘No, it’s not what I want.’ ” Method: write Artoo’s English (“I’m hungry,” “Look out, the stormtroopers are coming”), get George’s okay, phrase by phrase; reel one took six weeks. Matching English intonation “didn’t work.” Then “basic emotional levels: excitement, cute squawks, inquisitive whistles.” Synthesizer plus microphone: “I’d whistle and coo—a bunch of coos, that’s the sound that George does very well, it’s one of his ideas—I’d sigh and groan…” Always a blend of human and electronic. **Chew**: first effect laid in is Artoo hit by the Jawa shot, falling over — “it just broke me up. The film really came to life.” 30 November ILM memo: correct vs. incorrect blasterfire animation.

## DOGS AND MONSTERS

November: Viskocil and Logan help Edlund blow up seven models at Van Nuys; “one really glorious explosion of one of the TIE fighters, and Richard almost lost his ear.” Dykstra nearly “cut my head off” feeding film around his neck into the Moviola. Dailies room: flea-ridden dogs on dilapidated couches; most frequent critique “Bluespill! Bluespill!” **Beckett** (roto): maybe impossible to avoid entirely; “grumbling that ‘they weren’t as careful as they could be’ in England… derive[s] from our initial failure to supply the plates.” **Muren**: start/stop motors, aerodynamic rolls “absolute killers”; sometimes avoids motors, using earlier space-film experience. **Dykstra** on Barry: compressed schedule made bluespill/yellow-light problems inevitable. **Richard Alexander**: first English footage — “Hey, this might be pretty neat.” Make-believe percentages drop; new tracker until “OKAY G. L.” Escape-pod shots finished first, but first shot “accepted” is the Death Star firing a laser cannon, photographed by Muren. Schedule: two other shots approved **16 October 1976**; only a few more by November. 15 November memo: 22 November meeting with “monster people” to review the cantina and prepare the still-unfunded reshoot. Bantha notes: “New tail will be dragged… Head piece depends on what elephants will tolerate.” Discuss disguising the elephant trainer as a Tusken so he can stay close. Union: ~three-quarters through production, Jerry Smith and Joe Berna visit; **Pollock**: “a rather brilliant performance.” **Edlund** pushes a programmed camera down the track, “lights flashing and the counters buzzing”; “Okay we’ll take you guys in.”

## MOTION OUT OF MODELS

Edlund and Muren split the main camera night and day. **Johnston**: Rebel blockade runner “almost the last ship finished”; first pirate ship short on detail, amateurish paint; second version after they’d learned on other models. “It was supposed to look like a ship that had been assembled from other ships.” After the original cockpit went on the *Falcon*, “George said he wanted something that looked like a fish head… a little sketch of this hammerhead thing.”

**Edlund**: “The opening shot, most of the pirate ship shots, most of the bigger ships I did; and I did the trench itself.” Muren: “Richard liked the dogfights… He also shot the training remote.”

**Muren**: “multiple ship shots, like the Y-wings diving down together… most of the shots with the ships flying through the trench, the ship elements, although Richard shot the trench element; and I did all the shots of the ships flying toward and away from the Death Star.” Edlund: “Dennis did a lot of the X-wing stuff… the armada scene and the approach to the Death Star, and the peel-off shot.”

“Maps of the galaxy” for sun position, “but it really worked out best when the shot dictated how it was lit” (Edlund). Muren first skeptical of the Dykstraflex for quick dogfights that might have been high-speed wires/poles; later its advantage: reprogram in two minutes, “spectacular-looking shots done by an individual instead of a group.” Nine-Ten low-tech flybys ultimately not compatible with ILM’s rising level. “the pantomiming of the ships… completely created by Richard and me… like they’ve got weight, because you’re creating motion out of nothing.” Script corrections change “Blue” Rebel pilots to “Red”; in the final film at least one helmet still has a blue insignia. Edlund: cardboard for a sun flare — “that’s real professionalism.”

## EDITING, THE SPIRIT OF ’76

New temp cut every couple of weeks, same small group. Opening shootout: **Lucas**: “There were two shots of actual humans getting hit, with big explosions on their chests. But I cut those out after I saw it; it was a little too extreme. I did have big blasts on the stormtroopers, but I avoided them on humans.” Obi-Wan’s house rethought: as shot, they watch the hologram, discuss the Force, *then* decide to save the Princess — “a bit heartless” because of the lag. Re-edited as if already talking about the Force; as soon as they see the hologram, Ben decides to go to Alderaan; a brief impatient Artoo beep, culled from other footage, covers the join. Hangar: lines shortened; dialogue about Luke’s father being a great pilot cut. End battle: supporting pilots cut for identifiability. **Lucas**: “We had a Blue crew but we couldn’t use blue because of the bluescreen [parts of their ships would’ve disappeared]. So we were left with Gold Leader and Red Leader.” Two trench runs combined into one — tighter, but now one run must carry Luke’s computer intention, Ben’s dialogue, Vader’s actions, R2’s drama, Han’s arrival, other pilots’ fates, Leia’s feelings, in a believable trench length. Suspense drawn out with ILM second-unit of the Death Star preparing to fire, plus Peter Cushing coverage stolen from a shortened earlier scene. **Marcia Lucas**: “It was all editorially manufactured.” Just after Thanksgiving she leaves to help Scorsese on *New York, New York.*

## SOUND EDITING

Just before Christmas, another cut. **Hirsch**: second screening with only the work track, no effects; “everyone thought it was a disastrous screening except me” — better to see editorial without music/FX masks. Thereafter Burtt mixes each finished reel and returns it — unorthodox (sound usually last). Editors begin to cut to Artoo more because they know he can talk: “Why don’t we bounce to Artoo and get a reaction?” Jawa language: Burtt studies several African dialects, records Park Way employees speaking and yelling, speeds/alters; an editor “rolling on the floor laughing… little beings in those costumes.” Temp music: Dvořák / “The Planets” at the beginning (Chew says “Dvorak’s *The Planets*”); Liszt preludes over the jail break. George brings records from LA as a guide for John Williams. Chew suggests Tibetan chanting and animal-bone instruments for the cantina; **Lucas**: “No, I’m going to use Benny Goodman… Yeah, they’re gonna play swing, man!”

## Sound Origins by Ben Burtt

TIE fighters: “an elephant howling slowed down and stretched out electronically.” Spaceship doors: Philadelphia subways, air doors, mechanical noises; train clatter also for the sandcrawler motor. Y-wings: wind in radio-tower wires, Poconos, “almost a minor key sustained note.” Lasers: hammered Mount Wilson radio-tower wires mixed with a bazooka. Fox thunderclap (thunder plus sheet metal) in the blockade-runner main-hatch explosion and full-frame Death Star battle flashes; “sweetened-up” when ships dive into the trench. The “Wilhelm”: a man screaming used by Warner Bros. for ~25 years; named from *Charge at Feather River* (1953), cavalry private Wilhelm hit by an arrow; Burtt traces it to *Distant Drums* (1951), “a guy being eaten by an alligator”; used for the stormtrooper Luke shoots who falls into the power trench. **Peter Diamond** played the main Tusken and the stormtrooper shot off the ledge; by his count appeared more than fifteen times; with Reg Harding and Colin Skeaping, “The Three *Star Wars* Stooges.”

## BLAZING TRAILERS

Lippincott works the SF/comic-con circuit; novelization just published; Marvel adaptation under way. Trailer planned fall 1976, crystallized in November; few FX completed, so a short burst. Assistant optical editor **Bruce Green** day-by-day itinerary (abridged in the book): 26 Nov storyboard meeting (Kurtz, Lippincott, Beckett, ad agency); 29 Nov rough cut, unusable FX shots, music; 30 Nov–10 Dec negative hunts, stolen-dog aside, exploding-title matte reshoot, Deluxe answer prints, agency wants the end explosion adjusted. Budget **2 December**, 107 seconds (160 feet): $3,915.10, of which $1,268 MFE opticals. Exploding *Star Wars* logo: Viskocil and Logan. Ironically, the Leia-and-Chewbacca cockpit shot in the trailer is a **front-projection** one; other FX from the gunport battle. **Ralston**: “more about the spirit of the film… a couple of the very early lightsabers.” After Lucas approves, Fox anxiety. **Ladd**: people saying “Change the title”; board sees the trailer in December, “didn’t like it” but no big issue; “one of the board of directors disliked the title very much.” Title conjured, for some, “Hollywood starlets at war.” **Lucas**: “If you want to change the title, come up with more titles… They didn’t, so we let it go at that.” Trailer out by Christmas, saddled with what “sounds like” Vivaldi’s “Winter” from *Four Seasons* — slow, ominous, at cross purposes with upbeat visuals, “a schizophrenic excerpt from *2001*.” **Ladd** sees it at the Avco (not well received) and Tucson (badly received). Market research: the picture will “absolutely not work if we call it *Star Wars* and if we keep the trailer on—and that the picture really had *no chance.*” Ladd: “it had to have a chance just to at least get its money back!”
