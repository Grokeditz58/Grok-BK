## Opening (p. 82)

**1 October 1975:** Bunny Alsup to England to set up house; Kurtz and family follow **6 October**; Lucas shortly after. March start planned. Mid-October: Fox “worries mushroomed into full-blown panic,” colored by *Lucky Lady*. **Kurtz**: “*Lucky Lady* was a fiasco… started as an independent picture. Stanley Donen was controlling everything himself… halfway through production he got into serious trouble. The studio… eventually took over the entire production. That experience was very fresh in their minds.” Mid-October moratorium: salaries paid, but no further development financed until the **13 December** board meeting. **Ladd**: hysteria after *Lucky Lady* press as “the most expensive picture Fox had made since 1969,” that film’s cost “covered in guarantees,” while *Star Wars* “kept escalating”; “research and development kept going on and on, and nothing was being seen.” ILM’s developmental tech “confused” them (Ladd); Stears’s feasibility study didn’t please them. **Lucas**: they didn’t grasp that “It takes six months to design a robot and build it. They just figured you could do it in six weeks, because they’re used to making television movies… the more time and money that’s spent up front, the less time and money you have to spend actually making the movie.” **Pollock**: “no contracts, no approved budgets, no money… George was already $400,000 of his own money into the movie. Not yet reimbursed.” Accounting: personal loans to the Star Wars Corporation from October 1973; through fiscal 1975–76, **$473,368** total. Budget trajectory (Rinzler): $3 million at the treatment to about $6–$8.2 million; panic came after a balloon to $15 million had already been brought down. **Kurtz**: “My final realistic budget was $8.2 million direct [without overhead]. But they insisted we cut back… to $7.5 million… Laddie kept asking me if this was going to be realistic, and I said, ‘I doubt it.’ ” **Lippincott**: “George made the comment that it’s really a $15 million movie being made for half its budget.” **22 October 1975** *Variety* notes Lucas and Kurtz “in town working on budgeting of their multi-million dollar sci-fi epic, ‘Adventure of Star Killer’ [sic].” Agreed start **28 March 1976**, locked to desert heat (robot-suit actors). After they turn in $7.5 million, Fox insists that figure include overhead — another $600,000 must be shed, to **$6.9 million direct**.

**Lucas**, typography locked, at this nadir (no contract, still on a pre-*Graffiti* writer-director salary, large percentage of savings invested): “I thought *Star Wars* was going to make anywhere from $16 to $25 million… But when the price got way up there, I became very pessimistic. I could rely on a low-end box office of $16 million, but if it cost $8.2 million, after you put the advertising and all the costs on it, we’d barely break even. And when Fox took away six weeks of our preproduction time, I knew it was going to cost us three or four weeks of shooting time… those three or four weeks were probably going to cost ten times as much as the six weeks in preproduction.” **Spielberg**: “George always described it to me as a kids’ picture, a little Disney film, that he didn’t think anyone would want to see, but he wanted to see it… he would be putting down its commercial chances.” **Lucas**: “In the end… I really didn’t think we were going to make any money at all on *Star Wars.*” Caption: Lucas’s notes on “the development of the Han Solo character” and the developing fourth draft, “in particular the attack on the Death Star.”

## ONCE MORE INTO THE BREACH

**Kurtz**: “We cut back 10 percent of John Barry’s art department budget, across the board, without even consulting him.” Lucas, mid–fourth draft, and Barry go through the script for cuts. **Lucas**: combine extra-heavy scenes; eliminate camera angles that would require more set; “I cut out scenes. The compromises had started… we had already cut it down to the bone and then we were faced with the fact that we had to cut it even further.” Cost cuts, small to large: Mos Eisley hangar loses a sliding concrete slab overhead; **“Alderaan was cut out of the picture, and all of its scenes were transferred to the Death Star”**; rebel base moved from jungle exterior into a temple; Ben’s house scaled from “a very bizarre three-story cave, all carved out of rock” to a one-story home with windows, easier to light. Circa January 1976 McQuarrie painting: rebel fighters moved inside on Yavin; McQuarrie recycles a WWII navy pilot with clipboard; “the overhead would just be black [instead of jungle].” UK art-department hangar maquette.

Unsworth has to back out; Lucas hires **Gilbert Taylor**. **Lucas**: loved *A Hard Day’s Night* (1964) and *Dr. Strangelove* (1964); had seen *Macbeth* (1971) for color; “I wanted the film to look like those; that was really why I hired him.” **Taylor**, impressed by *Graffiti*, delighted; timing lets much of *The Omen* (1976) crew join; many with him “over twelve years.” Editor: local **John Jympson** rather than Richard Chew (*The Conversation*, *One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest*) — cost, no travel, no work-permit problems. They first tried John Victor-Smith; Jympson had cut *A Hard Day’s Night*. **Lucas**: “I had cut my own movies… I liked him, and he seemed like he was going to work out.”

## A DOUBLE-EDGED SWORD

ILM relief: no Alderaan cloud city (**Dykstra**: “All of those clouds”; a rented Learjet for formations can be scrapped); fewer jungle-planet scenes. Long-term cost: quality front-projection plates in time for live action become “pretty much impossible.” **Dykstra**, typography locked: “I guarantee you a week of no nourishment from the umbilical cord to a fetus is going to do a lot more than just make him a week younger when he comes out… They just said, ‘Well, there’s no money, but we want the stuff on the same day.’ That’s bullshit… that really hurt us badly—and it was really a morale loser, too, because it was obvious that we weren’t going to make it.” **Blalack**: delivery starting April 1976; first sequence the gunport, “actually, the first part of it was to be the pirate ship cockpit, distinctly different from the gunport.” Johnston takes over effects storyboards from Tavoularis (finished mid-June); boards submitted and revised **10 and 22 October 1975**, from the 16mm aerial cut plus script, “each frame.” **Johnston**: three- or four-hour meetings; “George would talk, I would draw, John would sometimes suggest how the shot could be done more easily.” **Lucas** is uneasy about storyboards: artists “bring a lot to it… In a lot of movies, I’ve seen scenes that looked like storyboarded scenes… I’ve always been much more loose about how things come together while shooting it.”

## VOYAGE TO THE SANDS

November 1975: Watts and Barry’s second North African recce, Morocco and Tunisia (Barry knew Tunisia from *The Little Prince*, 1974, and a prior solo *Star Wars* trip). Shortlist from Iran, Libya, Turkey, Spain, Algeria; Lucas wanted Antonioni’s *The Passenger* Land Rover location in Algeria (US release 9 April 1975). Algeria and Libya “too politically volatile for Fox”; Iran too far. **Barry**: “Djerba had this white architecture, and George liked the look of that from the photographs; he also liked Matmata, where people live in these holes in the ground.” Prerequisites: meet the script without overwhelming the scene. **Watts**: Morocco’s south had dunes and a canyon, but “the architecture was very Arab; everything looked like *Beau Geste.*” They call George from Casablanca: Tunisia is better. Meet Lucas and Kurtz on Djerba. **Watts**: “the minute George saw the island of Djerba, he made the decision to film there… because it is strange and it was right.” **Lucas**: “I loved the architecture in Djerba and I loved the salt flat and that hole… I came up with the idea of having the homestead out on the flat, in one location, with the hole in the ground in Matmata… everybody was very upset about that. It meant we had to shoot two locations instead of one… But I thought it’d work out very well—it was one of the really rewarding finds.” **Barry** on unused grain stores as a Mos Eisley street: “We didn’t get to use it, but you’ll notice George used its name in the script, Tatooine—one of the planets is called that.” Caption: a Barry sketch reads “Djerba Hotel, Tatanouine,” which became “Tatooine.” The Mos Eisley grain-store scene never made the draft, “but would’ve featured the Jawas.” **Lucas**: “little grain houses that were four stories high but with little tiny doors, little tiny windows, it was a hobbit village… We had a whole sequence with these little hobbit-world slum dwellers but we had to cut it out.” Tunisia also wins logistically: dunes, ravine, and salt flat “all within thirty minutes of one another”; dunes accessible by truck. **Barry**: “It’s dreadful, isn’t it, to think that that’s what moviemaking revolves around? Instead of ideas, it revolves around, ‘Can you get hotel rooms or can you get there on a plane?’ ” Fox delays now overlap tourist season; **Watts** on packaged tours booking rooms a year ahead.

## THE STARFIGHTERS HAVE LANDED

Mid-November, Lucas and Kurtz stop in New York for East Coast casting: **Jodie Foster for Leia** and **Christopher Walken for Han** — the latter, “according to Kurtz, becoming a favorite in the eyes of Fred Roos.” Back in LA by end of November; Kurtz to England after the first week of December; Lucas on the script, ILM, and new McQuarrie assignments (McQuarrie freelance September–October: *The Adventures of the Wilderness Family*, 1975; *The Winds of Autumn*, 1976). McQuarrie sometimes works at ILM to fold in Johnston vehicle changes. **Paul Huston** (early hire, former student of Jamie Shourt): maybe ten people; he and Johnston drawing X-wings all day; when McQuarrie comes, “one of Ralph’s illustrations… this painting of a TIE fighter over the Death Star with an X-wing… I was just knocked out.” “Ralph’s illustrations became a focus… It unified the whole facility.” **Dykstra**: “real quiet, but he’s really fun underneath.” **Stears**, in England: “I think McQuarrie’s paintings are why the movie’s taken the shape it has, because those sketches are so specific.”

Three key ships must be finalized for full-scale UK builds (sandcrawler interior/partial exterior; rebel-ship hallways; pirate vessel cockpit, central area, gunports, “a partial but massive exterior”). Then a complication: **Han Solo needed new “wheels.”** *Space: 1999* had debuted, and “Lucas found that its main ship looked too much like the first pirate ship—which the model makers had finished literally the weekend before they heard.” **Lucas**: “They were all very upset that I changed the design… They had spent an enormous amount of money and time building that other ship, and I threw it out. It’s one of those decisions that was very costly, but I felt that we really needed the individuality and personality of a better ship.” First pirate ship roughly **$25,000** — “about a third of the total budget for the models” (McCune: “it got billed for everything”); seven feet long, “four hundred cycles of electronics.” It is reused as the rebel ship (only a couple of shots); the vessel with more screen time is redesigned. **McCune**: “Joe, George, and John worked up this new idea for what they called the round ‘Porkburger.’ ” **Lucas**, typography locked: “The flying hamburger was my favorite design… I thought that the other design was too close to *Space: 1999* and too conventional looking. I wanted something really off the wall, since it was the key ship in the movie; I wanted something with a lot more personality. I thought of the design on the airplane, flying back from London: a hamburger. I didn’t want it to be a flying saucer, but I wanted to have something with a radial shape that would be completely different from anything else.” **Johnston**: “The pirate ship was a highly modified smuggler’s freighter… George wanted it to look like it’d been hot-rodded, so we put bigger engines on it and stripped things off of it. He also liked the idea of having a double inverted saucer shape.” Johnston’s series had the cockpit “in the center on the top instead of being on the side.” “The second pirate ship was probably the ship we designed the fastest… less than a week.” First ship’s cockpit sawed off and stuck on the new one (McCune); the leftover rebel ship gets a “hammerhead shark” cockpit — “two cardboard buckets filled with Styrofoam.” Caption later in the chapter: Paul Huston and David Grell work on the **Falcon** model — the ship name appearing in a photo caption while running text still says “pirate ship.”

## SPECIAL FORCES

More ILM hires: art/industrial-design students (many from Long Beach State); “high school or college dropouts or burnouts who knew how to run an electric drill” (Shepherd); specialists. **Jon Erland** and **Lorne Peterson** start **8 December**, complex rubber-mold experts. **Shepherd** scrounged almost the whole model shop except McCune; McCune wanted “mental chutzpah.” **Peterson**: knew Shepherd from Seal Beach; “I think when I started there were thirty-some people.” **McCune**: they arrived as Death Star molds began; vacuum former and injection molder; “those two guys really saved it.” **Edlund**: Doug Smith “started out sweeping the floors.” Johnston sketches of the second pirate ship: mandibles added because “a hamburger or saucer doesn’t really have a front”; “We justified the mandibles by saying that they could be a freight-loading area.”

## Dated McQuarrie paintings (untitled cluster in the extract)

- **25 November 1975:** “Fighters low over Death Star” and “One of the fighters dashing down trench.” Early Death Star surface: smooth walls, cannons low to the canyon floor. **McQuarrie**: “George just wanted an atmosphere sketch of the action and the hail of laserfire.” The painting that impressed Huston and others.
- **5 December 1975:** “Throne room scene-princess receives heroes.” **Barry**: Lucas described it to Ralph from “the concept we knew we could handle… those wing effects that keep disappearing” so they wouldn’t have to build “the part ‘round the corner.”
- **7 December 1975:** “Tusken Raiders painting.” From July–August costume sketches. **McQuarrie**: “I thought they should have goggles on because they have some sort of vision problem, the result of their species’ mutation… The mouthpiece is a filter because of the constant sandstorms; the little tank underneath is filled with gas or a chemical, as part of their life-support system to counteract their mutation problems.” Early-1976 John Mollo Tusken costume sketch also captioned here.

## GREEN LIGHT, GO!

**13 December 1975** Fox board meeting. Lucas is “fully prepared to take his project elsewhere if necessary.” **Warren Hellman**, present: “Laddie came in and said, ‘I’m a believer in this—we’ve gotta go ahead with this project. Now’s the time we really have to get behind this.’ Dennis Stanfill was being neutral, and finally went along with it. But the board never had enthusiasm for the project.” Green light; Lippincott portfolios of McQuarrie and Johnston art; Ladd’s backing is the key. Pollock letter: “George Lucas is extremely pleased with his relationship with Alan Ladd Jr.” **Michael Gruskoff** (*Lucky Lady* producer): would have loved to see Ladd present “*Star Wars* as a $7 million movie with a small furry animal and two robots, with the budget constantly going up and just a few sketches to show—and he needed an answer *right then.*” **Pollock**: “If at any time, Laddie would have left, the project would have died. There is no doubt.” **Lippincott**: “*2001* didn’t break even until late 1975—and that was the most successful science-fiction film of all time… You had to be crazy to make a science-fiction film when we wanted to.” Shortly after, concern over a possible management change: Pollock to Immerman, **5 January 1976**, typography locked: “Unfortunately, after some negative experiences in the past at other studios, George does not feel that he can entrust the product of four years of his life to an unknown person… George feels very strongly about this point—as strongly as about anything else we have ever discussed.” Fox, as a publicly held company, will not grant final cut.

Purse strings loosen; frenetic pace. **17 December** Lippincott interviews Lucas (contemporaneous Lost Interview). Key answers, typography locked in substance:

- Why this story: loved Flash Gordon serials on TV “at 6:15 PM every day”; after *Graffiti*, “very few films being made for young people between the ages of twelve and twenty… since the Western had died, there hadn’t been any mythological fantasy movies… Westerns and pirate movies, Errol Flynn… Now all you get is cops and hard drama. So instead of making important, gripping, isn’t-it-terrible-what’s-happening-to-mankind movies, which is what I started out doing, I decided it would be much more useful for me to make movies that made kids have a fantasy life and feel good, so they could go ahead and have a more productive life.”
- Evolution: started as a concept — “an action-adventure movie just like Flash Gordon… People running around in spaceships, shooting each other… and an Empire. I knew I wanted to have a big battle in outer space, a dogfight… Then I asked myself, *What story can I tell?* … Each story was a totally different story about totally different characters before I finally landed on *the* story… I pilfered some of the scenes from the first script, and I kept doing it until I finally got the final script—which is the one I’m working on now… Now what I’m doing in this rewrite is I’m slowly shaving down the plot… After that, I’ll go through and do another rewrite, which will develop the dialogue and the characters.”
- “This film has been murder. It’s very easy to write about something you know about and you’ve lived through—it’s very hard to write about something you make up from scratch… like being in a candy store… I didn’t want to have, like exposition… more of a straight adventure movie rather than something that had such complex technology that most of the film was spent dealing with the technology… everything is unfamiliar and therefore, by definition, needs to be explained.”
- Characters, typography locked: “My original idea was to make a movie about an old man and a kid, who have a teacher-student relationship [Rinzler bracket: treatment, rough, and first drafts]. And I knew I wanted the old man to be a real old man, but also a warrior. In the original script the old man was the hero. I wanted to have a seventy-five-year-old Clint Eastwood. I liked that idea. Then I wrote another script without the old man. I decided I wanted to do it about kids… I don’t know that much about old people… Then I had a story about the kid and his brother, where the kid developed—and **a pirate character developed out of the brother** [Rinzler bracket: second draft]. As I kept writing scripts, more characters evolved… I pulled one character from one script and another character from another script, and pretty soon they got into the dirty half dozen that they are today. It was a long, painful struggle, and I’m still doing it, still struggling to get them to come alive.” Aim now: knock out plot loopholes, “make the characters more interesting, more enjoyable.”
- Visuals: “a film that looks very real, with a nitty-gritty feel… a filtered look, nostalgic, a looking-back quality… lighting style that is very dramatic, in the style of Greg Toland [*Citizen Kane*; *My Darling Clementine*]… Framing-wise, I’m going to try for a semi-documentary, loose frame that will give it a nervous ‘now’ quality… a fantasy-documentary look.” Cinema verité “in my blood.” “*Apocalypse Now* was going to be a real Peter Watkins… you-are-there, newsreel-type movie.” *Graffiti*: “Sam Katzman, AIP, hot-rod movie style… center frame.” *THX*: “two-dimensional drawn-on, surreal style… but it also had that documentary quality… mainly through the lighting. In this film I’m going to use a documentary frame but with theatrical lighting.” Acting: more than one camera, “lead back away from the people… The cameras are onlookers… I won’t really demand that they get the lines right.” Casting: “I look for magic… the personal and physical qualities that I want that character to have.” Two directions: “a little more serious, a little more realistic; the other is a little more fun, more goofy. Right now my first choice is to make it fairly serious… the more serious characters will give it the stronger reality.” Guinness: “We’re negotiating.” No movie stars: “If it’s a Robert Redford movie, it’s no longer a fantasy… In order to create a fantasy, you have to have unknowns.” DP: interviewed thirty or forty; first time with a real DP (*Graffiti* was “Haskell Wexler… helping me out”). Wants someone who will “take chances”; this picture has “a lot of front projection, a lot of huge sets.” Taylor: “very free-thinking… I don’t think he’s even read the script yet, quite frankly… A lot of the decisions will be made for him.” Open to suggestion: “less than more, I’m afraid… This is the first time I’m going to have to deal with a real crew in a real movie… I’ve loosened up a little bit.”
- Design of everyday things in another galaxy: “I’m trying to make props that don’t stand out… a casual almost I’ve-seen-this-before look… the one that Ralph McQuarrie did of the banthas. You look at that painting… and you say, ‘Oh yeah, Bedouins …’ Then you look at it some more and say, ‘Wait a minute, that’s not right.’ … Like the X-wing and TIE fighter battle, you say, ‘I’ve seen that, it’s World War II—but wait a minute—that isn’t any kind of jet I’ve ever seen before.’ I want the whole film to have that quality!” Instruction to collaborators: “Keep it nondescript… I don’t want anything to stand out… If they ask, ‘What did the ray guns look like?’ You’d say, ‘I don’t know—they just looked like ray guns.’ … It’ll be absolutely the opposite of what all the science-fiction movies are… I’m working very hard to keep everything nonsymmetrical. Nothing looks like it belongs with anything else… one thing came from one part of the galaxy and another thing came from another part of the galaxy.”
- Williams: “old-fashioned, romantic movie score”; liked *Jaws* (released 20 June 1975). Cantina musicians: “a very bizarre, kind of primitive rock. I’m toying with the idea of adding a country-and-western influence to the film, combining country and western with classical. If I can get away with it, I might do it.”

## Revised Paintings

**McQuarrie**: “When new spaceships were designed, I would put them in and paint out the one I had.” Series of paintings of the second pirate ship in context. An early Mos Eisley docking-bay painting with the first ship and a crane “considered too old-fashioned and not included.” Later painting: revised pirate ship. **Han’s clothes also redesigned, typography locked:** “Instead of being in blue and with a cape, he wears a vest and sweater, light trousers, and dark boots. The beard has been shaved, too, so that he looks more like Buck Rogers.” After Alderaan scenes move to the Death Star, McQuarrie updates docking-bay paintings: new vessel, starfields instead of clouds.

## THE BIG BATTLE BEGINS

Before and after the board decision, Lucas is also “hammering out the fourth draft… honing the end battle, which ILM needed to get a jump on.” **Lucas**: “It wasn’t until the fourth draft that I described the dogfight in detail… Each time before that… write, ‘There is this big battle and Luke wins.’ … That seven minutes of film time, when it was translated shot by shot, became about fifty pages of the script, because each shot was described in detail.” To Lippincott on 17 December: twenty-five boards (shot descriptions) a day; up at three in the morning to finish by nine, before casting or ILM. Caption: model work on the second pirate ship / “*Falcon* model.” Handwritten notes: a rebel pilot leftover named “Chewie” was going to survive — “Chewie: ‘Here we go again … I’ve got a malfunction, Luke … I can’t stay with you … You did it! You did it! They went right in!’ ” “But with *Chewie* becoming the nickname of Chewbacca, Solo’s copilot in the fourth draft, rebel pilot Chewie became rebel pilot Wedge.” Solo’s end heroics solidified: “Full shot of wingman POV. Out of the sun charges Han Solo, in his pirate manta-ship heading right for the TIE ships.” Model crew works through Christmas and New Year’s, Sunday nights at 4 AM; eleven artists sign the finished pirate-ship model. Gawley and Johnston carpool Long Beach–Van Nuys, 124 miles round-trip; sometimes sleep in the screening room.

## AMAZING STORIES

Videotaped casting sessions exist for **12, 15, and 30 December**. 12th: Kurt Russell, Patti d’Arbanville, Richard Doran, Terri Nunn. 15th: Russell again, Chris Moe, Forest Williams, Amy Irving, Christopher Allport, Nunn again, Will Seltzer. 30th: Nunn a third time, Lisa Eilbacher, Robby Benson, Eddie Benton, Linda Purl, Andy Stevens, **Mark Hamill**, and **Carrie Fisher**. **Harrison Ford present all three days.** Most tests are for Luke and Leia; “Nunn clearly the front-runner for the latter”; Ford is called to read Han’s lines for each tryout, after **Fred Roos** helps persuade Lucas to amend his policy of not considering *American Graffiti* actors. Cindy Williams and Charles Martin Smith (*Graffiti* veterans) also allowed to test after the inconclusive first session. Roos “casts” Ford in a staged performance: installs a door at Zoetrope precisely when Lucas will come by, coinciding with Richard Dreyfuss’s visit. **Ford**: “I left acting to become a carpenter because our second baby was coming and we like to eat. I wasn’t making it as an actor.” “I was out there kneeling down in the hall, putting up this elaborate doorway. Dreyfuss, the big movie star, came in to see George for the picture, and there I was cast as the blue-collar worker. That was the only time I saw George, not more than three or four weeks before they were set to make a decision.” **Roos**: “It just kind of clicked for George at that point.” **Lucas**, typography locked: “I thought, *Here’s a possibility.* Harrison was right for the part, so Fred suggested he read with everybody, which I thought was a great idea—but I wasn’t going to commit. **I wanted someone just like Harrison, but not Harrison, because he was in *Graffiti* and I didn’t want people thinking of another film while watching *Star Wars*—I wanted it to be new.** … I really wanted to see all the diverse possibilities and come at it fresh. Plus, I wasn’t going to choose anyone until I’d tested the whole cast together.” **Ford**: “I think I did fifty or sixty tests for them—testing other people… Many times, I was asked to explain to the testees what the story was about, or George would offer a very simple explanation—that there was this farm boy sent off to the big city, who got involved in this adventure.” Lucas’s process: readings, videotape, film tests; weed down; “you have one impression when you meet somebody for five minutes and another… when they’ve played the scene on tape.”

Calls also for “little people and giants” (Crittenden); ads in the trades. Lucas contemplates “doing the film almost entirely with little people — according to Lippincott, ‘rewriting the whole film for midgets.’ ” **Lucas**: New York screen tests for little people; “I explored all kinds of ideas of making it exotic. I think that idea was a little influenced by *Lord of the Rings.*” “Short-lived.” “Lucas had not forgotten his idea of casting a black Han Solo, Ford notwithstanding.”

## LUKE STARKILLER

Dialogue for the Luke tests was originally written for Ben Kenobi in the third draft (after Organa Major is destroyed, coercing Han to help rescue Leia): “Well, for one reason, we don’t have your other five thousand.” / “Who’s going to pay me then?” / “I think there are some things we should talk about.” / “I’m beginning not to like you.” Video excerpt list includes Lisa Eilbacher, Linda Purl, Eddie Benton, Cindy Williams, Carrie Fisher, Amy Irving, Perry King and Charles Martin Smith, Robby Benson, Mark Hamill, Kurt Russell, Frederic Forrest, Harrison Ford, Andrew Stevens, William Katt, Will Seltzer. Hamill was not a shoo-in; **Crittenden**: “George was open to re-seeing some of the people, like Mark, who he’d dismissed early on… This is one place where Fred Roos came in.” **Hamill**: had forgotten after Christmas; agent Nancy got him a test “way out of nowhere. Four pages of dialogue… ‘We can’t turn back. Fear is their greatest defense. I doubt if the actual security there is any greater than it was on Aquilae or Sullust. What there is, is most likely directed toward a large-scale assault.’ Who talks like that? But you’re selling it.” He had followed the production in *Variety*’s “Films in Preparation” and asked his agent early on for a walk-on, curious how SF films were made. Waiting with Ford: “And he’s Harrison… ‘Hi, George!’ and was so loose with him it made me feel good.” Lucas: “No. Let’s just run through it.” Then “Let’s shoot it.” “He was so unenthusiastic, I thought, *Oh, well, it’s probably hopeless.* … ‘Cut. Okay. Thank you. Harrison, can you stay a couple of minutes?’ And I thought, *That’s it.* I didn’t feel bad or disappointed. It was just a clean no-go.”

## PRINCESS LEIA ORGANA

Carrie Fisher, daughter of Debbie Reynolds and Eddie Fisher, was at the Central School of Speech and Drama in London when first contacted in early December. **Roos**: “Carrie Fisher was a social friend of mine. George was about to cast another actress, but I kept urging him to meet Carrie and test her.” Fisher (*Shampoo*, 1975) couldn’t leave school mid-term; took a rain check; “I thought I’d totally missed testing for it.” Contacted again at home in LA over Christmas. **Fisher**: “Leia was unconscious a lot… And I wanted to be unconscious; I have an affinity for unconsciousness… I also wanted to be involved in all of it, with Wookiees, with the monsters in the cantina. I was caught by my mother and some of my family rehearsing it in my underwear… ‘General Kenobi!’ … ‘A battle station with enough firepower to destroy an entire system.’ ” Designated scene: Leia reveals what R2-D2 is carrying. **Crittenden**: they softened her look (hair had been pulled straight back, dressed severely); “Carrie was very unique in that she was formidable for an eighteen-year-old. She had a tremendous amount of sophistication, so in fact the hardest thing to do was to get her to be young.” De Palma had told her Jodie Foster “had the inside track.” “They had me do it a couple of different ways: conversational and then arch… very little direction—and I thought, *There is no way that I have it.* I didn’t hear anything for about three weeks, so I thought, *Well, I’m not going to get to have lunch with monsters.*”

## HAN SOLO
