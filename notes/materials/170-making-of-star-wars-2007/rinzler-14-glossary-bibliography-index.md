## GLOSSARY (Rinzler p. 309)

Terms and definitions locked from the EE (captions: “Rebel soldier sketch by Ralph McQuarrie”).

**ANAMORPHIC PROCESS.** Fitting a wide-screen image onto a standard, almost-square film frame by optically “squeezing” it (cylindrical plus spherical lens; unsqueezed in projection). On camera, the developed image is squeezed in the horizontal axis, “half as wide as it should be. A circle becomes an upright oval…” These distorted images are “particularly difficult to deal with in the creation of optical effects, so the ILM technicians usually use a nonanamorphic camera system (VistaVision) when they composite images.”

**BI-PACK.** Two films run through a camera or projector at once, one usually a matte. “*Star Wars*” matte paintings photographed with a bi-pack camera system; twin magazines, emulsions touching. At ILM also on optical printers and aerial image printers for traveling-matte elements.

**BLUESCREEN PROCESS.** Filming in front of a brightly illuminated blue screen; after optical rephotography, actors/objects against black, and silhouettes against clear-white, so subjects can be placed into any background (silhouette cuts a moving black hole; the image is inserted). At ILM, bluescreens are self-lit with fluorescent tubes; subject lit white or amber; no blue reflecting off the subject, no subject light spilling onto the screen. Developed film rephotographed twice: a filter that turns all the blue to black (subject unaltered), and a filter that turns the blue to white and blocks other colors (subject becomes the silhouette).

**BLUESPILL.** When light from the blue screen illuminates the object and is seen by the camera. Uncorrected, “these contaminated areas may become transparent during the optical compositing process and create an undesirable matte line wherever the blue light fell on the object.” Common when the subject is glossy or white, or at a reflecting angle. Some lenses more susceptible. “An experienced bluescreen cinematographer is often able to eliminate or minimize bluespill through special lighting techniques.”

**FRONT PROJECTION.** “Also known as the Alekan-Gerard process and the Scotchlite process.” Highly reflective screen behind the subject; projector next to the camera at ninety degrees; beam splitter reflects the projected image toward the screen while the camera sees through. Screen so reflective that only a very dim projection light is used, so foreground objects do not reflect the projected image.

**GARBAGE MATTES.** Animated mattes that block out “garbage” (unwanted lights, cables, C-stands, unused areas of FX elements). “Usually they are quickly made with pieces of black paper placed above a light box and photographed with an animation camera.”

**OPTICAL COMPOSITE.** A finished special-effects shot of two or more images combined on an optical printer — “as simple as a flash of lightning… or as complicated as fifty individual spaceships in a space battle.” Most commonly, actors photographed on a stage placed into imaginary environments combining live shots and matte paintings.

**ROTOSCOPE.** Individual frames blown up and traced, one at a time, onto animation cels. Live action can become animation; ghostly effects added; “technical marvels can be put into real people’s hands (the lightsabers), and so on.”
