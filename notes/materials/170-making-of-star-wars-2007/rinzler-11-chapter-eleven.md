# 170 — Chapter Eleven: Celluloid Transfiguration (December 1976 to April 1977)

Print pp. 242–283 (TOC: chapter starts p. 242; Chapter Twelve starts p. 284). Typography from the Enhanced Edition XHTML. Quotes circa 1975–1978 unless flagged. Rinzler glosses flagged.

Christmas dinner at Park Way: the whole company at one table. **Howard Hammerman** (started June 1976, general repairman) teases Lucas about sound in space. Lucas: “I’ll tell you what. We’re going to put a little platform out there floating in space [laughs] with an orchestra on it so that it’ll make sense.” January 1977: only **Paul Hirsch** stays on editorial; originally due at year’s end, “George asked me to stay until the end of the picture.” Escape from the Death Star begun **29 September 1976** after Johnston reboarded it; by late January Lucas has approved most of those shots. Combining Luke’s two trench runs eases the load (Muren). George would give “a rough storyboard representing one frame… ‘make it run about 86 frames.’ ” Van Der Veer: written laser-battle instructions and lightsabers; ILM animation does the Death Star blast and vets outside work. **Blalack**: once trained, a medium-difficulty shot in “about two and one-half weeks.” Film Control so crowded they knock out the wall into Johnston’s art department.

## OVERTURES

Temp track before showing a cut to John Williams. Lucas designed the film as a “silent movie,” primarily visuals and music. **Hirsch**: flip side of Stravinsky’s *The Rite of Spring* for Threepio in the desert and Jawa music (George: “nobody ever uses that side of the record”); Rózsa *Ivanhoe* for the main title; “George was talking about having a majority of the film set to music.” **Burtt**: Dvořák *New World* Symphony for the great-hall awards; Bruckner’s Ninth for Luke’s theme; essentially complete FX plus patchwork music; “At this point Johnny Williams was brought in.” 14 December 1976 Dan O’Bannon status to “Gary Kirtz” [print typography as in EE]: gunport targeting (Jay Teitzell) done; “bomb down shaft” (John Wash) done; tractor-beam wall screen and rebel targeter awaiting camera; only unfinished artwork the “Death Star wall screen (approach to Yavin 4)”—“considered by all to be the most important… Imperial technology should appear more advanced” (Image West later).

**Williams**: “I started with George just after New Year’s… he showed me the film.” With Lucas, Lionel Newman, Ken Wannberg, and Hirsch, they spot cues over two days. **Hirsch**: a lot of music in the first three-quarters; after the gunport, “no music for a long time… You have to go slow in order to go fast.” **Williams**: source music convinced him George was right about the idiom — not electronic, not “futuristic cliché, outer space noises.” Because the picture is so physically unfamiliar, “the music should be on fairly familiar emotional ground… the disparity of styles was the right thing.” Writes the score in January and February. Even before the new year, veteran sound editor **Sam Shaw** (*American Graffiti, Cabaret, The Candidate*) starts; most of his crew mid-January. **Lucas**: Burtt created sounds for “a couple of years”; Shaw cuts the final and remaining effects; “Ben did mainly the special intricate things that had to be created.” Burtt uneasy handing over the library; asked to do it themselves; George and Gary: no time. Shaw’s first task: C-3PO and R2-D2 motors (Burtt had never reached them). **Shaw**: “There is more sound in that one picture than there is in ten average pictures put together. There were over a hundred effect units for each sound reel, so over twelve hundred reels.”

## Escape from the Death Star

**Blalack**: the sequence is about 15 percent of optical’s total output; first time they see their work with live action and sound; “the energy level went up significantly.”

127-P (*P* = processed): docking-bay miniature from English set blueprints, comes apart in four pieces; ceiling reused. **McCune**: “Up on the ceiling on one of the gantries… is the most exact miniature of the Dykstraflex… upside down hanging from the roof.”

129-P: **Johnston**: George suddenly: “Now I’ve done it! We drove them right in there, and now they’re escaping, but it’s hard for them to escape—if they’re *backing* out!” Docking bay going through the Death Star discarded (planet-sized). Third-draft original: land on the cloud-city prison; “Alderaan was the mushroom city where they were going to go out the other side.” Last-minute cuts left the problem unanticipated. Solution: “back out… spin around, and take off” on a “very tricky model mount.” **Edlund**: most of a day to program; normally 20–30 minutes once the model is up.

132: McQuarrie Death Star matte (~three feet), blue where stars go, matching starfield pan, *Falcon* with the same move; three elements on the optical printer.

133-P: Luke’s stars left to right, Han’s right to left; yaw and subtle variations; multidirectional roaming stars from “icons” off the original negative (never used as a printing element).

137-P: **Beckett** on communications: “George doesn’t like light effects” via Blalack is a general judgment; “he dislikes light effects that look like matte lines. He likes light effects that look like light being cast by laser beams.” TIE cockpit from England; after other effects the black-costumed pilot “had become greenish black”; Lucas unhappy — looked rotoscoped in, not part of the set.

138-P: **Dykstra**: “Lasers are a terrible pain in the ass… some cockamamie perspective…” Lucas never happy with the effect despite tests.

153-P: first ILM explosion the roto department enhanced; sent to England and “apparently approved”; later “wasn’t approved at all”; redone with gas, powder, metal flakes. **Johnston**: TIE wings warp under stage lights; thin perimeter beams holding paper.

## THE FOX PREVIEW

Late January: first outside-group screening. Mix for Williams with the temp track; then Ladd, his wife, Gareth Wigan, Jay Kanter, and Leonard Kroll, in the screening room behind Park Way. **Lucas**, typography locked: sitting next to Gareth, “he turned to me and he had tears in his eyes… I thought he was crying because he was thinking, *Oh my God—our whole careers are destroyed.* But he said, ‘This is the greatest film I’ve ever seen.’ ” Wigan, home: “The most extraordinary day of my life has just taken place…” **Lucas**: “Laddie didn’t know what to think. His wife loved the movie… Laddie was quiet and said, ‘Gee, George, you did a really good job.’ ” After they leave, Lucas and Hirsch: “It could’ve been worse!” **Barwood** (with two ~ten-year-old kids): slugs and Magic Marker, “knocked silly… I’d been set up to be stunned because of my doubts previously.”

Theater owners remain uneducated and unenthusiastic. Fox alone backs the film (unlike co-financed *Lucky Lady* and *Hello, Dolly*). **Ladd**: no guarantees; exhibitors going heavily into *A Bridge Too Far, Exorcist II, Sorcerer,* and *The Deep.* Bid forms out since October, without a print: exclusive 12-week run and 90/10 split — “strangely exorbitant” given studio confidence, scaring most exhibitors.

January principal interviews (Lippincott), still in the dark. **4 January, Fisher**: “when I see it, I’m going to be as surprised as anyone… Are they going to have my planet blown up?” **5 January, Hamill**: “I would do anything for George. I’d go paint his house… He told me once that he didn’t want to make features anymore, that he wanted to go back and make student movies. I’ll do those, too.” **20 January, Ford**: “I don’t know what it is going to look like, not at all… It was a mystery. And still is to this day.”

## Color Relations

**Lucas**, typography locked: “You are sitting in a nice light room for the first half of the film, but in a darker room for the second half… happy and sunny… more oppressed and moody. It’s subtle, but I think it will have an effect on people.” Costumes: “Leia is dressed in white and is part of the technological world—black, white, and gray… She would be like Artoo” on Tatooine (tan, brown, green). Together, everyone except Artoo blends into Tatooine; on the Death Star the opposite — Artoo fits, “primarily white.” Stormtroopers white “also to mix things up, so not all the bad guys were dressed in dark colors.” Threepio “out of place in the Death Star… part of the people, earth side, which was an esoteric idea, but I liked it.” “The only thing that we varied a little bit was the Han Solo costume: He’s dressed in browns, and has a spaceship, because he is an eclectic. He takes a little from everything.”

## NO END IN SIGHT

Mid-January to early February: second and third units, pickups and reshoots; almost all location; only set a reconstituted cantina alcove. **Kurtz**: Fox production meeting said the cantina set would cost $100,000; studio said no. Fifteen or twenty creatures; “the elephant suit would cost $25,000.” Kurtz to Ladd: estimate $30–40,000, “certainly under $50,000,” “extremely important.” Also Fox refused to pay the Huycks $15,000 for their dialogue polish; insisted George pay from writing fees. “That’s why we were so pissed at the studio.” **Rick Baker** (via a friend of Muren; from *The Thing with Two Heads* to *King Kong* 1976 remake): George wants “some more aliens” in the bar; long excited talk. Bid accepted; only **Stuart Freeborn** gets a credit “as he was the primary artist.” Team: Jon Berg, Phil Tippett, Laine Liska, Doug Beswick. Kurtz nets **$20,000** (possibly after Ladd’s preview). Baker: “cut down the number of weeks… George wanted like seven monsters… we ended up with about six weeks to do as many aliens as we could.”

## THE UNITED STATES DESERTS

Alcoves in a small Hollywood stage; McCune, Peterson, Johnston et al. shoot miniatures on location. **Johnston**: six desert trips; four just for the sandcrawler (constantly breaking; “probably ended up costing more per second than anything else”); twice with the landspeeder. One night, model left in a van, “pieces of styrene popping and cracking.” Trip 1: inner bulb breaks, no spare; 2: windy/cloudy; 3: exposure wrong; 4: perfect.

Mid-January, Friday night before the crew leaves: **Hamill**, driving himself, has a bad car accident. **Kurtz**: County General, then Mount Sinai for severity; operated ~9 AM to ~4 PM; at 4:30 Mark says, “Oh. I’m sorry I got delayed. As soon as I get out of here this morning, we can go.” “He evidently had no idea what he looked like.” **Lucas**: at least ten close-ups with Mark lost; “that was just fate… a double for the landspeeder shot.” **Baker** worried about the elephant’s ~300-pound bantha headgear. **Lucas**: “We all fell in love with Mardji… first time she’d ever been out in the real world… just loved to play around in that creek.” Hovering landspeeder: several failed ideas, then a mirror, sturdier/longer in Death Valley, still not enough; **Bob Dalva** (USC classmate, Zoetrope founder) gets one shot; *another* cameraman gets the final two. **Carroll Ballard** (*The Rain People* acquaintance; later *The Black Stallion*, 1979, Coppola producing) is second-unit DP: about two weeks in the desert, then “a tiny stage right across from Kodak” for the bar.

Bantha: **Mardji**, a 22-year-old, 8,500-pound female Asian elephant, normally at Marine World Africa U.S.A.; trainer in Tusken outfit. **Ron Whitfield**: six men, a month; howdah; horns from flexible home ventilation tubing; coat of palm fronds; head mask chicken wire and foam; beard of horse hair; water-skis at the park; wooden bristled tail a problem, compensated with apples. EE video: mid-January 1977 Death Valley dailies; trunk “sneaks out here and there” despite training (no audio, 1:05).

## The People

**Baker**: George wants a band of aliens playing music; Baker suggests mass-casting identical masks from one; “Fine!” **Berg**: primarily from Ron Cobb key drawings; “great deal of freedom.” **Tippett**: four or five plus Rob Botine; “fifteen or sixteen different makeups,” Baker donates more, “about twenty-five monsters” vs. a contract for about seven; donated characters never filmed before. **Berg**: after seeing the cut and ILM, “This was the movie we’d all wanted to see… we got to seven—come on! Let’s keep grinding away at it!”

## The Monsters

As described by Berg and Tippett: **Spider-guy** — “silver furry creature with the little nose and the four eyes, who scratches its head… the most extensive body.” **Hammerhead** — torso and two arms (unseen in film); “looked like a lush who was trying to get his drink.” **Brain-guy** — “A blind old man who had his brain exposed.” **Skull-head** — “A four-armed hooker; half insect, half octopus, with butterfly wings on it and tennis shoes.” **The band**: Beswick sculpts from one of Rick’s designs, mass-cast; Phil in one; “some of the girls from ILM… nearly killed by our own creations” — one tiny hole for the instrument mouthpiece, not enough air; dance to “an old Benny Goodman tape that George brought in”; Jon’s instrument “made from a buoy or a sea anchor.”

## GONZO ILM

February 1977: still no sure May release, three months away; Ray Gosnell intensifies the Fox crackdown. **Dykstra**: arguments with George; “paradoxical situation… he was sick and he does drive himself pretty hard. It was a nightmare for both of us… I was paranoid as hell… Mather had been brought in. I asked, ‘Do I get to fire Mather, if I don’t like him?’ … I truly really like George.” **Kurtz**: “John has a tendency to talk everything to death… explain to you for an hour why it won’t work… organized anarchy.” Dykstra: “I’m not the bad guy! … That’s why I want to get out of doing this…” Also fought with Blalack (Kurtz: composites with minimum dirt and matte lines, but they “didn’t always get along”). Memos: dozens of reshoots, wrong negatives, starfields the wrong way, bad matte lines. “Only two people were fired, and no one ever quit.”

## NOTORIOUS PREVIEW

Mid-February 1977: rough cut for close friends — De Palma, Robbins, Barwood, the Huycks, Spielberg, Jay Cocks, a few from ILM. **Lucas**: “Some were confused… Only Brian, as is his nature, said anything really negative about it.” **Hirsch**: they overdid the fresh pickup monsters; opening prologue still the third-draft “hundred years before”; Brian and Jay felt it should explain what happened *right before* the film. Brian and Matt Robbins missed Kenobi turning off the tractor beam; solved by substituting some robot dialogue and shooting one insert. **Spielberg**: “only a couple dozen final effects shots; most of them were World War II footage… I was probably the only one who liked it.” **Lucas**: Steven: “This is the greatest movie ever made and it’s going to make a hundred million dollars!” Huycks dubious and worried about George; “Brian was saying, ‘What’s all this Force shit?! Where’s all the blood when they shoot people?’ ” **Spielberg**: that night, a “verbal duel in a Chinese restaurant”; “De Palma inspired the new crawl, which gave the audience some kind of story geography.” **Lucas**: “Brian was the one who actually sat down and helped me fix the roll-up, he and Jay Cocks… Brian dictated it to Jay. He typed it up and it got rewritten a couple of times after that.” Ladd calls Spielberg after, anxious: “I think it’s going to make a fortune.” **Scorsese** sees pieces on the KEM, battle scenes with bluescreen: “tapping into this extraordinary revolution in technology, particularly in video games” (Rinzler: Atari’s *Pong*, 1972). Editorial: cantina shortened. Subsequent **Coppola** screening: the only structural changes. **Hirsch**: “Francis thought that there was too much explanation in the beginning… So we moved the first scene inside the Death Star to much later in the film.” Originally Vader, Tarkin, and officers in the second reel, right after C-3PO reunites with R2 in the sandcrawler; after Coppola, the Death Star debate is placed after Ben’s cave, fourth reel. “once you’d followed Luke and picked up Ben, you knew how *bad* the bad guys were.” Chain reaction: “We dropped some scenes that didn’t serve much purpose, like the scene with Vader and one of his aides walking through the Death Star halls, where he was saying something about searching for the robots—we knew all that stuff anyway.” **Coppola**: “so filled with grease-pencil lines, and missing shots, and Japanese fighter planes diving… maybe a tad repetitive.” **Lucas**: Francis saw it later, projector kept breaking, “out of order”; Ballard, living in Francis’s pool house, also there. **Ballard**: “I felt so unbelievably sorry for George… no special effects, just the performances, hardly any music… ‘You can probably fix it. Maybe we can do some retakes.’ … Had I been in the studios at that time, I would have pulled the plug.” Caption: Edlund prepares the opening crawl, “still the one written, with a couple of changes, for the third draft (with old logo).” Seeker ball: made in the model shop in two hours, bluescreen; Jedi Lesson had to go to an outside house (anamorphic 35mm, not VistaVision); Lucas instructions dated **19 February 1977**. Fisher hologram filmed on a turntable, multiple angles.

## MATTE MAGIC

20–25 February 1977: McQuarrie on “*Star Wars* one-sheet roughs.” His duties winding down; more experienced matte painter needed. **Harrison Ellenshaw** (son of Peter Ellenshaw) contacted nearly a year earlier. Spring 1976 McQuarrie planet mattes reused creatively (escape pod, Star Destroyers, *Falcon*). **Ellenshaw**: Jim Nelson, January or February ’76; Kurtz with McQuarrie paintings a week later; deal completed when George returned from England. First resisted following his father; Alan Maley tutelage. Still heading Disney’s matte department; ~$1,050/week as sole freelancer, Master Film Effects; Disney allowed it (as they had for *The Man Who Fell to Earth*, 1976 — how word reached Lucas). Big push February–March 1977; average four to six weeks per painting, many simultaneous. Stop-motion Bell & Howell camera box, originally 1912 for Disney, one-second turnover, painted matte plus live action onto the negative. First three rough; “George loved them… I was sucked in.” Didn’t mind redoing. Great hall, causing ILM problems, turned over to him (Blalack: “magnificent”).

## Matte Painting Notes by Harrison Ellenshaw

Storyboards marked DISNEY or PETER — “he officially changed his name to Harrison from Peter, so he wouldn’t be confused with his father… sometime after the release of *Star Wars*” (Rinzler).

Great hall: George wanted a shaft of light on Luke, Chewbacca, and Han, others “almost in black”; production footage overlit the sides; couldn’t go as dramatic as desired or it wouldn’t intercut. “I repainted completely three times… columns were too thin, the blacks would go funny,” added people, reduced the plate, men in black, another plate, window shafts.

Sandcrawler at dusk: Death Valley plate, Jawas carrying Artoo; heads almost behind the painting; magic hour, “rusty glow.”

Point of view of “Hoe Eisley from ridere” [EE typography/typos as printed]: Tunisia over Guinness’s shoulder unused (jiggle rules out a matte); Death Valley empty valley POV. George: “I want the buildings bigger and taller… ‘You wouldn’t see the buildings at that distance’… ‘go out to Mulholland Drive on a clear day’… happy compromise.”

Luke and Leia in power trench: laser blast as an added exposure (three slits, successive one-two-three, three or four frames) rather than another generation.

Ben on power-trench ledge: “way the hell up there”; “blue power glow” on and off through ripple glass; plate reduced slightly. **Larry Cuba**: tractor-beam machine screens are “little versions of the Death Star that actually relate to the Death Star I used later in the briefing room… they got the data from the Death Star—they stole it.”

Chasm near “elevatore”: McQuarrie started **7 February**, Ellenshaw finished (~30 hours), bi-pack, couldn’t reduce the plate, “slightly soft.”

“Hassassi” hangar, interior: one complete X-wing, a Y-wing, bits; same plate reduced twice and painted around, “lots of little lights.”

Exterior: first “almost Mayan-like”; George “dead silence… ‘Would it be possible to make it look more like Ralph’s?’ ” Repainted; then moving bluescreen foliage on the optical printer.

*Millennium Falcon* in hangar: two paintings — Leia’s “You came in that thing?” (quick) and a longer establishing; England plate of stormtroopers plus 5247 still of the miniature, rear-projected and blended.

McQuarrie’s Yavin matte used in at least two shots.

## VADER’s VOICE

**1 March 1977**, Goldwyn Studios, one day, voice only. **James Earl Jones** chosen for Darth Vader dialogue, **$7,500**. **Jones**, typography locked: “George Lucas always wanted a voice in the bass register. I understand that George did contact Orson Welles to read for the voice of Darth Vader before he contacted me. I was out of work and he said, ‘Do you want a day’s work?’ ” **Lucas**: “He was the best actor that I could possibly find. He has a deep, commanding voice.” With Lucas’s prompting, Jones watches the scenes, studies “his character’s lack of emotion,” invests “a menace that would’ve been difficult to imagine before.” Masked, no lip-sync; finishes in about **two and a half hours**. **Jones**: “Vader is a man who never learned the beauties and subtleties of human expression. So we figured out the key to my work was to keep it on a very narrow band of expression—that was the secret.” Technique originated with Walter Murch on *American Graffiti*: “worldizing” (**Sam Shaw**): play the sound back through speakers in a regular room “so it has a natural presence… and then re-recording it.” **Burtt** hunts breathing. **Lucas**: “He did about eighteen different kinds, through Aqua-Lungs and through tubes, trying to find the one that had the right sort of mechanical sound. And then we had to decide whether it would be totally rhythmical like an iron lung—that’s the idea, which was a whole part of the plot that essentially got cut out.”

Perhaps the same day, **Larry Ward** does Greedo’s voice-over. **Lucas**: first try, a person going “Oink-oink,” fast, with “electric equipment… a very bizarre language, but that didn’t work.” Burtt and “a graduate student from UC Berkeley, who is a real expert in languages,” invent a language; Burtt edits; processed electronically “to give it a phasing sound.” Deal: 25 February 1977 note, Lucy Kroll Agency to James Nelson, agreed terms; much later, 29 August 1977, Bunny Alsup sends the deal memo to Jacob Bloom for the legal files.

## ROMANCE AND MUSIC

Williams begins soundtrack recordings on schedule in England, **5 March 1977** — a fait accompli (budget; sessions coincide with looping). Anvil Studios, Denham (old J. Arthur Rank; near Pinewood); Williams had done *Fiddler on the Roof* and *Jane Eyre* there, 1971. Fourteen sessions, about seven working days; a session is three hours; normally morning and afternoon, a couple of days three sessions (~twelve-hour days). Forty-two hours, **London Symphony Orchestra** — Williams’s first organized symphony for a film. “particularly the brass section… such nobility and such a wonderful heraldic sound.” Lucas flies over to produce; incredibly happy; phones Spielberg so he can hear about half an hour of themes first-played, the receiver directed at the musicians. Rinzler ranks it with Herrmann (*Vertigo, Psycho, North by Northwest*), Jarre *Lawrence of Arabia*, Steiner *Casablanca*, Korngold *Robin Hood*. Operatic leitmotifs. **Williams**: “the music relates to the characters and the human problems, even when they are Wookiees… a very romantic theme for the Princess, a heroic march for the Jedi Knights—all of this material has to do with the fairy-tale aspect of it.” Didn’t want “a piece of Dvořák here, a piece of Tchaikovsky there… themes of our own… a solid dramaturgical glue from start to finish.” **Lucas**: “a very Max Steiner–type of romantic movie score… I decided just to do it all the way down the line… old-fashioned and fun, but going for the most dramatic and emotional elements that I could get.”

Between sessions, Lucas runs to London to loop Guinness, Hamill, et al.; some read at Anvil. **Daniels** does C-3PO despite dozens who auditioned. **Lucas**: “It was primarily because of the fact that it was a British voice. I really wanted to keep the whole thing American… I wanted Threepio’s voice to be slightly more used-car-dealer-ish, a little more oily. More of a con man, which is the way it was written, and not really a fussy British robot butler… We went through thirty people that I actually tested, but none of the voices were as good as Tony’s, so we kept him.” Rinzler: possibly the used-car dealer cut from *American Graffiti*. EE audio: Lucas directing Guinness ADR, March 1977, various line readings (0:51). **Daniels** on the beep cue; enjoys watching; “the banthas are one of the best things… George of course won’t tell me how they were done… I used to see George standing up against all these physically big people… getting his own way very quietly. And if he couldn’t get his own way, then accepting the fact.” As Lucas leaves London, McQuarrie, *Star Wars* work finished, arrives England **25 March** to begin concept art for the first *Star Trek* motion picture. EE audio: Williams on the “Death of Ben” cue, Lippincott interview **22 April 1977** (0:51).

## Star Wars and Psycho

Hirsch, longest-serving editor, partly responsible for Herrmann’s late-career resurgence: laid *Psycho* (1960) as temp on De Palma’s *Sisters* (1973); Herrmann, out of favor with Hitchcock, scored it and revived his American career. For *Star Wars* temp: “The scene where they pop out of the hatch in the *Falcon*, I laid in a very famous piece of *Psycho* music… a three-note motif that Scorsese had insisted that Benny use in *Taxi Driver.* … Johnny—I don’t know if he did it deliberately or what—but it’s now incorporated into his cue for that moment… the very same notes that appear in *Psycho*, they appear in *Taxi Driver*—and now they’re in *Star Wars*.”

## GARBAGE CANS AND THE TEMPLE OF TIKAL

While Lucas is in England much of March, only one camera crew needed; **Edlund**, **Richard Alexander**, and **Pepi Lenzi** (translator/guide; Ray Gosnell contact, “get-’em-there” guy, ten languages) go to Guatemala for Yavin jungle plates, twelve Johnston storyboards. “one week… 1,200 pounds of luggage.” Thirty-five cases (Technorama camera, built 1931 as a Technicolor camera for Disney); derelict DC-3 to Tikal, “oil… dribbling,” Levi’s-covered seats, chickens, not enough seat belts, beans and rice, ~21 hours, mud runway. Jungle Inn, grass huts, peacocks, snakes; ~90 degrees, smoke rings hanging twenty minutes. Eight guides, five or six miles; “exact place on top of temple number three… first ledge… a couple of hundred feet up… only about six feet wide.” Coral snake ingesting a bat in a cave. Two shotgun guards overnight. Key shot: Rebel lookout — two $28 trash cans on an aluminum pole (tested at Sepulveda Dam; police stopped). Nobody wanted to climb in; a local, then Alexander, then **Lorne Peterson** (joins day three) is coaxed into the crow’s nest; also dressed as the Rebel tracking the pirate ship with a Minolta spot meter, tube and batteries taped on, “to make it look sci fi.” Shooting five days, away eight; worst part: flat tire, San Diego Freeway rush hour with all the luggage. Early 1976 the planned destination had been Mexico. Caption typo: “Pepe Lenzi” / “Lome Peterson.”

## Themes by John Williams

Ben Kenobi and Darth Vader: “I think of Ben’s theme as also being the theme of the Jedi Knights, the Old Republic that Ben remembers. It also overlaps into the area of being the theme for the Force, the good Force that Ben represents. There is a lot of English horn in Ben’s theme, which is often heard under dialogue… Vader’s theme is a lot of bassoons and muted trombones and low things, since he is the bad side of the Force.”

Luke: “fanfare-ish and brassy and bold and masculine and noble… the full glow of the glorious brass section of the London Symphony Orchestra [LSO].”

Leia: “very romantic. The first time Luke sees her, he says how beautiful she is. It really is a fairy-tale princess melody.”

Sunset: George asked for Ben’s theme once he’d heard it; Williams had scored it with Luke’s; “he was very right.”

Cantina: George’s temp; “What he said to me was, ‘Can you imagine these creatures in some future century having found in a time capsule or under a rock an old 1930s Benny Goodman swing-band record? Can you imagine what their distorted idea of how to play it would be?’ ”

Ben’s death: part of the princess theme at the beginning — “the most sweeping melody,” and “what’s inside of her and Luke during their reaction to his death.”

Death Star battle: “exciting battle music… includes most if not all of the themes in this picture.”

Throne room: “big fanfare… Ben’s theme is used in a kind of parade way. In this sense it represents the triumph of the values of the Old Republic… presentation of the medals… a kind of ‘land of hope and glory’ bit. It is almost like coronation music.”

## STOP-MOTION CHESS

After cantina “gross-looking” sculptures sold George (**Muren** / Tippett / Berg), Lucas, back from England late March 1977, hires Tippett and Berg for the chess set (droids vs. Chewbacca). **Berg**: originally “little people in costumes placed on a giant chessboard”; *Futureworld* (1976) had already used the concept, “took the wind out of their sails.” **Tippett**: “Make them about six inches tall… ‘I don’t care. They can slither and slide, or you can put wheels on them.’ ” Ten sculptures, latex/foam/plastic foam; McCune makes the board. George blocks the sequence the afternoon before sound mixing: “a collaboration.” Muren sets shots; two low-angle close-ups not used — “Once the audience had seen a thing in the movie, he didn’t want them to see it again later on.” 4-perf Mitchell with squeeze lens to match backgrounds. Night work against black velvet; eight figures, board split (Jon four including lead characters, Phil four); singles, one frame at a time; out at dawn; five days.

## THEY KEPT ON TRUCKIN’

Latter half of March: many FX short of Lucas’s original vision (time, money, or technically impossible). **Edlund**: George’s visits dwindle to Mondays and Tuesdays, then just Mondays. **Muren**: Saturdays; shoot as many as possible without unloading the camera (save twenty minutes, maybe an hour/day); “George compromised on his end and we compromised on our end… It would’ve been nice if there had been another three months… although we were pretty burned out.” **Hirsch**: constant changes after Williams scored; Ken Wannberg tailors cues. **Burtt**: music dropped in two places — Artoo ambushed by Jawas (played better without), and the Dia Noga (“more frightening and suspenseful without the music”). Marketing/distribution screening, end of March, Park Way, ~25–30 people from a rented airport tour bus. **Ladd**, in Hollywood, waits by the phone, breaks a lunch date, finally: “Everybody was crowded into a phone booth saying, ‘The picture is extraordinary. I don’t even believe what I’ve seen!’ ” **Kurtz**: “their attitude changed a bit… Ray Gosnell and the production office got off our back.” EE audio: Lippincott check-in, **24 March 1977** (0:51).

## GIANT IMPERIAL STAR DESTROYERS

December 1976–April 1977: the opening shot — “It’s the first two or three shots in the movie, where the big ship is chasing the little ship—that was my vision of the movie” (Lucas). Budget cuts reduce Imperial ships from several to one. Shots 101 and 102 paperwork:

21 DECEMBER 1976: SHOT 102, REBEL BLOCKADE RUNNER NEEDS TO BE RESHOT. THERE IS A PROBLEM WITH THE MATTE LINE.

25 JANUARY 1977: SHOT 102… GL WANTS STAR DESTROYER ALMOST TWICE AS BIG, SO THAT IT FILLS THE SCREEN. REBEL SHIP WOULD GO OVER THE STAR DESTROYER FOR ENTIRE SHOT.

8 APRIL 1977: SHOT 101: LAZERS ARE TO BE OPAQUED OUT OF TAIL… JD MARKED ON PRINT WHERE LAZERS ARE TO STOP FOR FINA COMP (3 FRAMES BEFORE KEY #D6X89474). [typography as printed]

Lucas also wants the Rebel ship to enter from the upper right instead of directly overhead. **Edlund**: “the most important shot in the show… the ‘leap of faith.’ … If I lost any sleep… it was when I was worried we might blow it by making one shot that would make someone in the audience say, ‘Model!’ ” Star Destroyer only ~three feet long, must fill the screen ~fifteen seconds; Rebel ship more like six feet, must look swallowed by a five- or six-inch docking bay. George considered a huge Destroyer model / fifteen-foot section / even revamping the opening, but “had his heart set on… ‘no cut.’ ” Edlund test: tiny model on a wire in front of the Destroyer, camera move against black. Kurtz: George reluctantly agrees it might work; kept as a potential redo. Johnston: two guys detailing the Destroyer for weeks, especially the bay. **Blalack**: “We wanted to improve on that one, but we never got that shot right.” **Lind**: four synchronizers, two sawhorses, and a door for all the elements (stars, large moon, small moon, horizon, runner, “mother Star Destroyer,” two flak, two lasers, planet mattes). Lucas signs off mid-April 1977. **Burtt**: opening pass-by, music and FX conflict; wanted the ship to “come exploding through”; music “camouflaged the explosiveness”; “we compromised, half and half of each.” **Lucas** on crawl color: light blue for the first words, “less of an impact than the yellow STAR WARS… We went back and forth with green, which was put at the end. The beginning and the end are the earth colors… The beginning is the fairy-tale part… *I’m going to tell you a story: Once upon a time, there was a boy who lived on a planet*…” Models composited with McQuarrie’s three-planet matte. McCune: everything but the right topside of the Destroyer built (access); docking bay 20–30 bulbs, six weeks (Beasley and Jones). Rebel ship hung upside down “to simulate the vacuum of space”; pulse-motor radar; pirate-ship guns already taken for the *Falcon*; five or six high-speed VistaVision takes of a pyro behind the radar tower. EE video: B&W dupe, winter 1977, different opening crawl (technical difficulties) and grease-pencil blaster marks (no audio, 1:31).

## IN THE TRENCHES

End-battle pieces handed over mid-December 1976; ILM doesn’t finish until mid-April 1977. **Johnston**: George would “pick out a shot or even pick out a frame and say, ‘Move the ship forty-two degrees this way’… He knew exactly how he wanted it.” Variety of Death Stars needed (matte won’t rotate; surface vs. trench; size increased). **McQuarrie**: “The Death Star became a sphere of enormous proportions, when the guys at ILM decided that it had to have a flat surface in order to run their camera above it and then we calculated the size of the sphere that would have a flat horizon when you were standing on the surface.” “Threshold scale” for low passes; “sub-miniature scale” surface from photographs of parts at 150-to-1 on a curved surface. Modular blocks (Johnston concepts) in almost endless patterns; battle modeled on WWII dogfights. Trench scale: Dykstraflex only forty feet; trench supposed to be about forty miles → a mile a foot; eight modular units of three sizes; surface, six pieces; ~$5–6/sq ft (manufacturer wanted a credit, then went bankrupt). **McCune**: disagreement on trench size (3×3 then 2 deep × 3 wide); built three times (a month, two weeks, three hours); finally “about sixty running feet,” including the target end-piece. Then ~10,000 sixteenth-inch retro-reflective tape “windows”; George thought them all too big; McCune, Peterson, and Gawley paint out half of each. **Johnston**: “the equivalent of being sent to the Russian front… It took us months to build.” Many assumed an equatorial trench; per Johnston it is “one of eighteen trenches that started about halfway up from the equator and continued to the northern pole.” **Kurtz**: George wanted walls “close to a blur”; “John’s biggest argument was that if the trench is three hundred yards wide, the walls are not going to go by fast… that argument George and John had a lot.” Director eventually makes his point. Death Star explosion: Edlund, Borendo stage, Photo-Sonics super-trick 35mm anamorphic up to 300 fps (Logan). **Viskocil**: “special titan blend… gas explosion, then silver sparks… George just flipped and said, ‘There’s our Death Star, no doubt about it!’ ” 13 January 1977: shot “182 DP,” three X-wings; #1 X-wing negative goes missing (Lind, three days, reshoot); 21 January Muren reshoots all three; 25 February Death Star color toned down (too blue); 18 March one X-wing still problematic, Lucas considers only two — all three appear in the final.

## TRANSFERENCE

Mid-April 1977 the end battle goes to **Sam Shaw** (sound mix by day, dubbing at night). Example: ~2,000 silent feet before they blow the Death Star; Burtt library, all augmented; gunshots, explosions, ricochets, passbys, interiors; dub 6–7 PM to 8–9 AM, then mixers until at least 3 PM, sleep, up at 5. “Every night on the stage, you’d see George sitting there with his fast-food burger.” **Burtt**: barely making days; no time to correct mistakes; becomes George’s representative at Hollywood mixes; “sparks” with mixers when he asks for changes; “George would concur when he came.” **Muren**: a shot diving toward then flying through the trench — he shot the first half matching McQuarrie’s painting to motions Edlund had already shot in the trench. Group photo circa 4 February 1977, many (not all) ILM staffers, 1977 in-house titles, including “Jim Nelson, ‘chief mushroom’ ” and John Dykstra’s dog. EE video: cast-and-crew gag reel, some without sound (2:35).
