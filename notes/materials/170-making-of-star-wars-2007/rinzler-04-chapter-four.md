# 170 — Chapter Four: Frenzy (August 1975 to September 1975)

Print pp. 64–81 (TOC: chapter starts p. 64; Chapter Five starts p. 82). Typography from the Enhanced Edition XHTML. Audio captions EE-only. Quotes circa 1975–1978 unless flagged (the Roger Christian clip is a 2012 Rinzler interview, not a Lost Interview). Rinzler glosses flagged.

## Opening (p. 64)

August 1975: Lucas and Kurtz in England for the first of several brief preproduction stays. Kurtz arrives first; Pinewood is the front-runner. **1 August:** production designer John Barry officially starts, renting space at Lee Electrics in London. First assignments: a landspeeder, and the robots with John Stears. Cardboard R2-D2 mock-up from the McQuarrie paintings. **Kenny Baker** hired to be the man inside the droid. He arrives with cabaret partner **Jack Purvis**: “I said, ‘I can’t just walk into a movie and leave my partner stranded.’ They said, ‘Well, we have plenty of work for Jack,’ and they made him head Jawa.” **Barry**: Kenny is “only forty inches” and “very strong”; specially made boots lace the robot boot to his leg “so that it moves as one”; harness “quite important.” **Baker**: “It just wasn’t very comfortable… They had screws going through the robot’s head that stuck into *my* head.” Mid-August, Barry shows Lucas: Kenny “came around the corner in the studio in this little wooden dust bin, a sort of mock-out trash can. He’s waddling across the floor, and George says at once, ‘That’ll work.’ ” **Stears**: a mechanical R2 also needed for things Kenny “was not physically capable of doing”; studies amputee limbs, atomic-research mechanical arms, a robotics professor “who thought I was crazy.” Barry: panels and doors all over so another arm or antenna can be “right behind that panel.”

Art department grows to about twenty-five to thirty. **Harry Lange** (class of *2001*) on control panels: “come back with an eggbeater and half a vacuum cleaner.” **Liz Moore** on the landspeeder and C-3PO fabrication. **Robert Watts** (production supervisor): Kurtz first spoke to him in 1973 (*The Wilby Conspiracy*, Kenya); Peter Beale (Fox, London) set a 1 May 1975 Soho Square meeting; further talk in June; now the last interview, with Lucas. Watts’s path: Ealing office boy 1960, then *Repulsion*, *Thunderball*, *You Only Live Twice*. EE audio (Rinzler EE): set dresser Roger Christian remembers the beginnings of R2-D2 (Interview by J. W. Rinzler, **2012**; 2:37) — later than the Lost Interviews; do not collapse with 1975–78.

**Barry** on Lucas’s taste vs. his own: “My personal taste leans more toward *Barbarella* [campy sci-fi film, 1968], which George didn’t want to do. He wants to make *The Star Wars* look like it’s shot on location on your average, everyday Death Star or Mos Eisley spaceport.” Sets designed “simultaneously,” knowing how one turns into the next; sets are “a large percentage of the overall budget, in that there’s not a large number of very expensive actors.” Barry flies to the States; McQuarrie’s notes: arrives **20 August 1975**; they meet Lucas over the paintings. **Lucas**: “John was very willing to step in and say, ‘Yeah, these are good designs, let’s use those.’ He wasn’t hung up on the idea that everything would have to be his.” Barry idea for the Alderaan escape: retractable bridge, Luke throws a hook and swings Leia across — “the ‘Lash La Rue’ scene.” McQuarrie sat in on about three days of talks; “it was definitely established at that point that I was a sketch artist and not the art director.” McQuarrie later painting titled “Lash La Rue scene—Luke vaults over space left by retractable bridge” (21 November 1975); also Alderaan interior and “the elevator scene” — individual tubes rather than open doors; “this big canyon wall was part of that Lash La Rue scene.”

## A CAST OF THOUSANDS

Late August: Lucas begins interviewing actors while still on the fourth draft, Fox contract still outstanding, ILM still setting up. **Fred Roos** organizes, unpaid, from the American Zoetrope office in Los Angeles (next door). “Fred didn’t get paid or anything… He really just did it as a friend” (Lucas). Roos: “Deciding to cast unknowns was not a controversial decision, because nobody cared at the time.” Casting director **Dianne Crittenden** (possibly via the Huycks / *Lucky Lady*; she and Coppola had been at Hofstra together); two assistants as interviews multiply. She hadn’t read the script or talked to George “until right before we were ready to start.” Brief: “We need two young guys and a girl for this thing, and George will be here for three weeks.” She reads it eight times “before I finally got into it.” Casting brief, typography locked — **Crittenden**: “But George knew what he wanted. He wanted Han to be a cowboy, the ‘James Dean’ character, but instead of a horse, he’s got a spaceship. Luke was a farmer. I said, ‘What do you mean, a farmer?’ And he said, ‘It’s a moon-farm or an interplanetary farm.’ George’s concept of the princess was an independent, strong one. Someone who was capable of leading her people, as opposed to some fragile fairy-tale kind of princess.”

Three weeks at the Zoetrope offices, Goldwyn Studios, late August: 8 or 9 AM to 6 or 8:30 PM, “as quickly as two people every five minutes, around 250 actors per day.” **Lucas**: “I saw thousands of kids… one minute, three minutes, five minutes… Your first impression of them is the same impression more or less as the audience’s when they meet them… with film, in a close-up, you are looking into somebody’s eyes… I have to rely half on the human being and half on the actor.” Typical exchange (Crittenden): “What have you been doing?” — “You mean, today, or with my life?” — “Either.” They’d ask what it was about; “Well, we’re doing a science-fiction movie.” Hundreds of later phone calls: “What was I doing there?” “George was looking for something very *visual.*”

## DOUBLE JEOPARDY

Brian De Palma, also hiring for *Carrie* (1976), sits in: “I’ll just sit in on this.” Adds confusion — more people knew Brian’s name; they’d praise *Hi, Mom!* (1970). “Which didn’t bother George… it became easier for him to watch the performances.” Scores: “George would say, ‘A definite three,’ and Brian would say, ‘I’d give them a four.’ ” Editor **Paul Hirsch**: “Brian was terrifying these new actors… they got it down to less than a minute. If they knew they weren’t interested… no sooner had George finished the opening speech than Brian would launch into the closing speech.” During that time George tells Hirsch he liked his work, “maybe we’d work someday together”; Hirsch already wanted *Star Wars*. De Palma would visit ILM miniatures and tell Hirsch he was envious: “Brian was doing this movie with girls slamming doors and George was having all this fun making models.” **Lucas**: the sessions are “ten hours a day, with a person coming into your office every two minutes… So Brian asked if he could sit in… it’s great to have company… so you don’t become completely blotto toward the end of the day.” Captions: McQuarrie early Han sketches “may reflect Lucas’s changing idea of his appearance and costume, from a more savage pirate to a more debonair James Bond or gunslinger type.” Another McQuarrie drawing “perhaps reflects Lucas’s fixed pursuit of Alec Guinness for the role of Ben Kenobi—the drawing, which is markedly different from earlier depictions, has the hairdo and look of Guinness. Written by Lucas in the corner are the words ‘Ben Kenobi.’ ” Costume concept for Leia with blaster in holster.

## THE GAUNTLET

**Lucas**: “In the beginning we were very open. We had two male characters, so I wanted the farm boy to be shorter than Han Solo, so they didn’t conflict visually… I also made a decision in the casting to lower the age considerably from what I’d had in mind, because I wanted to make a movie about kids for kids, though, in a way, it’s an adult kid’s movie.” **Crittenden** on Jodie Foster, “about fifteen at the time. We were down to that age. The princess was supposed to be about sixteen, Luke was about eighteen, and Han was in his early twenties. He was the old guy. But for purposes of shooting and having a full eight-hour day, people had to be over eighteen.” Letters to every school; some who had never acted. First week, by the third day: “we’d seen nearly everybody—John Travolta, Nick Nolte, Tommy Lee Jones… Mark came in the second day.” Twenty-four-year-old **Mark Hamill** already had a recurring role in *The Texas Wheelers*; Crittenden: “the TV series almost kept Mark from doing it”; she persuaded agent Nancy Hudson that a feature would be better than “career stagnating in television.” **Hamill**: no script; “the only thing she knew about the kid was that he’s from a farm… I started practicing a midwestern accent… Thousands of people sitting on the floor; you wait two and a half hours… I talked to Brian De Palma. I think he introduced me, but George didn’t say anything all the way through it. I told them about my brothers and sisters, and how we moved around, living in Japan.” De Palma hired Travolta, Bill Katt, Sissy Spacek, and Amy Irving; Lucas was not deciding yet.

## RETURN OF THE LADYKILLER

Crittenden: second week “into the dregs already”; third week “just pulling people from colleges. But George wanted to see *everybody.* After a while Brian got tired and he dropped out.” Lucas flirts with casting only African-Americans in key parts; talks with Lawrence Hilton Jacobs (Freddie “Boom Boom” Washington on *Welcome Back, Kotter*). Also considers doing the whole film in Japanese with subtitles — Rinzler: not just casting drudgery, but Kurosawa and “the feeling of disorientation he’d felt as a student watching films from different cultures… a foreign film as if it had just washed up on the shore—all of its customs, history, language, and mannerisms strangely exotic, somewhat familiar, but not explained,” informing *Star Wars* as it had *THX 1138*. **Crittenden**: “There was talk at one point about having the princess and Ben Kenobi be Japanese, which led George into thinking Han Solo might be black.” **Lucas**, typography locked: “This was actually when I was looking for Ben Kenobi. I was going to use Toshiro Mifune; we even made a preliminary inquiry. If I’d gotten Mifune, I would’ve also used a Japanese princess, and then I would probably have cast a black Han Solo. At the same time, I was investigating Alec Guinness.”

Crittenden puts a script, with Lucas’s attached sketches, into Guinness’s hands in September, via a friend on *Murder by Death* (1976), no agent. Guinness calls about two hours later: “I love this; I’m very interested in it.” Lunch set directly. **Guinness**, typography locked: script arrived on his dressing-room table on his second-to-last day; he knew Lucas from *American Graffiti*, “which I admired very much… but when I opened the script and saw it was science fiction, I said, ‘Oh lord.’ I’ve never done a science fiction [film]; I’ve seen one or two of them and enjoyed them, but I always thought they were sort of cardboard, from an actor’s point of view. But because of Lucas, I started reading it—and I found myself involved… It had a touch of Tolkien’s *Lord of the Rings.* It was a rather simple outline of a good man who had some magical powers. It was an adventure story about the passing of knowledge and the sword from one generation to the next.” Lunch goes well; Guinness “impressed” by the “new breed of filmmaker.” “But they were far from a contract.”

## A BAG OF TRICKS

During the same three weeks, the ILM model shop ramps up. Cantwell by this time has built a Star Destroyer, X-wing, Y-wing, Skyhopper (“originally a good-guy fighter”), landspeeder, small Death Star sphere, TIE ship, and sandcrawler. **McCune** on the sandcrawler: “a truncated pyramid with tiny ‘gravity grippers’… It had two little tanks that pulled it around and it looked more like a barge for picking kelp.” Concept models “heavied up” so they would work with mattes, large enough for motors and lights. “The X-wing just kept getting fatter and fatter… But the basic ideas for the pirate ship, Star Destroyer, X-wings, and Y-wings were all nailed down tight within the first thirty days. The TIE ship came right after that.” **Cantwell** on a silver Death Star prototype: “I asked George whether the Death Star was always going to be near a planet. He said it was, so I explored a very risky thing… I tried making it silver, so you can set scenes where it would show in space.” Models needed first because England cockpits and interiors would be built from them, and because they planned **front projection** on set for the Alderaan escape / TIE battle, plates needed by early 1976 (Dykstra had used front projection on *Silent Running*; Don Trumbull built a 4×5-plate rotating rig). David Beasley hired from the “Unemployment Department.” **Joe Johnston** hired August 1975 to start the art department (redesign concept models; storyboards for front-projection sequences from the third draft); he had been at Magic Camera on an aborted Paramount *The War of the Worlds*. **Johnston**: “I was going to the beach every day… and he ruined it! … It looked like a fun job.” Steve Gawley hired to turn Johnston drawings into orthographic schematics, then moves into the model shop. **Lucas**: Dykstra needed wings fatter for bluescreen, bodies fatter for suspension bars; “Joe was the one who added all the aesthetics in terms of detailing.” **Johnston**: “George wanted all the rebel ships to look secondhand, old and beat up… not as well built or well designed as the Imperial ships. If he didn’t like a ship, he’d say, ‘Do more sketches.’ ” **Shepherd**: Joe “could really make images that you understood”; then the model shop “embellished it from their own head.”

## THE DEMISE OF THE LOCKED-OFF SHOT

**Andy Rigrod** on patents: “a huge staff, and every one of them had to be employed under agreement so they would promise to not give away the secrets that were involved in the processes.” Rinzler explains the pre-motion-control status quo: starfield, ships, lasers, planet shot separately, combined on an optical printer; “hot” sets left standing; nearly all shots static because the camera “could never successfully and repeatedly film all the needed elements if it did.” ~350 shots, each with as many as seven or eight elements. **Dykstra**: need systems to shoot, tear down, set up again, “with the certainty that it would be okay,” and a camera that could repeat if the first day’s take failed. Status quo SF — “static spaceships flying on rails” — wouldn’t match Lucas’s aerial short. “You could make a zoom or a pan or a tilt… but to zoom, pan, and tilt in the same shot and maintain matte integrity was really difficult. So the moves were primarily linear.” The “locked-off shot” was “a telltale sign that they were about to see something fake.” “Lucas was adamant that he wanted his effects to occupy the same reality as his protagonists.” **Dykstra**: a moving camera is “the subliminal cue… that he’s watching real live-action photography… Instead of viewing a postcard, he’s seeing a three-dimensional reality.”

## Pirate Ship

McQuarrie photographed Cantwell’s models and updated paintings. Cantwell finished concept models in May after about two weeks of talks that then stretched “several months later” — **Lucas**: “By the time he was done, ILM had been set up.” Johnston sketches from Cantwell’s rebel/pirate ship, incorporating an alternate prototype’s extra rocket boosters; McQuarrie painting; Dykstra additional sketches; Gawley orthographics; Shourts and McCune build. **McCune**: “It’s really an amazing acrylic sculpture… like a six-inch tube with four-inch tubes stuck out at the ends, and wings of eight-inch Plexiglas. Jamie Shourt… figured out every piece of it on paper with his little mini computer… It weighed about 125 pounds.”

## FANTASTIC MOVING MACHINES OF THE FUTURE

The motion-control camera combines a specialized camera, mechanical rig, and electronic-control device — the **“Dykstraflex.”** **Dykstra**, typography locked: “Doug Trumbull deserves a lot of credit, because a lot of the people who worked on *Star Wars* were people that he nurtured. The name *Dykstraflex* was really a joke. The Dykstra-flex is not my camera; it’s not a *new* concept. It’s been around for years, from the days of the RKO General Pictures [in the 1930s]… There was a Trumbullflex, and the same people that made the Dykstraflex had made the Trumbullflex.” Built by Dykstra, Don Trumbull, Richard Alexander, and Al Miller; outline over “two bottles of wine” at Dykstra’s Marina Del Rey house; then Bill Shourt, Edlund, Alexander, Don Trumbull all had input. **Edlund**: designed around Lucas’s 16mm WWII-edit; camera as small as possible for close passes without soft focus — “One fuzzy shot in the whole movie and you’ve lost your credibility”; narrower head so models could just miss the camera. Hardware about $60,000; major construction about six months (June–December 1975); modular, pipe-clamp tracks; “It’s a workhorse device; it’s not a computer… It’s a calculator.”

## Sandcrawler

Circa **5 April 1975**, McQuarrie revises Cantwell’s sandcrawler and illustrates the robots’ escape as in the second draft (stalled by falling rocks; Jawas trying to put the track back on). Originally inspired by NASA rover photographs. “The Jawas themselves are outgrowths of the ‘shell-dwellers’ in Lucas’s *THX 1138.*” **McQuarrie**: “very large, old, rusty, and tracked… I envisioned this thing on the front with teeth to be part of a scoop… like a garbage truck.” **Johnston**: McQuarrie’s was “real long and streamlined. I thought that it could be higher and more awkward and rustier, kind of clumsy looking, so that influenced my redesign.”

## X-Wing

**McCune**: “a box inside… gears made of rubber bands, pipes, and motors, and it was continually evolving.” **Lucas**: “I wanted a dragster, with a long narrow front and a guy sitting on the back. Then Colin came up with the split-wing thing.” **Cantwell**: “Out in space it had to be able to draw its guns like in a Western. So that was the equivalent.” **Johnston**: Cantwell’s “a little bit too sleek and slender”; used a Revell 1/16-scale dragster body; six or seven body changes; “We had a lot of trouble with the nose… we worked with that a long time, getting it into a shape that didn’t look obscene.”

## VISTAVISION STRIKES BACK

VistaVision (Paramount/Technicolor, 1950s): horizontal 8-perforation frames, twice the area of standard 35mm (live action would still be 35mm). Surplus movements “perhaps cost $25,000 or $30,000 to build, we could get for a thousand bucks” (Edlund). “Double the quality up front”; better for heavy duping; reduce from a large negative toward original quality; “every single film stock known to Kodak.” **Robbie Blalack** brings his own 35mm printer; second printer from Howard Anderson, unused since 1963, originally built for *The Ten Commandments* (1956); rebuilt “from the ground up” over “a good ten months, possibly even a year,” with Paul Roth; no experts. Also a matte camera (parts later into a contact printer by Rich Peccarella) and a densitometer; many cameras old Bell & Howells from 1925. Custom six-piece VistaVision “motion-viewing system” took six to eight months (Dykstra).

Union fight: **Tom Pollock** — “ILM was easy to set up… The tough part was the union problems.” **Dykstra**: unions upset at outside hires; he interviewed roster people and “didn’t find anybody that I wanted”; “negotiations were broken off by the union, not by us.” **Pollock**: “Fox was real scared… They wanted us to use their sixty-five-year-old matte painters and miniature people… But George was absolutely adamant that we wanted to set up our own shop with our own people.” **Matthew Robbins**: “George learned a lot from Francis Ford Coppola… Francis would go out and hurl himself against the realities of filmmaking and film distribution, while George very quietly absorbed these lessons and built his own version of an alternate Hollywood… He felt that the Hollywood enterprise was corrupt. That it wasn’t really about creativity, it was about making money.” **Charles Lippincott**: “This is a business where hustlers come to town to make the quick buck… It’s the valley of the quick kill.” **Willard Huyck**: “George was shrewd. He understood Hollywood immediately… ‘Yeah, I don’t like the way it works, I’m going to move to San Francisco and do my own thing from there.’ ” Negotiations break off; both sides wait to see how things develop.

## Star Destroyer / Y-Wing / TIE Fighter

**Johnston** on Cantwell’s Star Destroyer: “antennas and guns sticking out the sides, which wouldn’t have matted well.” ILM lowered the profile, built it about twice as high: “We made it look like a fortress, while his looked more like a naval ship or an aircraft carrier.” **Cantwell**: shape partly from Teddy Roosevelt’s circa-1907 white dreadnoughts “and the shape of a paper airplane.” Caption dates the model-build photos to early-spring 1976 because images of the full-sized **Millennium Falcon** are pinned to the wall (taken after Elstree construction) — first use of that ship name in this book’s running text/captions; the body still calls it the pirate ship in this chapter.

Y-wing: Johnston revised sketch, sculpted in the shop (Dave Beasley), painted by Johnston. **Lucas**: “I described it and we worked on that together, but I would say that’s about 70 or 80 percent Ralph’s design.” **Johnston**: “an old, stock space fighter that was just barely flying… They’d taken everything off of it.”

TIE: **Johnston**, typography locked: “In World War II the super dive-bombers had an artificially created siren wail created by air ducts… It was intended that the TIE should achieve the same effect with just a menacing appearance.” “The acronym *TIE* was created by Lucas but not defined. ‘I came up with “twin ion engine fighter,” ’ Johnston adds. ‘There were other ideas, like “Third Intergalactic Empire,” but I thought “twin-I engine” made more sense.’ ” Later the wail is added by Ben Burtt. **Lucas**: “they were influenced by some paintings by John Berkey.”

## PRODUCTION NEEDS POWER!

September 1975: Lucas and Kurtz back in London for one week. **Robert Watts** officially starts, as do set decorator **Roger Christian** and art director **Les Dilley**. Watts hires the English crew from short lists (with Peter Beale); Lucas makes final choices. Kodak helps with VistaVision perforation/steadiness; special ESTAR Base stocks. **Watts**: “I’d rather have a nasty person who’s good at his job than a nice person who’s lousy.” Studios: Barry had told Kurtz in August that one studio would not be enough — “if you are lucky enough to get up a big set, you shoot it in two days—and it takes another month to get up another set on that stage—which means you can only use a big stage twice in your schedule.” By fall, **EMI (Elstree)** plus **Shepperton**. EMI, a few miles north of London, main studio: no scheduling conflicts; Stages 1–9 except Stage 5 (leased to Paul McCartney); best facilities; “four walls” rental, freelance crew. Two largest sets on Shepperton’s H Stage.

Supporting roles from local actors. **Anthony Daniels**, typography locked: agent said “this man called George Lucas making a ‘space film’… the only trouble is, the part is a robot. So I said, ‘Oh come on, I’m a serious actor.’ ” At Fox he likes Lucas immediately; silence until Daniels asks about the production paintings on the wall: “he opened up and became quite dynamic.” Reads the script three times, writes out the story in sections; “I became very excited about the character See-Threepio. I realized he was not an ordinary robot and began to forget all of my original ideas about playing a robot.”

Stears’s special-effects budget long complete, but Fox money is “not forthcoming.” **Lucas**: they needed to start construction on the robots in September; “Fox wouldn’t commit.” Elliot Scott’s set-cost budget also done (he then left for a prior commitment); no green light, no money. **Kurtz**: meetings with Fox; “one of the things that made the studio nervous: the special effects area. Between the first and second and third drafts, the story changed considerably. We redid the budget over and over again. One of the budgets I did was $13.5 million direct, without overhead.” Lucas and Kurtz return for more budget talks; Watts and Barry depart to location-scout in North Africa. ILM bill **$241,026 by 8 August**, about $25,000 a week. **Kurtz**: “they were spending money right and left, and we hadn’t seen anything yet. But we weren’t supposed to see anything until Christmastime at the earliest.” **Shepherd**: “Fox didn’t know whether we were on schedule or ahead of schedule or behind schedule—or even if there was a schedule. It was a new type of picture.”
