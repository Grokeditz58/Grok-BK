## S228 — chapter opener

Circle emblem, **CHAPTER 5**, **THE GALACTIC CIVIL WAR**. Drop cap **T**. The public first experienced *Star Wars* in print: the 1976 novelization arrived in bookstores six months before the movie. *Splinter of the Mind’s Eye* (1978) was also a bestseller, but the original-trilogy era was only sparsely explored in novels; prose largely ceded the ground to comics. Marvel’s six-issue film adaptation kicked off a 107-issue run during and after the trilogy. Newspaper strips followed from the Los Angeles Times Syndicate in 1979 and 1981, both set between Episodes IV and V. Dark Horse later added *Empire* and *Rebellion* comics in this window.

Editorial consensus: Luke, Han, and Leia were extremely busy in the four years between Death Stars, and new stories featuring the trio are challenging given the larger Expanded Universe continuity. The most common form in this era is therefore the short story, especially the West End Games *Adventure Journal* (1994–1997), 280-plus pages, mostly new characters in the Galactic Civil War and early New Republic. - Chronological Note **“A Death Star Is Born”** (S254, major: Lucas’s canon history of the station). The Death Star has no single architect; its fictional history has been extensively revised. *A New Hope* credited Tarkin; the EU built on that (*Imperial Sourcebook*, 1989; *Death Star Technical Companion*, 1991). *Galaxy Guide 5: Return of the Jedi* (1990) was first to credit engineer Bevel Lemelisk. Later additions: the Maw Installation (*Jedi Academy* trilogy); Umak Leth (*Dark Empire*); Nasdra Magrody (*Children of the Jedi*). That mid-1990s roster “does not stretch credibility too much—but this mid-1990s snapshot barely scratched the surface.” *Rogue Planet* (2001) suggested Tarkin may have cribbed the idea from Raith Sienar. “Star Wars: Episode II Attack of the Clones (2002) had the biggest shocker: the Geonosians were chiefly responsible for the Death Star’s construction. In George Lucas’s canon history of the Death Star, it was the insectoid weaponsmiths who built the first battle station within the rings of their world, a fact verified by a visit to Geonosis in the Disney theme parks ride *Star Tours: The Adventures Continue* (2011).” Plans-theft is even more convoluted: Keyan Farlander in the *X-Wing* space combat simulator (1993) was first credited with recovery; then Kyle Katarn in *Dark Forces* (1995); also *Battlefront II* (2005), *Empire at War* (2006), *Lethal Alliance* (2006), and the *Lost Jedi* game books (1993). *Empire at War* attempted to unify the accounts by suggesting the stolen plans were deliberately scattered. (Hierarchy file; tight-crop S254 before any sealed verbatim.)
- SPOTLIGHT **The Farlander Papers** — boxed in a limited-edition 1992 *X-Wing* space combat simulator; a 96-page booklet by **Russel DeMaria** (S254 spelling) / **Rusel DeMaria** (S255 spelling) — lock both; do not regularize. Part sourcebook, part novella from Keyan’s point of view (Agamar orphan, Mon Mothma speech, *Independence*, Hamo Blastwell, steals Imperial data). Keyan is the player’s proxy. The 1993 Prima strategy guide continues as a novella woven through the guide content. The game culminates at Yavin; historically Luke destroys the station; the strategy guide deviates from the game mission to place Farlander in a Y-wing, so he is the anonymous Y-wing pilot flying away from the doomed Death Star at the end of *A New Hope*. Add-ons *Imperial Pursuit* and *B-Wing*; a 1995 expanded guide (DeMaria, David Wessman, David Maxwell) has no new novella. Farlander later appears in *The New Jedi Order: Dark Tide I: Onslaught* (2000) and *Destiny’s Way* (2002) as a New Republic fleet officer. A 2008 Hasbro action figure based on an otherwise unnamed *Return of the Jedi* B-wing pilot is described as “thus retroactively making Farlander present in the film.”
