# Chapter 3 — THE CLONE WARS (S101–S173)

Visual source: CBR FD0WCBbA, p101–p173. No printed folios. Chapter head on S101 uses arabic **3**. Chapter 4 (**THE DARK TIMES**) opens on S174. Caption-only or painting-split leaves: S109, S112, S124, S138, S144, S151, S159, S161, S170 (and captions riding the tops of S110, S113, S118, S120, S125, S131, S135, S139, S142, S145, S148, S152, S155, S162, S164, S168, S171, S173). “Click to buy …” links are ebook furniture.

Organizational quirk, locked as printed: after *Labyrinth of Evil* (the 19 BBY lead-in to Episode III) the chapter does not give the Episode III novelizations or the opening volumes of *Last of the Jedi* and *Coruscant Nights*. It instead runs *Last of the Jedi* #4–**#IO**, *Coruscant Nights* II–III, and the short story “Two-Edged Sword,” all dated c. 18 BBY, then stops. Those mid-series Dark Times entries therefore sit at the end of Chapter 3; the film novelization and the series openers are expected in Chapter 4.

A running Companion rule in this chapter, introduced on the opener and applied to many 2002–2005 books: because *The Clone Wars* animated series (a story line direct from George Lucas) knights Anakin Skywalker near the start of the war, tales that still present him as a Padawan are moved into approximately the first four weeks after Geonosis, and the old month-by-month publishing dates are discarded. The stories themselves are not disavowed. See the hierarchy file.

## S101 — chapter opener

Circle emblem, **CHAPTER 3**, **THE CLONE WARS**. Drop cap **W**. The Clone Wars had been “a long-roped-off area of continuity, first mentioned in the prologue of the novelization of the original *Star Wars* (1976).” Starting in 2003, LucasBooks filled the era with an interconnected program of books, comics, articles, and games that “proved to be a double-edged sword.” Early authors exercised creative license; unbeknownst to them, a television plan was already percolating. Typography: **nota-ble** (hyphen as printed). “In some nota-ble cases, these early stories did not align with the version of events that would later be established by George Lucas and his team at Lucasfilm Animation. Some differences have proven more irreconcilable than others, and are noted where appropriate. It is not the intent of this Companion to discard any Clone Wars tales, though it is evident that some published events must have at least transpired differently, or in a different order, than first presented.”

The biggest surprise for many was the introduction of Ahsoka Tano. Books published before her arrival run all the way through *Revenge of the Sith*; most Clone Wars novels avoided Anakin and Obi-Wan as central characters, which blunted the conspicuous nature of Ahsoka’s absence.

From 2002 to 2005, a detailed month-by-month time line charted an estimated three-year war. The animated series does not adhere to that calendar, so published dates should be taken with a grain of salt. *Jedi Trial* (2004) pegs Anakin’s knighthood at about thirty months into the war; the series advances him at the very beginning. That leaves either an incorrect date, or a compression of all the television stories into the last six months of the war. “As always, a story line direct from George Lucas trumps publishing continuity, and it’s the Jedi Trial date that gets discarded. However, it’s important to note that it’s not the novel’s story that is disavowed as a result.”

A further paragraph on hindsight: the television series was produced after Episode III and had information unavailable to earlier publishers. None of the original Clone Wars literature knew General Grievous would be a key player until 2004, by which time the early months of the war had already been extensively detailed, so he appears late in the Expanded Universe time line. The animated series, “having full knowledge of Grievous’s prominence, depicts him as active from the very start.” (Hierarchy file; typ101 still needs a clean recrop covering the Jedi Trial rule, the “not the intent… to discard” sentence, and this Grievous graf.)

## Episode II novelizations, “Precipice,” and the early *Boba Fett* juveniles (S107–S116)

- **STAR WARS: EPISODE II ATTACK OF THE CLONES** — R. A. Salvatore (adult); Patricia C. Wrede (juvenile); “based on the story and the screenplay by George Lucas and Jonathan Hales.” Covers: Steven D. Anderson (hardcover); David Stevenson (paperback); Louise Bova and Lucasfilm (juvenile). Adult: HC April 2002, PB April 2003, *Prequel Trilogy* omnibus May 2007; Scholastic juvenile April 2002. 22 BBY. Film-plot summary; Dooku is revealed as Darth Tyranus, engineering the war at the behest of Darth Sidious.
  **BTS (S108–S109, continuity / Lucas):** “GEORGE LUCAS REVEALED new information about his universe in Episode II that ran counter to earlier stories of the Expanded Universe.” (1) The Jedi Order is monastic; love and marriage are forbidden. That necessitates reforms to the Jedi Code over time, separating the ancient era in which Nomi Sunrider was married (*Tales of the Jedi*, 1993–94) from the post-Empire era in which Luke Skywalker married Mara Jade (*Union*, 1999–2000). LucasBooks also needed plausible exceptions for Ki-Adi-Mundi, who had multiple wives in *Prelude to Rebellion* (1999). (2) The Republic had been without a military or sizable conflict for over a thousand years. Incidents such as the Stark Hyperspace War (comic, 2001) and organizations such as the Katana Fleet (*Thrawn Trilogy*, 1991–93) are exceptions rather than the rule. (3) “Perhaps the biggest surprise to longtime readers was the true history of Boba Fett,” a perfect clone of Jango Fett. Prior Expanded Universe backstories: Marvel *Star Wars* #68, “The Search Begins” (1983)—one of three survivors of 212 Mandalorian Supercommandos from the Clone Wars; *Dark Empire II* #2 (1995)—a former Imperial stormtrooper who murdered his superior officer; *The Essential Guide to Characters* (1995)—a former Journeyman Protector from Concord Dawn named Jaster Mereel, exiled for murdering a fellow lawman. “Fett’s true roots have revealed these narratives to be historically inaccurate, as the legends of notorious characters often are.” S109 is a painting of Jango and young Boba facing Obi-Wan in the Kamino rain; caption at top of S110: **Young Boba Fett attempts to help his father, Jango Fett, as the bounty hunter tangles with Jedi Knight Obi-Wan Kenobi on Kamino.**

- **SECRETS OF THE JEDI** — Jude Watson; David Mattingly; Scholastic; HC March 2005; typography **Collected withLegacy of the Jedinto single omnibus**, PB May 2006. 39 BBY (part 1); **c. 22 BBY** (part 2). This tale therefore must occur within the first four weeks of the conflict.” The change also fits Halcyon’s relationship with Slayke: the prewar pursuit is a fresh memory rather than an event nearly three years past. Much of the setting is pieced together from earlier Expanded Universe material: Praesitlyn and its communications center were first mentioned in *Heir to the Empire* (1991); Halcyon first appeared as part of Corran Horn’s backstory in *X-Wing: Rogue Squadron* (1996); Freedom’s Sons is a very old reference to a glancing mention in *Han Solo at Stars’ End* (1979). S124 painting of Anakin with troopers, Erk, and Odie; caption at top of S125: **In a Republic command center on Praesitlyn, Anakin Skywalker commands Erk H’arman and Odie Subu, for whom he will perform marriage services in a rare moment of calm in the Clone Wars.**

## *The Clone Wars* movie novels, Secret Missions, *Wild Space* (S125–S131)

- **THE CLONE WARS** — Karen Traviss (adult novel); Tracey West (juvenile); “based on the movie *Star Wars: The Clone Wars*.” John VanFleet (adult) / Lucasfilm (juvenile). Adult Del Rey hardcover July 2008; juvenile Grosset & Dunlap paperback July 2008. 22 BBY. Worlds include Christophsis, Coruscant, **Kem Stor Ai \* [Q-7]** (asterisk as printed; the delayed footnote **\*Adult novel only.** appears much later on the almost-blank S159 leaf), Tatooine, Teth. After Christophsis, newly Knighted Anakin is assigned Ahsoka Tano; they rescue Jabba’s infant son Rotta from a Dooku/Ventress kidnapping plot on Teth and return him on Tatooine, while Padmé uncovers Ziro’s conspiracy.
  **BTS (S125–S126, continuity):** the animated movie was a big-screen introduction to the television series; there were also young-reader storybooks and activity books. The novels are listed as “based on the movie,” yet unlike other movie novelizations they do not list screenwriting credits. The movie was actually built out of four episodes—“The New Padawan,” “Castle of Deception,” “Castle of Salvation,” and “Castle of Doom”—which have never aired in their original formats. Writing credits go to Henry Gilroy, Scott Murphy, and Steven Melching. “As a note of caution for chronological readers, Karen Traviss’s novel depicts Darth Sidious’s double identity in the open, under the assumption that this secret was exposed in *Star Wars: Episode III Revenge of the Sith* (2005).” The adult novelization includes many more Expanded Universe connections than the junior novelization or the movie itself, including repeated mention of Ventress’s original EU backstory from the *Republic* comics and an incident in Dooku’s past on Galidraan from *Jango Fett: Open Seasons* (2002). A brief mention of the conflict on Jabiim points to *Republic* #55–58 (2003). The Battle of Jabiim was originally listed as occurring fifteen months after the start of the war and chronicled a pivotal experience of Anakin as a Padawan. The animated series begins with Anakin already Knighted, compressing his apprentice adventures into the first four weeks; Jabiim “may have still occurred in Expanded Universe continuity, but it must have occurred months earlier than originally presented in the time line.” The young-reader book suggests that the usual age to become a Padawan is sixteen, and that Ahsoka, at fourteen, is remarkably young for apprenticeship. “This runs counter to the *Jedi Apprentice* series, which states that a youngling needs to become an apprentice before age thirteen.”
- SPOTLIGHT **The Clone Wars Adaptations.** Grosset & Dunlap (Penguin) young-reader novelizations of television episodes, later gathered in anthologies, occupying a span of the overall time line as the series does: *Grievous Attacks!* (2009), Veronica Wasserman, Tracey West, and Rob Valois (“Rookies,” “Downfall of a Droid,” “Lair of Grievous”); *Bounty Hunter: Boba Fett* (2010), Jason Fry (“Death Trap,” “R2 Come Home,” “Lethal Trackdown”); *Defenders of the Republic* (2010), Rob Valois (“Clone Cadets,” “Rookies,” “ARC Troopers”); *Warriors of the Deep* (2011), Rob Valois (“Water War,” “Gungan Attack,” “Prisoners”); *Darth Maul: Shadow Conspiracy* (2013), Jason Fry (“Revival,” “Eminence,” “Shades of Reason,” “The Lawless”), from Scholastic Inc.
- **“Star Wars: The Clone Wars: Out Foxed”** — Rob Valois; Grosset & Dunlap; published online at Target.com, July 2008; 22 BBY; Coruscant. Clone Commander Fox stops a Trandoshan bounty hunter, T’doshok, before he kidnaps a Senator.
- **THE CLONE WARS: SECRET MISSIONS** — typography on S127 runs the four titles together with odd commas/spaces: **SECRET MISSIONS #1: BREAKOUT SQUAD  ,SECRET MISSIONS #2: CURSE OF THE BLACK HOLE PIRATES  ,SECRET MISSIONS #3: DUEL AT SHATTERED ROCK  ,SECRET MISSIONS #4: GUARDIANS OF THE CHISS KEY**. - **THE CLONE WARS: WILD SPACE** — Karen Miller; Lucasfilm; Del Rey trade paperback, December 2008; 22 BBY. Atzerri, Coruscant, Geonosis (flashback), Munto Codru, Zigoola. Meanwhile Anakin and Ahsoka are assigned the Bothawui mission. **BTS (S130, continuity):** the novel begins right after Episode II, with noteworthy character-history beats (Anakin discovering his arm has been amputated; Obi-Wan suspecting and confronting Padmé about her relationship with Anakin).
