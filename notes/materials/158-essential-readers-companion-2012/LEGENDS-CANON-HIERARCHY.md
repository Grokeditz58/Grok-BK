# Legends canon hierarchy — notes158, The Essential Reader’s Companion (2012)

This file is the owner-requested dedicated record of the Legends-era canon hierarchy, with George Lucas as supreme authority. It has two clearly separated parts:

1. **From this book**, quoted only from opened CBR crops (or, where a crop is still a working file, from a visual Read of that same leaf, to be replaced by a certified crop at seal). The book never uses the labels G-canon, T-canon, C-canon, S-canon, or N-canon, and it never uses the later trademark **Legends**. Those names are not smuggled into the book’s own wording.
2. **Outside this book**, labeled as such, with sources: the Lucasfilm Holocron’s G/T/C/S/N levels and the usual rule for resolving conflicts. This is the system the owner asked to have written down, and it is the system that was in force when this 2012 Companion was published. The 2014 “Legends” / new-canon reboot is also outside the book and is noted only for context.

## 1. What this book itself says

### 1.1 “Canon and Continuity” (S008 / Introduction)

Complete section, tight-read from `/tmp/erc-ov/q_canon_continuity_s008.jpg` (full heading through the closer and the brown double rule; left and right edges of every line are visible on that crop):

> **CANON AND CONTINUITY**
>
> Common questions are: How “real” are these stories? Do they count? Did they really happen?
>
> The most definitive canon of the Star Wars universe is encompassed by the feature films and television productions in which George Lucas is directly involved. The movies and the Clone Wars television series are what he and his handpicked writers reference when adding cinematic adventures to the Star Wars oeuvre.
>
> But Lucas allows for an Expanded Universe that exists parallel to the one he directly oversees. In many cases, the stewards of the Expanded Universe—editors within the licensing division of Lucasfilm Ltd. who work with authors and publishers—will ask for his input or blessing on projects. Though these stories may get his stamp of approval, they don’t enter his canon unless they are depicted cinematically in one of his projects.
>
> That said, unless something occurs in a canon project to directly contradict a published source, it can reliably be said to have occurred. Extensive records track the growth of the Expanded Universe, cataloging planets, characters, technology, and events, to allow for a sprawling, believable continuity connecting the published works of the Star Wars universe. It’s not perfect—when errors occur, the Companion does sometimes call them out. This is not to diminish these tales in any way, but rather to illustrate that the Star Wars Expanded Universe is a living document that grows and evolves over time.
>
> The Reader’s Companion is not meant as a replacement for the experience of reading these works firsthand. Those truly interested in the stories are strongly encouraged to read them whole. No matter how detailed, a summary is no substitute for experiencing a story as the author intended.

What the book is doing in this passage, in its own terms:

- **Supreme / “most definitive” canon** is the feature films and the television productions **in which George Lucas is directly involved**. The movies and the Clone Wars television series are what Lucas and “his handpicked writers” use when adding cinematic adventures.
- The **Expanded Universe is parallel** to the continuity Lucas directly oversees. Licensing-division editors may seek his input or blessing, but a blessed EU story still **does not “enter his canon”** unless it is **depicted cinematically in one of his projects**.
- **Conflict rule, as the book states it:** unless something in a “canon project” directly contradicts a published source, that published source “can reliably be said to have occurred.”
- Continuity is a “living document”; the Companion will sometimes call out errors, not to diminish the tales.

The book does **not** here name G-canon / T-canon / C-canon / S-canon / N-canon, and it does not use the word Legends.

### 1.2 “A story line direct from George Lucas trumps publishing continuity” (S101 / Chapter 3 opener)

Chapter 3, **THE CLONE WARS**, opens by calling the era “a long-roped-off area of continuity.” The 2003 LucasBooks program of interconnected books, comics, articles, and games proved “a double-edged sword”: early authors exercised creative license, unbeknownst to them that a television plan was already percolating. From the S101 overview (working visual read; typ101 still has a sliver of a prior sentence and does not yet include the Grievous graf—recrop before certification), including the typography **nota-ble**:

The early stories “did not align with the version of events that would later be established by George Lucas and his team at Lucasfilm Animation. Some differences have proven more irreconcilable than others, and are noted where appropriate. It is not the intent of this Companion to discard any Clone Wars tales, though it is evident that some published events must have at least transpired differently, or in a different order, than first presented.”

Ahsoka Tano was the biggest surprise; books published before her introduction run through *Revenge of the Sith*, and most Clone Wars novels avoided Anakin and Obi-Wan as central characters, which blunted the conspicuous nature of her absence.

Tight-read from `/tmp/erc-ov/typ101_continuity.jpg` (crop currently includes a sliver of the preceding Ahsoka sentence at the top; a tighter certified crop should start at the paragraph “The Clone Wars stories from 2002 through 2005…”):

> The Clone Wars stories from 2002 through 2005 provided a detailed time line that comprised a month-by-month account of the estimated three-year war. The animated series does not adhere to this time line, thus requiring that all published dates be taken with a grain of salt. Anakin Skywalker’s ascension from Padawan to Jedi Knight, for example, is pegged by the novel Jedi Trial (2004) as happening around thirty months after the start of the war. Yet the animated series advances Anakin to this rank at the very beginning of the conflict. This leaves few options: either the date provided in Jedi Trial is incorrect, or all television stories are compressed to the last six months of the war. As always, a story line direct from George Lucas trumps publishing continuity, and it’s the Jedi Trial date that gets discarded. However, it’s important to note that it’s not the novel’s story that is disavowed as a result.

A further paragraph on the same leaf (overview; not yet in typ101) explains the hindsight problem: the television series was produced after Episode III and had information unavailable to earlier publishers. None of the original Clone Wars literature knew General Grievous would be a key player until 2004, by which time the early months of the war had already been extensively detailed, so he appears late in the Expanded Universe time line. The animated series, “having full knowledge of Grievous’s prominence, depicts him as active from the very start.”

This is the book’s concrete conflict-resolution example: a story line **direct from George Lucas** (here, the Clone Wars animated series) **trumps publishing continuity**. The *date* in *Jedi Trial* is discarded; the novel’s *story* is not disavowed. The Companion then applies that same Padawan-to-Knight compression throughout Chapter 3 (see 1.9).

### 1.2a Episode II revelations that “ran counter to earlier stories of the Expanded Universe” (S108–S109)

From the behind-the-scenes text of the Episode II novelizations (S108–S109 overview; tight-crop before any certified verbatim). The book’s own lead: “GEORGE LUCAS REVEALED new information about his universe in Episode II that ran counter to earlier stories of the Expanded Universe.”

1. The Jedi Order is monastic; love and marriage are forbidden. That necessitates later reforms to the Jedi Code, so the ancient era in which Nomi Sunrider was married (*Tales of the Jedi*, 1993–94) and the post-Empire era in which Luke married Mara Jade (*Union*, 1999–2000) can stand apart from the prequel-era rule. LucasBooks also needed plausible exceptions for Ki-Adi-Mundi, who had multiple wives in *Prelude to Rebellion* (1999).
2. The Republic had been without a military or sizable conflict for over a thousand years. The Stark Hyperspace War (comic, 2001) and the Katana Fleet (*Thrawn Trilogy*, 1991–93) are therefore exceptions rather than the rule.
3. “Perhaps the biggest surprise to longtime readers was the true history of Boba Fett,” a perfect clone of Jango Fett. Prior Expanded Universe backstories—Marvel *Star Wars* #68 (1983), one of three survivors of 212 Mandalorian Supercommandos from the Clone Wars; *Dark Empire II* #2 (1995), a former Imperial stormtrooper who murdered his superior officer; *The Essential Guide to Characters* (1995), a former Journeyman Protector from Concord Dawn named Jaster Mereel, exiled for murdering a fellow lawman—are now, in the book’s words, historically inaccurate: “Fett’s true roots have revealed these narratives to be historically inaccurate, as the legends of notorious characters often are.”

This is the same supremacy rule as S008/S101, applied to a film reveal: Lucas’s Episode II overrides earlier licensed backstories, which the book then reframes as in-universe legends.

### 1.3 Tight guard on Jedi mythology (S009 / Chapter I opener)

Tight-read from `/tmp/erc-ov/typ009_jedi_guard.jpg` (working crop; recertify edges at seal if any leftover line remains). As printed, including irregular spaces:

> Before the late 1990s, when George Lucas and his crew began work on Star Wars: Episode  I  *The Phantom Menace,*  Jedi lore was a precious rarity in the Expanded Universe. Lucasfilm kept a tight  guard on Jedi mythology, to give the stories Lucas wished to explore in Episodes I,  II, and III as wide a berth as possible. Enterprising authors found a way to tell  Jedi stories by going far back in time, millennia before the fall of the Republic.  Thus, they afforded themselves considerable creative freedom since differences of  interpretation could be smoothed out by the passage of centuries.

What the book is doing: Lucasfilm restricted Jedi-mythology publishing until Lucas had room to tell Episodes I–III; earlier-era Jedi stories were a licensed workaround, not a claim to equal authority.

Tight-read from `/tmp/erc-ov/typ022_kotor_canon.jpg` and `/tmp/erc-ov/typ022_revan_defines.jpg` (working): the 2003 BioWare/LucasArts game can take “a wide variety of shapes depending on the player’s  actions.” “A true canonical order of specific events has not been established, but the  “light side” ending (as opposed to the ending achievable if the player concentrates  on dark side actions) is accepted to be the historically accurate account.” The novel *The Old Republic: Revan* “canonically defines” Revan’s appearance, history, and personality, and identifies the Jedi Exile as Meetra Surik.

### 1.6 Lucas’s vision of the Sith in *The Phantom Menace* (S038 / Darth Bane BTS)

Tight-read from `/tmp/erc-ov/typ038_zahn_veitch.jpg` and `/tmp/erc-ov/typ038_tpm_lucas_sith.jpg` (working; quote typography, including irregular spaces, from the TPM crop which is complete from “In 1999” through “only two.”):

The Bane behind-the-scenes history, in the book’s own sequence: *Star Wars* (1977) does not mention Darth Vader’s title on screen, but “the  term Dark Lord of the Sith is indeed in the script and in tie-in publications, like  the film’s novelization.” In developing *Heir to the Empire* (1991), Timothy Zahn “attempted to establish the Sith as a species of assassins completely subservient  to Darth Vader. Lucasfilm rejected this idea, and Zahn’s imagined Sith instead became  the Noghri.” “With insights from George Lucas,” Tom Veitch began revealing more about the Sith in *Tales of the Jedi* (1993)—a Dark Lord and an apprentice, but “no restriction on the number of Sith present at any time.” Later TotJ stories treated the Sith as a civilization and as red-skinned humanoids. *Dark Forces II: Jedi Knight* (1997) described Ruusan without explicitly calling the practitioners Sith.

> In 1999, The Phantom Menace finally shed light on George Lucas’s vision of the Sith,  which at first glance would not seem to align with what had appeared previously in  the Expanded Universe. This canonical history describes a strict limitation to two  Sith at one time—a Master and an apprentice—and establishes that the Sith have been  extinct for a millennium. Working with author Terry Brooks, Lucas revealed more specific  details in the Episode I novelization. Two thousand years in the past, a Jedi had  left the Order to found the Sith. A thousand years after that, the Sith Order disintegrated  due to infighting, and a sole survivor—Darth Bane—restructured the order to number  only two.

The book then says this canonical history was merged with the Ruusan tales in *The Essential Chronology* (2000) and *Secrets of the Sith* (2000), then dramatized in the *Jedi vs. Sith* comics (2001) and the Bane novels. This is the same rule as S008/S101 in a different dress: Lucas’s screen (and the Episode I novelization he worked on with Brooks) is the “canonical history”; earlier EU Sith concepts were revised to fit it.

### 1.7 Other in-book continuity asides (Chapters I–4; working visual reads, not yet certified quotes)

These are from visual Reads of the CBR leaves (and, where noted, working tight crops). They will be confirmed with tight crops before use as sealed verbatims. Paraphrase is used here unless a crop is already opened.

**Chapter I**

- **S018:** *Crosscurrent* (2010) also begins in 5000 BBY, with a cameo by the ship *Omen* just before *Precipice*, but because it spans eras the Companion places it later, at 40 ABY.
- **S024:** James Luceno was originally slated to write a *The Old Republic* tie-in developing Darth Malgus; those early concepts did not fit BioWare/LucasArts’s Malgus, and Luceno reworked some of them into Darth Maul’s history in *Darth Plagueis* (2012).
- **S027:** At the end of *Red Harvest*, Zo intends to visit the Jedi Temple on Coruscant, but eight years after *Deceived* the Temple should be in ruins.
- **S034 typography / S039:** *Jedi vs. Sith* (2001) and *Path of Destruction* (2006) differ in focus and in Githany’s death; the Companion calls the other differences “mostly superficial.”

**Chapter 2 (S040–S100)**

- **S049:** Obi-Wan’s memory of a brother named Owen “stems from the earlier, now-apocryphal idea introduced in the screenplay of *Return of the Jedi* (1983) that Owen Lars and Obi-Wan were brothers.” The book itself labels a screenplay idea **now-apocryphal** when later continuity (the prequel-era Lars family) displaced it.
- **S052:** *The Captive Temple* shows Ki-Adi-Mundi already on the Council; *Jedi Council: Acts of War* (2000) has him join only after Micah Giiett’s death in 33 BBY. *The Evil Experiment* (2001) “corrects this inconsistency” by saying he would occasionally sit in Micah’s absence. In-book example of a later licensed work used to patch an earlier one.
- **S061:** *The Death of Hope* “deftly avoids any scenes with [Qui-Gon’s] Master, who would not be identified as Dooku until Episode II (2002).”
- **S062:** *The Only Witness* mentions Chancellor Valorum, but “term limitations would prevent this from being the same chancellory seen in Episode I—Valorum would not become chancellor until 39 BBY, according to *Cloak of Deception* (2001).”
- **S070:** “The movies Episodes I and II include deliberate hints connecting Palpatine and Darth Sidious, but any explicit statement that they were the same person was carefully avoided by all Star Wars media until the 2005 release of Episode III.” *Shadow Hunter* is called one of the more overt connections, yet the conspiracy “continues unabated.”
- **S073:** The Episode I novelization was written by Terry Brooks from Lucas’s screenplay “benefiting from direct input from Lucas.” Differences from the finished film are attributed to being based on the screenplay, not the final edit. An unnamed 400-year teacher of Qui-Gon in the novel “certainly does not fit Dooku, who is revealed to be Qui-Gon’s mentor in Episode II.”
- **S076–S077:** The Companion “assumes that newcomers to Star Wars fiction have already been exposed to the story of the six films,” which is why *Darth Plagueis* is placed in the time line *before* Palpatine is exposed in Episode III; “Episode III was the first source to identify Darth Plagueis as Sidious’s Master.” LucasBooks nearly canceled the novel “due to sensitivities regarding shining too direct a spotlight on these shadowy Sith characters”; it survived with “direct input from Howard Roffman, president of Lucas Licensing.” Fan-created elements (Romeo Treblanc, Bal’demnic) were “officially sanctioned as Star Wars lore by the Star Wars Fan Club in 2008.”
- **S081:** *Rogue Planet* is “essentially a chapter” in the already-begun New Jedi Order; the Yuuzhan Vong are implied as the Far Outsiders. The Senate’s post-Naboo demilitarization of the Trade Federation “was evidently poorly enforced,” given the droid armies of Episode II. Thracia Cho Leem’s marriage and children are “in direct opposition to the rules of the Jedi Code established later in Episode II.”
- **S089:** *Jedi Quest: The Dangerous Games* “states that Anakin has not raced since he was eight years old, when in fact he was nine years old during the Boonta Eve Podrace”—an in-book error call-out.
- **S092:** The change in Jedi Council roster from Episode I to Episode II “allowed the intervening stories of the Expanded Universe to eliminate prominent Jedi Masters in a dramatic fashion” (Yarael Poof in *Zam Wesell*; Yaddle in *The Shadow Trap*), with Coleman Trebor and Shaak Ti filling the seats.
- **S095:** *The False Peace*, though set between Episodes I and II, “was written as Episode III was in development. Palpatine and Anakin’s relationship is portrayed in a manner that better reflects their camaraderie in *Revenge of the Sith* (2005).”
- **S097:** The Sith Lord in *The Final Showdown* is not named Darth Tyranus until *The Desperate Mission* (2005). The same book describes *The Dangerous Games* as six years earlier; “the time line fits in such events as being four years earlier.”
- **S099:** *The Approaching Storm* springs from a single Mace Windu line in Episode II about a border dispute on Ansion. “Curiously, Luminara Unduli is beside Windu at the time he says this, even though she, too, was on this mission.”

**Chapter 3 (S101–S173)** — working visual reads, not yet certified quotes. The S101 and S108–S109 statements are in 1.2 / 1.2a. Other asides:

- **S111:** *Boba Fett* #I is the first source to pair Aurra Sing and young Boba, something that would also occur in *The Clone Wars* Season 2 (2010).
- **S115:** *Maze of Deception* claims Boba is eleven; “most time lines would peg his age at ten.”
- **S116, S117, S119, S120, S122, S123 (Companion time-line moves):** because Anakin is still a Padawan in these tales, the Companion moves them into approximately the first four weeks of the war, discarding original dates of four months (“Storm Fleet Warnings”), six months (*Legacy of the Jedi*), twelve months (*The Cestus Deception*), two years (“Changing Seasons”), twenty-nine months (*Secrets of the Jedi*), and thirty months (*Jedi Trial*). This is 1.2 applied repeatedly. *The Cestus Deception* also has Obi-Wan note that a clone has never disobeyed orders, “contradicted in the *Clone Wars* episode ‘The Hidden Enemy.’”
- **S117:** *Legacy of the Jedi* “clarifies—at the behest of George Lucas—that [Dooku] learned under Yoda as all younglings did, and his official Padawan apprenticeship was actually to another Jedi Master.” Direct Lucas behest on a point Episode II had left ambiguous.
- **S122:** “When the Prequel Trilogy was in production, the editors at Lucasfilm restricted authors from including Padmé Amidala in the spin-off fiction, particularly in the period between Episodes II and III, as her pregnancy was not yet known to many beyond George Lucas.” Her appearance in *Secrets of the Jedi* is a rare exception; she is more routine after the 2008 animated series. A Lucas/Lucasfilm embargo enforced until the films revealed the fact.
- **S126:** Traviss’s *The Clone Wars* adult novel “depicts Darth Sidious’s double identity in the open, under the assumption that this secret was exposed in” Episode III—flagged as a caution for chronological readers. The Battle of Jabiim, originally fifteen months in and a Padawan-era experience for Anakin, “may have still occurred in Expanded Universe continuity, but it must have occurred months earlier.” The young-reader book’s claim that the usual Padawan age is sixteen, with Ahsoka remarkably young at fourteen, “runs counter to the *Jedi Apprentice* series, which states that a youngling needs to become an apprentice before age thirteen.”
- **S130:** *Wild Space*’s seven-week jump after the 2008 feature “necessitates the compression of time for all of Anakin’s Padawan Clone Wars stories from before the animated series.” Malevolence vs. Bothawui order, the *Resolute*’s command, and a Coruscant explosion “at odds with the Season Three episode ‘Heroes on Both Sides,’ which makes it clear that the capital world has not been attacked in a thousand years.”
- **S134:** The *Republic Commando* game and novels built a strong clone–Mandalorian cultural connection “that would be downplayed in the animated series with the establishment of Mandalorian pacifists,” and “the TV series also brings into question whether Jango Fett was ever truly a Mandalorian.” The Companion reconciles this via Season Two’s “Clone Cadets”: bounty hunters with Mandalorian roots trained the commandos specifically; those traditions “weren’t necessarily applicable across the entirety of the clone ranks.”
- **S135:** “Omega Squad: Targets” has Senator Meena Tills as male, “though the fourth season of *The Clone Wars* would canonically establish her as female.” The book itself uses “canonically establish” for the television series versus a short story.
- **S137:** *Shatterpoint* puts Depa Billaba into a coma “as a hedge against her possibly appearing in Episode III,” and thereby explains her absence from the Council in that film.
- **S140:** The Altisian Jedi’s multiple apprentices and relationships, first presented as ordinary in *Children of the Jedi* (1995) when prequel-era Jedi strictures “were not known to Expanded Universe authors,” are carefully reframed in *No Prisoners* as an exception to the Code. Same Code-reform pattern as S108.
- **S143:** *Clone Wars Gambit* is placed in 21 BBY rather than in the late-war Outer Rim Sieges window once described in the Episode III script (20–19 BBY), because Ahsoka has served Anakin only months and is still hesitant about a repair skill she has in Season Three; Obi-Wan is depicted as not on the Council, though “Senate Spy” shows him with a seat.
- **S147:** *True Colors* presents future clone funding as a mystery and finds no long-term clone health plan; both “run counter” to the animated series (public Senate debate and Kaminoan representation; Kaliida Shoals).
- **S154:** Sean Stewart made his participation contingent on writing a Yoda story, “a rare exception to Lucasfilm’s policy of keeping this character reserved.”
- **S155–S156:** *Boba Fett* #5, though always intended to follow *Jedi Trial*, still calls Anakin a Padawan. *Pursuit* is contradicted by later *The Clone Wars* encounters and by the first book in its own series (Boba had already been to Coruscant); the animated series also has him incarcerated on Coruscant.
- **S158:** Luceno’s *Labyrinth of Evil* was built from questions Stover took to a Skywalker Ranch interview with George Lucas for the Episode III novelization. The *Clone Wars* micro-series (Genndy Tartakovsky) built its climax around the already-finalized novel plot but departed in the interest of a more explosive animated story; Roron Corobb and Foul Moudama were then inserted into the novel text, and Nelvaan is fleetingly mentioned.
- **S163:** *Street of Shadows* “erroneously suggests, through Typo’s recollections, that Padmé’s death had occurred before Palpatine declared himself Emperor, though the movie *Revenge of the Sith* has Typo seated next to a very-much-alive Padmé at that exact moment.” Film trumps the novel’s recollection.
- **S168:** *Last of the Jedi* #9 describes less-than-one-year-old Leia as walking and talking—“remarkably advanced development for a child that age … at least, by our terrestrial standards.”

**Chapter 4 (S174–S227; body notes now written in `body/04-the-dark-times.md`)**

- **S174 opener:** this span of time “has long been roped off from active exploration. For decades, uncertainty regarding the details of Episode III kept storytellers from venturing too deeply into this era. Even now, other potential projects in development mean that Expanded Universe storytellers need to tread lightly on an area filled with possibility.” The early Han Solo and Lando Calrissian romps occupy the era because they were dated just prior to Episode IV, not because of proximity to the Republic.
- **S182:** the Episode III novelization follows the I/II precedent of added scenes, but its greatest additions are internal viewpoints; it switches tenses and time frames so the story is both a past event and something “currently” unfolding, and it skips cutaways, distilling the Order 66 montage to the action Obi-Wan faces on Utapau.
- **S184:** *Republic Commando: Order 66* has the Coruscant attack spanning several days, versus a single day in *Labyrinth of Evil*. Delta Squad’s Kashyyyk mission and the abandonment of Sev are taken from the last mission of the 2004 videogame; “To date, Sev’s fate has yet to be resolved.”
- **S189:** Zahn wrote “Mist Encounter” “well before any details of Episodes I, II, and III were shared with Expanded Universe authors,” and though it “aligns fairly well with modern continuity, there is a conspicuous absence of any mention of the recently completed Clone Wars.” The original short story mentioned “President Palpatine,” changed to “Chancellor Palpatine” in the 2007 *Outbound Flight* paperback reprint.
- **S191 (television overrides the 2008 novel):** *Coruscant Nights I: Jedi Twilight* includes Even Piell at the start of the story line, which “does not align with canon as presented in the subsequent *Clone Wars* animated series. A 2011 episode (‘Counterattack’) kills Piell off at the height of the war, while this 2008 book presents him as a survivor of Order 66. Though Jax Pavan being Piell’s Padawan is still accepted as part of continuity, exactly who begins the hunt for the Whiplash droid has yet to be redefined.” Same pattern as S101/S135: the Lucas-supervised series is “canon as presented”; the conflicting novel detail is set aside; a compatible remainder is kept.
- **S194 (Lucasfilm veto):** early in development, *Last of the Jedi* “was envisioned as the continuing adventures of Obi-Wan Kenobi, but Lucasfilm ultimately vetoed this concept, reasoning that Kenobi would never venture far from Luke Skywalker unless exceptional circumstances demanded it.”
- **S196 Chronological Note:** Imperial Inquisitors began as a role-playing-game concept (a passing “Grand Inquisitor Torbin” in *The Star Wars Sourcebook*, 1987; later guidebook detail) and “rarely appeared in fiction because, until relatively recently, so few tales were told in their most pertinent era.”
- **S200:** Tremayne’s flashbacks in “Dark Vendetta” (1996) “don’t mesh easily with the continuity established years later with the release of Episode III.” The tale has Palpatine recruiting him to aid Vader in culling corrupt Jedi “prior to the Emperor cementing his rule.”
- **S201:** Han “estimates that he is nineteen years old at the start of” *The Paradise Snare*, “which would make him twenty-nine at the start of *A New Hope*, though his age is heavily caveated with uncertainty—he’s not sure when he was born.” A pre-recorded Bail Organa transmission with a dark-haired girl on his lap is “the first time Han sets eyes on his future wife, Leia, who would be ten years old at the time.”
- **S206 (Lucasfilm request):** *The Hutt Gambit* “does notably skip past the meeting of Han Solo and Chewbacca, which occurs between *The Paradise Snare* and *The Hutt Gambit*. This stemmed from a request from Lucasfilm to not dramatize this important event in the lives of these two characters.”
- **S208–S209 (major: Lucas approval does not elevate EU above Lucas screen work):** *The Force Unleashed* “received personal approval from George Lucas, but it does not occupy a higher standard of canon than any other Expanded Universe lore. That is, it still exists outside the movies and television series produced by Lucas and would be subordinate to mythology revealed in those media. For example, Lucas’s alternate explanations for the roots of the Rebel Alliance appear in season five of *Star Wars: The Clone Wars*, which take precedence over Starkiller’s involvement.” This is the book’s clearest in-chapter restatement of the S008/S101 rule, applied even to a project Lucas personally approved: approval does not move EU above the movies or Lucas-produced television. Tight-crop before any sealed verbatim.
- **S210 Spotlight:** “Pax Empirica—The Wookiee Annihilation” (2001 strategy-guide exclusive, never reprinted) is “a rare example of a pre-Episode II publication explicitly stating that stormtroopers ranks include clones”—at least 40 percent from a single genetic template, called GeNodes, genetically programmed not to self-identify as clones. A mention of Grand Moff Tarkin places it some time before Episode IV.
- **S212:** the Lando novels’ isolated Centrality setting “allowed the author to world-build without fear of continuity conflicts with the larger Galactic Empire.” All later EU sabacc is built on these books.
- **S215–S216:** Daley’s 1979 *Stars’ End* has Han tell Rekkon, “I happen to like to shoot first, Rekkon. As opposed to shooting second”—called “a notable moment of prescience” given later fan sentiment about the 1997 Special Edition edit in which Han shoots second.
- **S223:** “Interlude at Darkknell” is “very difficult to place” once *The Force Unleashed* is in the chronology: the Rebel Alliance (and the Corellian treaty) already exists, an event TFU depicts, but Garm does not know much about the Death Star, whereas the game has him immediately captured and taken aboard the battle station; TFU is a few years before Yavin, while Hal describing Corran as eighteen pins “Darkknell” to the same year as the Battle of Yavin.
- **S226:** *Rebel Dawn* “incorporates deliberate gaps in the storytelling to allow for the Brian Daley novels to occur,” then leads directly into *A New Hope*, specifically the Mos Eisley cantina meeting.

**Chapter 5 (S228–S312; body notes now written in `body/05-the-galactic-civil-war.md`)**

- **S228 opener:** editorial consensus that Luke, Han, and Leia were extremely busy in the four years between Death Stars, so new stories featuring the trio are challenging given the larger Expanded Universe continuity. The most common form in this era is the short story (especially the West End Games *Adventure Journal*).
- **S236:** the first *Star Wars* book is credited to George Lucas but was ghostwritten by Alan Dean Foster from Lucas’s fourth-draft screenplay. For decades its 362-word prologue was “the only real insight into events potentially covered in Episodes I–III” and, for years, the only source to identify the Emperor by name. Earlier printings used the later-corrected spellings Wookie, lightsabre, and 'droids.
- **S241 (major: “canon established by George Lucas” disproves a short story’s assumptions; television then blocks a guidebook fix):** “A Hunter’s Fate: Greedo’s Tale” makes assumptions “that have since been disproven in the canon established by George Lucas” (Greedo nineteen at death versus a *The Phantom Menace* deleted scene showing him as a child contemporaneous with Anakin). *The New Essential Guide to Characters* (2002) tried to preserve both accounts with a “Greedo the Elder” father; “However, ‘Sphere of Influence,’ a Season Three episode of *Star Wars: The Clone Wars* (2010), made this fix impossible, as it is clear that Lucas intends Greedo to be an older, luckless hunter who has spent years on Tatooine trying to make a name for himself.” Same pattern as S101/S191: Lucas’s screen work (here a deleted film scene plus a Lucas-supervised series episode) overrides both the short story and a later EU patch. Tight-crop S241 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S242 / Galaxy Guide 1 Spotlight:** “Hammertong” is an elaborate continuity fix for the Tonnika sisters (*Galaxy Guide 1* described them as identical; the film performers are different; the story makes the film sisters imposters). The Spotlight notes that from 1987 to 1990, while many regarded the brand as dormant, West End Games was quietly building “the foundations of the Expanded Universe … before the relaunch of the novels in 1991.”
- **S250:** the Squib trader Macemillian-Winduarté, “Mace Windu” for short, first appeared in *Galaxy Guide 7* (1993), years before the Episode I Jedi; the name was taken from an early Lucas draft script.
- **S252:** *Death Star* is placed so Episode IV is experienced first. Motti’s full name, Conan Antonio Motti, was a last-minute editorial change coinciding with Lucas jokingly revealing it on *Late Night with Conan O’Brien* (2007). The novel omits the station’s role in *The Force Unleashed* because the game’s story was not public until a year after publication.
- **S254 Chronological Note “A Death Star Is Born” (major: “George Lucas’s canon history of the Death Star”):** mid-1990s EU architects (Tarkin, Lemelisk, the Maw, Umak Leth, Magrody, later Sienar) are a snapshot that “barely scratched the surface.” “*Star Wars*: Episode II *Attack of the Clones* (2002) had the biggest shocker: the Geonosians were chiefly responsible for the Death Star’s construction. In George Lucas’s canon history of the Death Star, it was the insectoid weaponsmiths who built the first battle station within the rings of their world, a fact verified by a visit to Geonosis in the Disney theme parks ride *Star Tours: The Adventures Continue* (2011).” Plans-theft accounts (Farlander, Katarn, *Battlefront II*, *Empire at War*, *Lethal Alliance*, *Lost Jedi*) remain convoluted; *Empire at War* tried to unify them by scattering the stolen plans. Tight-crop S254 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S254–S255 Spotlight *The Farlander Papers*:** the 1993 strategy guide places Farlander as the anonymous Y-wing fleeing the doomed Death Star at the end of *A New Hope*. A 2008 Hasbro figure based on an unnamed *Return of the Jedi* B-wing pilot is described as “retroactively making Farlander present in the film.”
- **S259:** the *Executor* just entering service places “Small Favors” within six months of Yavin, matching the *Classic Star Wars* newspaper-strip *Executor* blockade dating. A sly Yoda reference (wisdom from an acquaintance after a crash on a swamp planet) is not elaborated.
- **S261:** Han’s stolen reward in *Scoundrels* comes from Marvel *Star Wars* #7 (1977), “the first issue to move beyond the events of Episode IV.”
- **S262:** the *Rebel Force* juveniles are “some of the few from this point in the time line that benefit from full knowledge of the events of Episodes I through III.” Luke’s Podrace is a deliberate nod to Anakin’s Boonta Eve victory; C-3PO is interrupted before he can cite it.
- **S263:** in *Hostage*, Han recalls Obi-Wan’s Alderaan quote “though he was not present to hear it said in” Episode IV. Ferus Olin reappears from *Last of the Jedi*.
- **S264:** “Although Luke Skywalker says in *Star Wars: Episode IV A New Hope* that he will never return to Tatooine, the Expanded Universe finds him coming back to the desert planet many times between Episodes IV and VI” (*Tatooine Sojourn*, 1979; Marvel #31–33; Blackthorne *Star Wars 3-D* #1; *Shadows of the Empire*). Film line versus EU practice, noted but not disavowed.
- **S266:** *Trapped* is suggested to be about two months after Yavin. Vader knowing Luke destroyed the Death Star “is first established in Marvel Comics’ *Star Wars* #35 (1980), and elaborated upon in *Vader’s Quest* (1999).”
- **S268:** *Allegiance* is the first chronological Mara Jade account; her first published appearance was *Heir to the Empire* (1991), so chronological readers already know her Imperial allegiance when she later introduces herself to the trio.
- **S270 (major: framed-tale canonicity may be “subject to interpretation”):** “The Most Dangerous Foe” has a 0 ABY frame, but the bulk is set c. 4000 BBY during *Tales of the Jedi*. “Because this story is presented as a tale told by Deen Voorson, handed down through the generations, the canonicity of its events may be subject to interpretation.” Tight-crop S270 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S271:** whether the Gun of Command Cecil Noone steals is the same one Han uses in *The Courtship of Princess Leia* “is not known.”
- **S272:** “Fair Prey” cites Connell’s “The Most Dangerous Game”; the same story inspired *The Clone Wars* episodes “Padawan Lost” and “Wookiee Hunt.”
- **S281–S282 (major: continuity non-meeting rule):** *Choices of One* “carefully manages several key character interactions.” “Mara Jade and Luke Skywalker, for example, work together to achieve the same goal of freeing hostages, but the necessities of continuity dictate that the two characters cannot meet until much later in the Expanded Universe time line. Similarly, Thrawn offers direct advice and guidance to Commander Pellaeon, via remote and faceless means, since the two cannot meet until just prior to the events of *Heir to the Empire* (1991).” Tight-crop S281–S282 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S284:** Dash Rendar’s *Outrider* “is erroneously called the *Outrunner*” in *Galaxy of Fear: The Doomsday Ship*, “which has since been retroactively declared another, earlier vessel in Dash’s career.”
- **S285–S286 (major: “one of the toughest stories to reconcile”):** *Galaxy of Fear: Clones* “introduces an extremely advanced form of cloning that outpaces all others depicted elsewhere.” Dantooine’s Jedi ruins are shown in active use in *Tales of the Jedi* and *Knights of the Old Republic* comics “but there is no indication of any cloning technology in use during that era.” Vader captures the tech for the Emperor, “but it never surfaces again.” “Also unanswered is why a clone of Vader would resemble a man who is mostly artificial.” Tight-crop S285–S286 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S288:** *The Hunger*’s “over a year ago” framing “suggests that this book takes place at least eighteen months after the Battle of Yavin,” but its epilogue has Vader contacting Boba Fett to pursue the *Millennium Falcon*, “too early to be the assignment seen in *The Empire Strikes Back* (1980).”
- **S292:** *Splinter of the Mind’s Eye*, “the first *Star Wars* spin-off novel,” was written with the possibility of being filmed (Gary Kurtz; Han’s absence is because Harrison Ford was not contracted for sequels). As early EU it has “dated inconsistencies in characterization and continuity” (war-weary cynical Luke; more-than-brotherly feelings toward Leia; an arch, snarling Vader; a lightsaber duel that “doesn’t match the level of skills displayed in *The Empire Strikes Back*”; swimming ability reversed versus contemporary Marvel). The 1996 Dark Horse adaptation softened those inconsistencies and added continuity-enhancing elements (Piett, the *Executor*). The Kaiburr crystal “stems from early drafts of the *Star Wars* screenplay, where it was a Force-amplifying gem employed by the Sith Lords,” later explained away as diminishing with distance from the Temple of Pomojema.
- **S296:** between “Side Trip” and *Choices of One*, “it would appear that the Empire owes its triumph at the Battle of Hoth” to Thrawn (narrowing the search to ice planets; Derra IV; recommending Veers), in exchange for access to Vader’s Noghri, who “will figure prominently in the Thrawn Trilogy.”
- **S300–S301 (major: novelization spoiler; Fett’s armor “aligns nicely with current Clone Wars canon”):** the *Empire Strikes Back* novelization reached bookstores on April 12, 1980, so “anyone who picked [it] up … could have found out the shocking truth over a month before the movie arrived in theaters.” Yoda is long-haired and blue-skinned, matching early concept art. By keeping Fett’s backstory out of the novel — he is simply “dressed in a weapon-covered, armored spacesuit, the kind worn by armored warriors defeated by the Jedi Knights during the Clone Wars” — Glut “avoided the pitfall of Mandalorian continuity that would complicate later books. In fact, this description of Fett’s suit aligns nicely with current Clone Wars canon.” Tight-crop S300–S301 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S305 (major: Lucasfilm reticent to fix *Empire*’s duration):** “Of Possible Futures” spans a month from Hoth to the Rebel fleet reunion, “unusual specificity about the length of time encompassed by that film, something Lucasfilm has been reticent to set down firmly.” A 1995 West End Games writers’ guide had an internal estimate of eight months, “but this length has never been backed up by published sources.”
- **S310–S311:** *Shadows of the Empire* was “a coordinated effort to tell a single, unified *Star Wars* story across multiple media,” mounted when a projected 1997 Episode I date proved unfeasible and the *A New Hope* Special Edition re-release moved from 1996 to 1997. It was “a movie without a movie”: all the expected theatrical tie-ins, except a movie.

**Chapter 7 asides (S458–S507; written into `body/07-the-new-jedi-order.md`):**

- **S458 (major Lucas: NJO overview; no dark-side invaders):** after Bantam, Del Rey’s New Jedi Order was originally planned as 29 books over five years, later whittled to 19. An early discarded outline had the invaders as proto-Sith and had Jacen die. “George Lucas commented on the story overview, making a number of specific changes. Among them, he disapproved of the idea that the invaders were dark side users. Per Lucas, such dark siders would ultimately vie for power, which would be their undoing as a group. Thus no Sith or proto-Sith appear in the series.” Tight-crop S458 before any sealed verbatim.
- **S466 (major: Chewbacca’s death; Vong name evolution):** Chewbacca’s death is “a seismic event designed to mark a bold new era”; the idea was initially suggested by Dark Horse editor Randy Stradley. Working names Adzakans / Vici-Vicians / Yunnan Vong / Yuuzhan Vong. Early concepts of dark-side humans were later dropped; Salvatore supplied the bio-organic hook. ## 2. Outside this book — the Holocron G/T/C/S/N levels

**This entire section is not from *The Essential Reader’s Companion*.** The Companion never prints these letter-level names. They come from Lucasfilm’s internal continuity database (the Holocron) as described in public comments by its keeper, **Leland Chee**, during the period in which this book was written (pre–April 2014).

The usual public formulation of that hierarchy, with **George Lucas as supreme authority**, is:

| Level | Name | Typical contents | Role in a conflict |
|-------|------|------------------|--------------------|
| **G-canon** | George Lucas canon | The six (later, in this era, six theatrical) feature films; material from Lucas’s own creative word (and, in some of Chee’s tellings, film novelizations to the extent they reflect Lucas’s involvement). | Highest. A G-canon fact overrides every lower level. |
| **T-canon** | Television canon | *Star Wars: The Clone Wars* (2008 film and television series), as a Lucas-supervised screen production. | Next. T-canon yields to G-canon but overrides C/S/N. This is the “television productions in which George Lucas is directly involved” layer the Companion describes without using the letter T. |
| **C-canon** | Continuity canon | The great majority of the licensed Expanded Universe: adult and young-adult novels, many comics, and other officially published stories tracked as in-continuity. | Yields to G- and T-canon when they directly contradict it; otherwise treated as having happened. This is the “Expanded Universe that exists parallel” and “can reliably be said to have occurred” layer in the Companion’s wording. |
| **S-canon** | Secondary canon | Older, peripheral, or only-partly-vetted material (some early EU, some RPG/ancillary work, and similar) that can be drawn from when needed but is more easily displaced. | Used when not contradicted by a higher level; lower reliability than C-canon. |
| **N-canon** | Non-canon | *Infinities*, explicit alternates, parodies, and other stories officially outside continuity. | Does not bind the continuity. |

**How conflicts resolve (Holocron rule, still outside this book):** a fact from a higher level overrides a conflicting fact from a lower level. G-canon is supreme because it is George Lucas’s own screen (and closely associated) work. T-canon is the next Lucas-supervised screen tier. C-canon fills the published universe unless a later canon project contradicts it. That last clause is the same idea the Companion states in its own words on S008 and applies on S101 (*Jedi Trial* date vs. the Lucas-supervised animated series).

**Useful public sources for this outside section** (not opened as part of the CBR read; listed so the hierarchy is not unsourced):

- Leland Chee, public explanations of the Holocron continuity database and the G/T/C/S/N levels, issued while he was at Lucasfilm in the 2000s and early 2010s (starwars.com “Keeper of the Holocron” / Holocron-related Q&As and blog posts of that period). These are the primary public descriptions of the lettered tiers.
- The Companion itself (this book, 2012) is *not* a source for the letter names, but it is a contemporary licensed-reference statement of the same underlying idea: Lucas’s cinematic work is the most definitive canon; the EU is parallel and stands unless a canon project contradicts it.

**The 2014 “Legends” label (also outside this book):** in April 2014, after this Companion was published, Lucasfilm announced that existing Expanded Universe material would be rebranded **Star Wars Legends** and that a new, unified canon would go forward from the films, *The Clone Wars*, and subsequent storytelling. That reboot is later than this 2012 book and is not described in it. The hierarchy recorded above is the **pre-2014 / Legends-era** system the owner asked for, with Lucas at the top, which is also the system the Companion’s own “Canon and Continuity” section is talking about in 2012 language.
