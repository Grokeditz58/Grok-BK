## Thursday, June 21 — carbon-freezing chamber; “I love you” / “I know”

Practical two-section carbon-freezing set: clawlike tong, deck “thirty feet in diameter and some thirty-five feet off the ground,” no guard rails, steam, hydraulics. Story gloss (Arnold): Vader’s “real quarry is Luke and by ordering Han to be put into carbon freeze, an ordeal that may prove fatal, Vader is merely experimenting. If Han survives being frozen solid, Luke will meet the same fate.” Lando “seems to acquiesce… he is powerless… beneath the might of Vader.” Han–Leia: “It has been a cat-and-mouse game up to now, but this scene of Han’s departure into the void must surely be the moment of truth.”

Arnold bugs Kershner with a cordless mike all day (Peter Suschitzky lighting; “Suschitzsky” typo also appears later in the book). Kershner plans the entrance: long lens, tong rising, “Then I’ll cut into a couple of close-ups of Vader and Lando. When Vader says, ‘Put him in the carbon freeze. . . .’ Boom! Cut to a close-up of the reaction. Cut back to the long shot as the Wookiee goes crazy.” Later beat: tong down, then “As it rises, you see Vader.” Norman Reynolds sketches a window; Gary “thinks it ought to be a square window” because “a square window suggests the idea of being on a ship.” Kershner: “Don’t they have round windows on ships?”

Trailer, 11 A.M., typography locked — Ford and Kershner invent the farewell:

- Kershner: the three don’t know Han is the one going into the pit; “we have to add some lines.” Leia and Chewie are there “so you will not make problems.”
- Ford: “I don’t have any lines… surely I should say something, try to talk my way out.” Wants to ask Lando what will happen to Leia; tries “She will be mine”; rejects “buddy buddy” stuff.
- Ford: “I think I should be manacled. It won’t stop the love scene. I mean I don’t have to put my arms around Leia to kiss her… It has to be rough and brisk and over with.”
- Kershner: “Absolutely. I don’t intend to mess around . . . ‘What’s up, buddy boy?’ . . . in the love scene.”
- Ford: “As I pass by her, I think Leia ought to say very simply, ‘I love you.’”
- Kershner: “(Tries it out) ‘I love you.’ And you say, ‘Just remember that, Leia, because I’ll be back.’ You’ve got to say, ‘I’ll be back.’ You must. It’s almost contractual” (no end punctuation in the XHTML).
- Ford: “If she says ‘I love you,’ and I say ‘I know,’ that’s beautiful and acceptable and funny.”
- Kershner: “Right, right.”

They then wrestle Vader’s presence, Boba Fett (“What if he doesn’t survive? He’s worth a lot to me.”), and lines (“What’s up, pal?” / “You’re being put in the carbon freeze” / “It’ll make you behave” / “easier to transport”). Billy Dee Williams rehearses; Carrie arrives late, not in make-up. Kershner explains why she is there (to keep Han from fighting). Fisher: “No crying, no kissing?” He: “The kissing comes after.” She considers slapping Lando; lands “quite a powerful whack” on Williams (“Don’t hit me like that”).

Confrontation, typography locked: “You talk to Harrison about the changes, but I always feel that you do it behind my back.” “I would just like to be there when you decide to change things.” Kershner: “You weren’t here to be there.” Fisher “(Shouts) I was in the studio!” “I yelled at Harrison about the changes.” “Harrison shouldn’t have to come to me with the changes. You should.” “And now I have to perform at half an hour’s notice scenes that have all been changed.” “All I’m asking is to be invited to watch you guys get a scene together. It may not center around me, like this one doesn’t, but I’m involved in it.” Kershner, to himself: “Jesus, what a day! I’ve got problems with the actors. Everybody’s furious with everybody else. . . .”

Dave Prowse interjects about his book *Fitness is Fun*; then Vader’s cue: “When they finish the dialogue you say, ‘Put him in the carbon freeze,’ and that is the moment of realization… Leia is horrified. She holds on to Han. Chewie goes berserk.” First printed take around 1:45 P.M.; after a Boba Fett early walk, Kershner prints the last two: “Perfect. In fact, it was very nearly good!”

## Thursday, July 19 — first long Lucas interview

Arnold’s gloss: Lucas “has put everything he owns into an enterprise… ‘I’m willing to take that risk because I started with nothing. Five years ago I had nothing.’” Third trip to London for *Empire*; watches dailies on videotape; works the second cut while the editor does the first; “Kersh is worrying primarily about the specific moment… what I’m constantly keeping my eye on is the whole picture… But one of my primary concerns is special effects.” Sets: “Ralph McQuarrie, Joe Johnston, and I worked on the designs… some of which are improvementS.” (capital S locked.) Only the second of his pictures he hasn’t directed — “More American Graffiti (1979) was the first” (missing punctuation around the title). “I can now see why screenwriters go crazy.” Differences of interpretation: “no two people see with the same eyes.” “With *Empire*, since I am a step away from it, I didn’t expect as much, yet it’s turning out much more like I imagined than did the first one.” “Reality, the reality of the world we know, is a tangible presence in most films… But in a film like this, where we’re creating a world that doesn’t exist, it’s very easy to puncture a viewer’s sense of reality by a missing or wrong detail.”

Typography locked, character-design questions from the first film: “But now I know what Luke Skywalker looks like; I know how lightsabers work; I know what Darth Vader’s appearance should be. On the first film I didn’t really know all these things. Should Luke be a young intellectual or just a crazy kid? Should Darth Vader be big and tall? How does Han Solo react to danger?” *Empire* not simpler: crew now “knows what kind of film we are making.” *Star Wars* shot in seventeen weeks (scheduled thirteen). Kershner hired “because his reputation is one of being a fast director.” “I don’t think we’re trying to make it bigger. We’re obviously trying to make everything better. I think we’re just trying to make it as good as the first one.” “Generally, I enjoy not directing… I like what Kersh is doing creatively.” “If you were directing, do you think you could speed up the process?” “I think I could.”

**Nine-film / two-trilogy claim — do not collapse** with Kaminski 167, Jones 166, later Lucas denials, or Kurtz’s “no chronological sequence” line from Norway. Typography locked: “The first script was just murder to write, just awful. It took me two years. Most of it was simply finding my way through that world. Now I’m much more immersed in the world. When I did the story for the second film, it was easier because it’s really part of the *Star Wars* story. The first script was one of six original stories I had written in the form of two trilogies. After the success of *Star Wars* I added another trilogy. So now there are nine stories. The original two trilogies were conceived of as six films of which the first film was number four.”

Leigh Brackett “write the screenplay, but tragically she died right after completing the first draft… I was forced to write the second draft… almost enjoyable. It still took me three months to do, but that’s a lot different from two years. I also had the advantage of Larry Kasdan coming in later to do a rewrite and fix it up.” Yoda: “I started designing Yoda with Joe and Ralph. We worked for about four months…” “Dagobah does appear in the next film”; they hoped to pre-shoot the set, “But time has gotten so tight… we’re just going to have to wait and rebuild the set for the next picture.”

Money, typography locked: “I’m faced with a situation where everything I own, everything I ever earned, is wrapped up in this picture. If it isn’t a success not only could I lose everything, but I could also end up being millions of dollars in debt… It has to be the biggest grossing sequel of all time for me to break even.” Dream of “a research retreat for film”; “*Star Wars* films are at the heart of it all… I don’t want to spend the rest of my life making *Star Wars* pictures, but I do want to get them set up so that they’ll operate properly without my having to get completely involved in all of them.” Imitation vs “outright copying that tries to convince the public that this or that is part of the *Star Wars* saga.” Cost: “when Kersh asks for something, he gets a little more than he asks for, and it’s that little more that translates into millions of dollars.” “*American Graffiti* was made in twenty-eight days.” Coppola / *Apocalypse Now*: “I was going to make that picture for $2 million; he made it for over $30 million… I’d be extremely worried.” (“breaking event” typo for “breaking even.”) “I believe in discipline… It’s like being a gladiator.” “People have said my films have no content, but the truth is they have much more content than most critics realize.” Cukor: “A filmmaker is like being a toymaker.” Lucas: “I’d rather be a toymaker.” “On *Star Wars* I couldn’t do that… If I were ever going to create on that scale again, I told myself, I would have to do it through others.” “At first I was contemplating selling the whole thing to Fox to do whatever they wanted with it. I’d just take my percentage and go home and never think about *Star Wars* again. But the truth of it is I got captivated by the thing. It’s in me now… I can see that world. I know the way the characters live and breathe. In a way, they have taken over.” “I learned the system and I beat it. Now I want to use it to make the kind of films I’m interested in, regardless of their commerciality.”

## Friday, July 27 — second Lucas interview (roots, folklore, fairy tale)

Secure 1950s California childhood; “It wasn’t until Kennedy was killed that I became involved…” *Graffiti* “was trying to portray change… you can’t stay seventeen forever… I was trying to portray the rituals of American teenagers… the whole concept of ‘cruising.’” Wanted to be a painter; raced cars; “a bad accident and realized that I wasn’t going to be a Stirling Moss.” Junior college, social sciences and anthropology. “Í hate writing—I’ve always hated it. Never was very good at it.” (“Í” typo locked.) Father “very much against” art school: “you can go to art school, but you’ll have to pay your own way.” USC via a friend: “the film department was real easy.” Sixties: “I went to marches, but I wasn’t an instigator.”

Why *Star Wars* after *Graffiti*, typography locked: “I had always been interested in outer space and science fiction. Through anthropology I had gotten interested in folklore and mythology and in their role as an anchor for societies. I came to realize that America has no modern fairy tales. You could say that the Western movie is the last of our myths. I feel that young people need some kind of fantasy life… In the earliest times it was the father who taught the young the way to live… The father taught the social conventions… Then organized religion became the father figure… But I feel with religion losing its influence over the young, television is becoming the major teaching force. But television has no central core, and is amoral… In *Star Wars* I was striving to make a strong positive statement. My first film THX-1138) was a parable about the way we are living our lives today” (missing opening parenthesis). “*THX*” “rather depressing… Being a pessimist doesn’t seem to accomplish anything… The way to make things progress is to point people in the right direction, to show how wonderful life can be.” Religion: “My parents sent me to Sunday school and when I got to be old enough, twelve or thirteen, I rebelled… I have strong feelings about God and the nature of life, but I’m not devoted to one particular faith.” “I was trying to design a modern fairy tale. I wanted it to have a strong moral basis designed to teach young people… It started with the idea of good and evil, which is found in most fairy tales.” Not market research: “an extension of my research into fairy tales, folklore, and organized religion. It was study rather than research… I didn’t say to myself, What do people want now? What’s going to sell?”
