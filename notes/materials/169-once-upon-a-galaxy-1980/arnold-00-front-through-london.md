## Foreword (heading locked as “Forward”)

Arnold, unit publicist, writing January 1980. Summer 1977, on a Greek location, hears of *Star Wars*; some technicians who had worked on it “were surprised, even puzzled.” London, autumn 1977: “in England no one, not even the critics, had seen it. The newsmen, not the publicists, were heralding its arrival.” Comparison to *Oklahoma!* as a convention-breaking original; “Something had come out of America that was quintessentially American, broke the conventions, and was destined to create its own legend.” December 1977, “the Dominion. Theatre, London.” Fox: “no film in living memory had launched itself with such meteoric thrust”; general release earlier than planned; Pickwick Papers docksides comparison (Arnold). Autumn 1978: hired as unit publicist for *The Empire Strikes Back*, “by which time *Star Wars* itself would have earned some $400 million at the box office and have become the most commercially successful picture in history.” He still did not understand “why so many Americans had come to regard the content of this movie as folklore worthy of near-religious zeal.” “I had never met George Lucas. I was not a science-fiction buff, and fantasy for me began and ended with *Alice in Wonderland*.” Gary Kurtz’s stance: “when you’ve got a success on the scale of *Star Wars* you can neither rest on your laurels nor proclaim to the world how clever you are. Information, not hyperbole, would be the order of the day.” Signed “-Alan Arnold / January 1980.”

## Chapter 1 — Norway (3–9 March 1979)

Train to Finse: Kurtzes, Hamills, Carrie Fisher and school friend Charlie. Arnold had met Carrie the previous October at a Northamptonshire charity equestrian event the *Star Wars* company sponsored. Oslo press conference: Kurtz “described *The Empire Strikes Back* as ‘a new chapter in the *Star Wars* saga,’ because the intention is never to refer to it as a sequel for the simple reason that future George Lucas stories do not have chronological sequence.” “*Star Wars* had so far earned $400 million throughout the world… the new picture would cost twice as much as the original to make, due partly to inflation.” Mark “said he had been contracted for three films in the saga, but that all depended on how the new one fared.” Carrie “had come to Norway out of curiosity, not to take part in the filming because the scenes at Finse do not require her—she just could not bear to miss the location atmosphere.” (em dash normalized in this note only where the EPUB has a hyphen mid-clause; quote from XHTML uses the hyphen if re-cited verbatim.)

Location logistics: Camp Sharman, Camp Kurtz; glacier 6,000 feet; white-outs; Robert Scott trained here. Robert Watts on why Finse (not Sweden or Finland). John Barry (Oscar, *Star Wars* production designer) had been helping prepare *Empire* while expecting to direct *Saturn 3*; Norman Reynolds became production designer when Barry took *Saturn 3*. Lucas, Kershner, and Kurtz liked Finse from photographs; Kurtz and Reynolds visited May 1978. Kershner, November 1978, helicoptered to the summit, left to “meditate on the vista,” then the chopper had engine trouble; he walked the four miles back in Hyde Park clothing.

**Kershner interview, Sunday, March 5,** typography locked. First saw *Star Wars* with his ten-year-old son: “a very satisfying thing about the film was knowing who was good and who was bad.” “Then, suddenly, you see an entertainment where the order of things is clear.” “People are interested in Zen, in the power that is in everyone… They are saying, ‘There is evil out there.’ But the evil people in the picture are really not that much different from the good people. They’re using the same power, the Force, but they’re using it for evil.” “An American film has a certain rhythm… In terms of the *Star Wars* films, we are spending more time in *The Empire Strikes Back*) developing relationships between the characters, but the picture will still move very quickly.” (stray closing parenthesis locked.) “It’s like telling a story breathlessly… We are dealing with kings and queens, people of immense power.” “Princess Leia’s Rebel forces will not do anything in order to win. They will not sacrifice lives. They do not descend to the level of the enemy… It’s possible to fight because you love, not just because you hate.” “These films are morality plays… They are also fairy tales… A morality play is at its best when it has mystery… I think the characters emerge more fully in this picture than in the first one… They have rivalries, jealousies, sexual tensions.” Huxley, 1950, via Kershner: “I certainly think it will move through naturalism into pure fantasy. . . . Film is the only medium that can make the unbelievable believable.”

**Kurtz interview, Monday, March 6.** Inflation; exchange rates; “the present picture is conceived on a broader scale.” The three American principals “each has the same deal” (final pay differs by days/overtime). On Lucas and stardom: Arnold, “Star status is something George Lucas doesn’t encourage, I believe.” Kurtz: these pictures “are essentially team efforts… there are few performers today who could single-handedly carry a picture.” Directors: “In terms of a saga of interrelated stories we always wanted to have each picture directed by a different person, to add a slightly different style or focus to the overall development.” They interviewed several directors; one declined as “a successor to someone else’s work.” They wanted someone who “would also develop the characters without losing sight of the inherent humor or slowing down the action… Also, Kersh isn’t cynical… The worst thing that could have happened would have been to engage a director who didn’t believe in the material.” Why Lucas is not in Norway: “He’s writing and I don’t think he could write where we are. Also, he’s engrossed with the special effects material being prepared in California. And he doesn’t like cold climates.” *Star Wars* spent “over $5 million… in Britain for materials and labor”; *Empire* “close to $15 million.”

**Harrison Ford interview, Wednesday, March 7** (arrival telex locks “Harrison. Ford”). Diversified after *Star Wars* (*Heroes*, *Apocalypse Now* as “Colonel Lucas—a little ‘in’ humor,” *Force 10 from Navarone*, *Hanover Street*, *The Frisco Kid*); carpenter years; *Graffiti* and *The Conversation*. On Han, typography locked: “It’s George’s creation. He gave himself a beautiful princess in Leia and a callow youth in Luke. He gave himself a sage warrior in Ben Kenobi and he gave himself a character called Han Solo, who is described as ‘a cynical space pirate.’ But he’s not really a cynic and the phrase ‘space pirate’ doesn’t really indicate what he does… He’s the most realistic character and consequently, in a way, the easiest one to play… So, from an audience’s point of view, my job was to help tell the story. Han helps make everything contemporary, recognizably human, in a way… I was just part of the equation George dreamed up.” “He’s not a cardboard character for me at all… I never thought of the character as having only two dimensions until the critics said so. And they’re wrong. The third dimension is me.” “There is no pretension. I enjoy working for them.”

**Mark Hamill interview, Thursday, March 8.** 1976 car accident; Kurtz found him in hospital; married Marilou. “In a sense, George Lucas did find me.” Lucas wanted “sincerity, a desire for adventure, a naive quality. Luke is the classic example of a character who is yearning for the adventure that lies beyond the horizon. Like Jim Hawkins in *Treasure Island*…” “You need one character who relates to everyday life… I think Luke is that character and that any one of the young people watching the picture can say, ‘I’m Luke.’” On *Empire*, typography locked: “Luke will be tempted by what he has to undergo. It was made clear in *Star Wars* that the dark side of the Force is very seductive, that it is easier to achieve your goals by allowing anger and aggression to guide you, which is what has happened to Darth Vader. As the saga continues there will be constant changes in all the characters. Luke certainly isn’t the same boy now as he was in the first film.”

Friday, March 9: mountain-rescue skiers as extras; “the ಇಜ್ಡy is making a donation to the Norwegian Red Cross” — garbage cluster, almost certainly “Army.” Tauntaun: “a new addition to the George Lucas bestiary.” Arnold leaves that night for London.

## Chapter 2 — London (10–11 March 1979)

Elstree history (Hitchcock, MGM, *2001*; now “the home of *Star Wars*”). Kurtz in 1975 was shown every British facility except Elstree (then closed after *Murder on the Orient Express*). Hollywood lots could not offer the space in the needed slot. Italy investigated; Britain chosen for “studio space availability, talent and labor pool, transport to North Africa… and language because, of course, everybody in England speaks English of a Soft.” (conversion slip, likely “of a sort.”) Pinewood’s internal staffing was a heavy overhead; Elstree was a “wall-to-wall studio.” They built the *Star Wars* throne-room on Shepperton Stage H. After the first film’s success, “George Lucas’s dream of making a saga of interrelated space adventures became a reality and the decision to make *The Empire Strikes Back* was taken.” New Star Wars stage at Elstree: “250 feet long, 122 feet wide, and 45 feet high… in excess of one-and-a- quarter million cubic feet.” Full-scale Millennium Falcon built by maritime engineers at Pembroke Docks: “65 feet in diameter, 16 feet high, with a mandible giving it an overall length of 80 feet. Its weight is 23 tons,” in sixteen sections, moved on hovercraft-style air pads. Five R2-D2 units (one submersible) at an Oxfordshire rocking-horse factory; Ogle Design snowspeeders at Letchworth Heath. January 24 fire: Kubrick’s *The Shining* Stage 3 “burned down… The *Empire Strikes Back* had only been waiting for Kubrick to finish on Stage 3 before moving in to build. Its total loss meant a complete rescheduling almost on the eve of production… Timewise it is doubtful the picture will recover.”
