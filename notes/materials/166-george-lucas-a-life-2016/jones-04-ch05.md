# Jones, *George Lucas: A Life* (2016): notes — ch. 5 *American Graffiti,* 1971–1973 (pp. 127–164)

Typography locked from the publisher XHTML. Citations: Jones p. N; endnotes as Jones Notes, ch. 5 n. Y.

## After Zoetrope; Kurtz; shelving *Apocalypse*; Flash Gordon (pp. 127–131)

Coppola is on *The Godfather,* paying Warner back. Lucas and Marcia scrape by on her *Candidate* work. *Apocalypse Now* still in script with Milius. Coppola introduces Lucas to Gary Kurtz (USC 1963; Corman; Vietnam marine film unit, later a conscientious objector / Quaker-ish). Kurtz comes to Mill Valley to learn Techniscope from the *THX* attic edit; “He was my kind of filmmaker.” (p. 129, n.3) Partnership that will oversee three huge films in ten years.

Coppola also pairs them on *Apocalypse.* Kurtz worries they are both too quiet to run a set. Lucas waffles: the film is technically Zoetrope’s, though he still argues it belongs to him and Milius. More important: **pp. 129–130 (n.5–6), typography locked:** “I was working on basically negative movies—*Apocalypse Now* and *THX,* both very angry.” “I realized after *THX* that people don’t care how the country’s being ruined. All that movie did was to make people more pessimistic, more depressed, and less willing to get involved in trying to make the world better.” He decided, “We’ve got to regenerate optimism.” *Apocalypse* shelved, with a vow to return.

They consider a modern *Hidden Fortress* (Sturges / *Magnificent Seven* model). What really fires them is Flash Gordon. Lucas loved the KRON serials; Kurtz is a fan. They approach King Features; “they were way too expensive for us” (Kurtz, n.7); restrictions “so draconian”; the syndicate is also dangling rights at Fellini (who also passes). **Jones, p. 131:** “Both Lucas and Kurtz knew that what they really wanted to do was ‘some kind of Flash Gordon–like science fiction story’… ‘something that we wanted to see, that we would pay to go see! And no one was making it!’ If Lucas couldn’t get the rights to Flash, then he’d simply create a world of his own that didn’t have to adhere to someone else’s rules or work with someone else’s characters. By denying Lucas Flash, King Features had inadvertently sent him down the path toward creating *Star Wars.*”

## “Warm human comedy”; selling *Graffiti*; Cannes and the birth of *Star Wars* (pp. 131–137)

**Lucas, p. 131 (n.10):** “After *THX,* I was considered a cold, weird director, a science fiction sort of guy who carried a calculator. And I’m not like that at all.” Coppola: “Don’t be so weird. Try to do something that’s human.” “Why don’t you try to write something out of your own life that has warmth and humor?” (n.11–12) Lucas: “If they want warm human comedy, I’ll give them one, just to show that I can do it.” (n.13)

Later he would claim an anthropology impetus: “the modern mating rituals of American youth who did their dance in cars.” Jones: “probably only partly true.” “If you’re a writer and a director, you make movies about the things you know about.” “Growing up in Modesto, I spent many years cruising Tenth Street, and I was enamored with the experience.” (n.14) Still hates writing (n.15). Five-page treatment of four young men, *I Vitelloni* model: “kind of the same issue about growing up. It was one of the themes in my first film, *THX,* and I wanted to expand on it.” (n.16) Huyck and Katz expand it to eighteen pages; promised the screenplay if it sells; agent Jeff Berg shops it. *THX* has just opened; doors slam. Only UA’s David Chasman takes a passing interest.

May 1971: *THX* in Directors’ Fortnight at Cannes; Warners won’t pay travel; last $2,000, hostels, Eurorail, with the Murches. Layover in New York with the Coppolas; Lucas pitches David Picker at UA. Ellie in labor with Sofia. **May 14, Lucas’s twenty-seventh birthday (p. 134, n.22):** “So it was my birthday, it was Sofia’s birthday, and I got *American Graffiti,* all on the same day.” $10,000 for the screenplay. Huycks decline (their own horror film); Richard Walter assigned.

**KEY, pp. 134–135 (n.25), typography locked — Jones calls this “really the birth of *Star Wars.*”** At the Carlton Hotel in Cannes, they seal *Graffiti,* and Picker asks if Lucas has anything else. “In a moment reminiscent of Coppola enthusiastically giving away the unfinished *Apocalypse Now*… Lucas told Picker all about his ideas for his unnamed ‘space opera fantasy film in the vein of Flash Gordon.’” “Great,” said Picker, “we’ll make a deal for that, too.” “And that,” said Lucas later, “was really the birth of *Star Wars.* It was only a notion up to then—at that point, it became an obligation!”

Walter’s script unusable (*Hot Rods to Hell* / a New York rock novel). Lucas writes it himself in three weeks, 8 a.m.–8 p.m., seven days a week, “bleeding on the page.” Picker rejects the four-interwoven-stories + rock-and-roll structure: “The kind of structure I had presented just wasn’t done in those days.” (p. 136, n.29) Lucas borrows from Coppola and from his father. Shoots a *Godfather* montage (newspaper headlines / Five Families war). Incorporates Lucasfilm Ltd.; leery of “Ltd.” vs. “Inc.” but keeps it “in perpetuity” (Kurtz, n.31). Turns down *Lady Ice* ($100,000 + net), *Tommy,* *Hair:* “a very dark period… I was so far in debt I thought I’d never get out.” (pp. 137, n.33–35)

## Universal, Coppola as producer, music, casting (pp. 137–145)

Title baffles studios (“an Italian movie about American feet”). Script’s first page: “*American Graffiti* is a MUSICAL. It has singing and dancing, but it is not a musical in the traditional sense because the characters in the film neither sing nor dance.” Ned Tanen (early *THX* student-film fan, car lover) gets the cassette-pitch: each character tied to a song evoking “what a certain generation of Americans thought being a teenager was really about—from about 1945 to 1962.” (p. 138, n.36) Budget $750,000, location only, music clearances out of that budget; no final-cut control (after *THX,* little room to negotiate). Studio wants a name; Tanen suggests a big-name producer: Coppola, post-*Godfather.* Lucas still nursing a Zoetrope grudge but knows Coppola will leave day-to-day to Kurtz.

Coppola offers to finance it himself; Ellie and the accountant stop a $700,000 City Bank loan (Ellie doesn’t like the script). Coppola: $25,000 + 10% of net; Lucas: $50,000 to write and direct + 40% of net. **The untitled space opera—“the Flash Gordon thing”—is rolled into the Universal deal as the next project if all goes well.** (p. 140) Lew Wasserman skeptical of both; Tanen goes to bat. Lucas: Universal “in effect wrote you a check and told you to go away and come back with a finished movie.” (n.43)

Music: executives want covers/orchestra; Kurtz: “We have to use the original records.” Elvis too expensive; Beach Boys via Dennis Wilson unlock other clearances; forty-three songs for about $90,000. Casting personal: “I’m sort of everybody.” “They were all composite characters, based on my life, and on the lives of friends of mine.” “I was Terry the Toad, fumbling with girls, then I became a drag racer like John.… And finally I became Curt. I got serious and went to college.” Steve “the only character I really wasn’t.” (p. 142, n.46–49) Videotaped callbacks (later an industry standard). Dreyfuss chooses Curt; Somers as the Thunderbird blonde — Jones/Lucas: Curt’s quest is “to *escape,* as Lucas explained it, the same theme that had driven *THX 1138.*” Dorothy: “George always wanted to have a blonde girlfriend. But he never did quite find her.” (n.51) Terry/Charles Martin Smith; Cindy Williams as Laurie; Ron Howard as Steve (confused by the “musical”). Harrison Ford as Falfa, carpenter, refuses a flattop, tucks hair into a white cowboy hat. “It would not be the last iconic hat Ford would wear for Lucas.”

## Spielberg friendship; shoot; Wolfman; theme; edit and recut; release (pp. 145–164)

Evenings at Matthew Robbins’s Benedict Canyon kitchen table with Spielberg (*Sugarland Express*). “That’s where we really got to know each other.” (n.55) Jones dates their first meeting as the 1968 (text here says 1969 — **flag: earlier, p. 92, the festival is January 19, 1968**; this chapter says “1969” — internal slip). Lucas teases Spielberg that *Name of the Game* episode “L.A. 2017” resembles *THX.* Praises *Duel.* Spielberg: “we’re constant complainers.” (n.58)

Huycks punch up dialogue: “The scenes are mine, the dialogue is theirs.” (n.59) Script finished May 10, 1972; cameras June 26, twenty-eight nights. Exhausting night shoot; San Rafael permission withdrawn; focus problems; assistant cameraman run over; five-alarm fire. Petaluma. Lucas asleep in a harness; flu; diabetes; “I don’t think I can last at this.” (n.64) Mushy natural-light footage; Haskell Wexler flies in as a friend. Lucas wants it to look like a jukebox, “very garish, bright blue and yellow and red” — “ugly,” muttered Marcia. (n.68–69) Little actor direction; multiple cameras; Terry’s Vespa crash is an accident kept in. Ford’s drinking; Kurtz cuts off the beer.

**Wolfman Jack, pp. 151–153.** First heard at USC, not actually in high-school Modesto (Jones corrects Lucas’s memory: Wolfman persona ~1962, XERF/XERB later). Lucas on the outlaw deejay as a make-believe friend (echo of *The Emperor*). Paid $3,000 plus a point. **Jones, p. 152, typography locked:** Wolfman delivers the film’s central message — “Get your ass in gear,” he tells Curt serenely, “dispensing wisdom in thoughtful sound bites much as Yoda would advise Luke Skywalker a decade later.” For Lucas, *THX* had been about escaping; *Graffiti* was about growing up. 1962, the year before Kennedy, as the year before the world changed. Evolve or face extinction. Title stays despite Coppola floating *Rock Around the Block.* **Lucas, 1974 (n.83):** “It’s about a period of transition in history in America… in the next two years everything changed… *Graffiti* is about the fact that you have to accept these changes—they were on the horizon—and if you didn’t, you had problems.… You try to fight it… and you lose.”

Filming ends Friday, August 4, 1972. Lucas to the *New York Times:* making movies is “excruciating… like climbing mountains.” (n.84) To Howard: “I’m really gonna direct it in the editing room.” (n.85) Verna Fields + Marcia; Murch “worldizes” the radio sound track (speakers in gyms, PAs, Lucas’s backyard). First pop sound track given its own dramatic presence — Jones points forward to Tarantino and the Coens. Cut from ~160 minutes down to the contractual 110, with Lucas overseeing the cuts.

Jan. 28, 1973, 10 a.m. Northpoint Theater preview: audience “went nuts”; Tanen livid, talks of maybe not releasing it / TV. Coppola explodes, offers to buy it; won’t speak to Tanen for nearly twenty years. Lucas: “Francis did what he was supposed to do. I was pretty proud of him.” (n.100) — contrast with his earlier anger that Coppola hadn’t stopped the *THX* recut. Universal takes the film; Lucas recuts ignoring Tanen’s notes; in-house editors, after Coppola’s persuasion, leave it mostly intact. **Universal pulls only three scenes, “a little more than four minutes.”** Lucas: “There was no reason for the cutting. It was just arbitrary.” (n.104) Murch: a “formative experience.” Lucas: “It’s more a moral issue than anything else.” (n.106) Wendy: “the beginning of his passion to become an independent filmmaker… total control.” (n.107) **Lucas, p. 159 (n.108):** “They’re people who have never made a movie in their lives—agents and lawyers with no idea of dramatic flow.… The movie industry was built by independent entrepreneurs, dictators who had a very strong feeling about movies.” Jones’s close: “Those four excised minutes would spawn an empire that would answer to the one independent entrepreneur and dictator who mattered: George Lucas.”

Opened Wednesday, August 1, 1973, limited NY/LA, then wide two weeks later; “stayed in theaters for, like, two years” (Tanen). Strong reviews (*Time,* *Post,* Champlin, *Times* “a work of art”). Lucas bristles at “art”: “I don’t like the word *art* because it means pretension and bullshit.… I don’t think of myself as an artist… I’m a craftsman. I don’t make a work of art; I make a movie.” (p. 160, n.115) *San Francisco Chronicle* pans it — the review his parents see. *Times:* “one of the world’s master directors.” Lucas: “I know how good I am. *Graffiti* is successful because it came entirely from my head. It was *my* concept. And that’s the only way I can work.” (n.124) Five Oscar nominations, zero wins (*The Sting*). Direct costs a little over $1 million; more than $55 million in rentals; Lucas nearly $4 million after taxes. **“As he had vowed to his father more than a decade before, Lucas was a millionaire before the age of thirty. In fact, he had done it with two years to spare.”** Points shared with actors, Wolfman, Wexler, Kurtz, Huycks, Pollock. Fight with Coppola over who pays Kurtz and Wexler from the split points; “Francis was questioning my honesty.” (n.129) Relationship sours for the better part of a decade.

Pays back his father and Coppola. Still drives a ’69 Camaro. Autumn 1973: buys the Victorian at 52 Park Way, San Anselmo — Marcia names it Parkhouse — screening room, offices, attic editing; free workspace for Ballard, Robbins, Murch. “It was a little filmmaking complex… his own miniature Zoetrope.” (n.132) At 30 Medway Road, a tower office and a desk built from three doors. **Close, pp. 163–164 (n.134), typography locked:** He is laboring over first drafts of the “Flash Gordon thing,” “a science fiction–Flash Gordon genre, *2001* meets James Bond, outer space and space ships flying in it.” “a much more plotted, structured film than [*THX*] or *American Graffiti.*… Since I’ve never done that before, it’s hard to say exactly what it is.” He has “a two-page treatment, handwritten on notebook paper, and a title, scrawled in cursive on the center of the treatment’s cover page: *The Star Wars.*”
