# Jones, *George Lucas: A Life* (2016): notes — ch. 9 “Darkening Skies,” 1979–1983 (pp. 278–321)

Typography locked from the publisher XHTML. Citations: Jones p. N; Jones Notes, ch. 9 n. Y. End of PART II EMPIRE 1973–1983.

## *More American Graffiti*; *Empire* production, Yoda, “I am your father” (pp. 278–288) — KEY

Jones opens with a feint: an actor holding out, a weak script, Lucas meddling and directing sequences himself — then reveals the sequel is *More American Graffiti,* not *Empire.* Universal re-released *Graffiti* in May 1978; Tanen reminds Lucas he still owes a film and threatens to produce a sequel without him. Lucas gives in — “exactly why he had insisted on all sequel rights in his *Star Wars* contract.” End cards from the first film already boxed him in. Dreyfuss out (“one-tenth my [usual] salary”). Howard Kazanjian as line producer; Bill Norton as writer-director Lucas “could control.” Four New Year’s Eve stories in four styles (Debbie split-screen; Toad’s Vietnam in scratchy 16 mm, “looking very much the way Lucas had envisioned shooting *Apocalypse Now*”). Deadline mid-August 1979, ahead of Coppola’s *Apocalypse.* Milius: “He wants to steal Francis’s thunder.” Forty-four-day shoot; Lucas directs nearly all the Vietnam sequences and takes over the edit. August 1979: a bomb. Maslin compares the Vietnam material unfavorably to *Apocalypse Now.* Lucas: “Everything I own, everything I ever earned, is wrapped up in *Empire.*… If it should be a [flop], I will lose everything.” (n.13–14)

Kasdan still punching *Empire* to the last moment; some Brackett/Lucas scenes make him think, “I can’t believe that George wrote this scene. It’s terrible.” Lucas will give Brackett a story credit out of respect. March 1979 Finse, Norway: blizzard; Kershner films Hamill from a hotel doorway. Elstree’s *Star Wars* Stage still unfinished, Stage 4 fire-damaged; pound vs dollar; budget $18 million → $22 million → $25 million by July. Lucas “done directing” but hovers, “itching to look through the eyepiece.” Kershner: “I’ve caught him looking through the camera only twice so far.” Personally oversees ILM; *Falcon* asteroid chase needs 200-plus composited pieces and a new optical printer.

Kershner lingers with actors; Ford’s “I know” is an ad-lib after a long talk with Fisher. When Kurtz steps between Lucas and Kershner he tends to take Kershner’s side; **even Marcia defends Kershner.** Kershner: “Marcia and I agreed a lot.… We thought the [*Star Wars*] movies could hold more character and more complexity. George thought they should be simple in another way. It was a serious philosophical difference.” June: Ladd resigns after a fight with Fox, triggered largely by the lopsided *Empire* contract. June 5: John Barry dies of meningitis at forty-three. Bank of America threatens to pull the loan (weekly payroll near $1 million). Lucas blames Kurtz for “coddling” Kershner. “George… banged me for the cost overruns on *Empire.*” Steers $525,000 from Black Falcon to Chapter II; moves to biweekly pay rather than go to Fox “on bended knee.” Finally gives Fox a few points to guarantee a First National Bank of Boston loan; Lucasfilm remains the ultimate guarantor. “I wanted my independence so badly.” Hovers daily; weaves a second edit from Hirsch’s first cut; some reels ruined in development.

**Yoda, pp. 285–287 (n.28–31).** Largely in Henson’s hands with Freeborn and ILM. Henson wants the smaller remote mechanisms for *The Dark Crystal.* Henson does not perform Yoda; recommends Frank Oz (Miss Piggy, Fozzie), who devises his own backstory, gravitas, humor, and a distinctive way of speaking. Lucas: giving a pivotal role to a rubber puppet “was a real leap, because if that puppet had not worked, the whole film would have been down the tubes.” Motors/rotors for eyes, ears, cheeks; two or three supporting puppeteers. Filming early August: Kershner often directs the puppet, not Oz; Lucas sits cross-legged in Yoda’s home “completely wrapped up in conversation with the puppet, even with Oz in plain sight.”

Guinness lured back over lunch for one-third of a point, less than a day’s work. End of August: Lucas replaces Kurtz with Kazanjian. “I had to get the film made… and that was all I really cared about at that point.” Principal photography ends Sept. 24, 1979. Lucas reassembles Hirsch’s complete cut for a quicker pace; result is a disaster (scenes too fast); Kershner, Hirsch, and Kurtz prevail, and he reverts to Hirsch’s slower edit.

**Vader reveal, p. 287 — typography locked.** James Earl Jones brought back, including “the delivery of a key piece of dialogue—‘No. *I* am your father’—that only Hamill, Kershner, and a few others had known about during filming. (Obscured by the Vader mask, Dave Prowse had delivered the line as ‘Obi-Wan killed your father,’ and wouldn’t know the real plot twist until he saw the movie in the theater.) For his part, as Jones recorded Vader’s dialogue in the winter of 1980, he remained convinced that Vader was lying.”

Yoda’s voice: “George didn’t want my voice,” said Oz; Lucas rejected the tape. After auditions he realizes, as with Daniels/Threepio, that “the person who is actually acting the role is really into it,” and Oz stays.

## Computers, Pixar, *Empire* release and the nine-part claim (pp. 287–292) — KEY

ILM: stop-motion AT-ATs and tauntauns; first use of a brand-new Apple computer to log shots. Lucas on film as “a stupid 19th century idea”; digital filmmaking would let him manipulate every element on-screen with no degradation — “The technology he envisioned didn’t exist yet.” First computer-division hire: Ed Catmull from NYIT; then Alvy Ray Smith and others, John Lasseter hired on the sly as “interface designer.” Lucas wants tools for digital movies, not “high-tech cartoons.” Over dinner the digital compositor is almost named “Picture Maker,” then “Pixer,” then **Pixar.**

***Empire* opens Wednesday, May 21, 1980, in 126 theaters, breaks records in 125.** *Time* puts Darth Vader on the cover: “a better film than *Star Wars.*” President Carter screens it at the White House for China’s Geng Biao, who “did not ask me for any of the weapons he saw in the movie.” Kael: “no sense that this ebullient, youthful saga is running thin in imagination.” Maslin: less lighthearted, less fun. Canby: “bland,” “directed at a distance.”

**Episode headers and retrocontinuity, pp. 290–291 (n.38–39) — typography locked.** Crawl now reads EPISODE V—THE EMPIRE STRIKES BACK; a re-release of *Star Wars* had already gained EPISODE IV—A NEW HOPE. “From here on, Lucas would always claim that he had taken his enormous first draft of *Star Wars,* divided it into thirds, then decided to film the middle third first, with *A New Hope* as the fourth installment of a nine-part saga.” **Kurtz: “That’s not true.… There were a lot of little bits and pieces that were reasonably good ideas and that ended up being in the final draft [of *Star Wars*],” but “there wasn’t enough material to do other movies.”** Lucas “would maintain he’d had a galaxy-spanning epic mythology in mind all along, though he would sometimes waffle on whether he had intended it to be six parts or nine.” Judith Martin (*Post*) bristles at the Wagner/Ring comparison as “nonsense.”

Break-even $57 million; five weeks to $64 million; nearly $210 million first run, third all-time after SW and *Jaws.* Lucasfilm pockets more than $100 million. “Lucas had literally bet the ranch—Skywalker Ranch—on *Empire* and won.”

## *Raiders*; guild fight; helping others; move north (pp. 291–302)

Marcia wants a vacation; instead, *Raiders.* Lucas shops it (self-finance $20 million, studio distributes) to Universal, Warners, Orion, Paramount. Eisner at Paramount, Nov. 7, 1979: 60–40 until break-even, then 50–50. Warners had dragged after wanting to make amends for *THX.* Indiana Smith becomes Indiana Jones after Spielberg thinks of *Nevada Smith.* Selleck locked by *Magnum, P.I.* Lucas had described Indy as “someone like Harrison Ford” but didn’t want a De Niro/Caan-style stable; they meet anyway and sign Ford June 1980. “Quick and dirty” like a serial; official schedule 88 days, private agreement shorter. Lucas vs Spielberg on Indy’s character (slick playboy vs more noble); Ford, as with Solo, finds the mix. Filming June 23, 1980; Lucas mostly leaves Spielberg alone, sometimes directs second unit, breaks two engines off a flying-wing model to save money. Kasdan asks why he didn’t direct it: “Because then I’d never get to see it.”

**Guilds, p. 295 (n.51–52):** *Empire* credits all at the end; DGA/WGA threaten to pull the film and fine Kershner $25,000. Lucas refuses to recut, pays the fine (“I consider it extortion”), resigns from both guilds. “The credits would stay at the end—where most films have put them ever since.” Wrap on day 73, fifteen days ahead of the official schedule, at Marcia’s birthday party. ILM lags on the Ark sequence (“They open the box and all hell breaks loose”); Lucas explodes, straddles their shoulders. Promised Spielberg final cut; few changes. Lucas prefers a longer scimitar fight; Spielberg keeps the short version; test audience proves Spielberg right. **Marcia notes they never see what happens to Marion after the Ark;** Ford and Allen return to film a City Hall “*Casablanca* moment.” “George might have intuitively known when a film didn’t work logically, but Marcia could always tell when he’d shortchanged the audience emotionally.”

USC gift nearly $5 million; later the George Lucas Instructional Building, Spielberg Music Scoring Stage, Marcia Lucas Post-Production Building, Gary Kurtz Patio. Helps fund Kurosawa’s *Kagemusha* via Ladd/Fox (Palme d’Or). Quietly produces Kasdan’s *Body Heat* (defers fee, keeps his name off). Funds Korty’s *Twice Upon a Time* (lumage animation); it sinks. May 14, 1980 — a week before *Empire* opens — he announces the next title: ***Revenge of the Jedi.***

Closes The Egg Company; “Hollywood is like a grandiose high school.” Dismisses Charlie Weber (who wanted to diversify); promotes Greber. Coppola, flush from *Apocalypse,* wants funding for Hollywood General; Lucas and Spielberg both say no. Lucas: Coppola “made a lot of money off me” and was “betraying all of us in San Francisco.” Coppola loses the studio after *One from the Heart.* Given $2 billion: Coppola would “borrow another $2 billion and build a city”; Lucas would “invest a billion… and use the other billion to build a town.” Ranch: Victorian octagons, Lake Ewok, tunnels, CCTV. “It’s a totally controlled environment.” ILM opened to outside work (*Dragonslayer* first; 13 VFX Oscars 1980–1995, often against itself). Catmull’s “Genesis Effect” in *Star Trek II* — “a sixty second commercial to George Lucas,” first fully computer-animated film sequence; Lucas watches from a doorway, compliments one camera shot, leaves. Hamill: “You traitors!” Lucas: “It’s business, kid.”

## *Jedi* story, Kurtz split, “Now I am Vader,” shoot, divorce, pyre, Fett (pp. 302–321) — KEY

“Lucas had no idea what was going to happen in *Revenge of the Jedi.* Beyond the title, he had little more than some notes scribbled on yellow pads.” Ford urged him to kill Solo; Lucas only froze him in carbonite. A third SW clause in Ford’s Indy contract makes Solo “seem safe.”

**Kurtz’s bittersweet ending, pp. 302–303 (n.76–80) — typography locked.** Kurtz loved *Empire*’s grown-up feel and wanted the last film to end “on a more poignant, almost downbeat note.” “The original idea was that they would recover Han Solo in the early part of the story… and that he would die in the middle part of the film in a raid on an Imperial base.” Leia would “assume her new duties as queen of the upstart Alliance, ‘with Luke riding off into the sunset, metaphorically, on his own. And that would have been a bittersweet ending… but I think it would have been dramatically stronger.’” Lucas “wouldn’t even consider it.” Kurtz: during *Empire* he had been ferrying Kenner reps around the set; “George then decided he didn’t want any of the principals killed.” Ford, sardonically: “George didn’t think there was any future in dead Han toys.” Kurtz: “They make three times as much on toys as they do on films. It’s natural to make decisions that protect the toy business, but that’s not the best thing for making quality films.”

The crack that ends the partnership: Lucas’s first *Jedi* treatment makes an assault on the Death Star pivotal. “It sounds redundant,” he confessed, “but we’ll do it again.” Kurtz: “I just didn’t want to do another attack on the Death Star.” “Mutual parting”; Kazanjian replaces him; Kurtz goes to Henson’s *The Dark Crystal.* They will not work together again.

Kershner declines (“I didn’t want to become simply another Lucas employee”). DGA blacklist risk. Looks at Donner, Dante, Cronenberg, **David Lynch** (Kubrick suggested *Eraserhead*; Lucas finds it “bizarre… but interesting”). Lynch visits the ranch, Ferrari ride, salad-bar lunch, migraine, “No way I can do this.” Hamill: “Lynch decided he didn’t want to do a George Lucas movie.” Lucas: “I think I may have gone a bridge too far on that one.” Settles on TV workhorse **Richard Marquand** (*Eye of the Needle,* *Birth of the Beatles*): “one who could understand that essentially it’d be George’s movie.” Marquand later: “I always had the feeling that possibly I’d find myself in a situation where I was a horse dragging this thing along, with George holding the reins.”

Lucas writes a full script first, leaves the ending blank for Kasdan, who takes it as a favor. Early summer 1981 Parkhouse story meeting (Lucas, Kasdan, Kazanjian, Marquand). He wants Jabba (now buildable); **“He wanted Luke to have a twin sister, though he *still* wasn’t certain it was Leia.”** A primitive society aiding the rebels, “another idea he’d lifted from the early drafts of *Star Wars* in which he’d brought a planet of Wookiees to the aid of the heroes.” McQuarrie and Johnston sketching before the script exists.

**Kasdan wants a death for “emotional weight.” Lucas, pp. 306–307 (n.93–97) — typography locked:** “You don’t have to kill people.… You’re a product of the 1980s. You don’t go around killing people. It’s not nice.” Then Lucas teases “the ultimate twist”: “Luke takes [Vader’s] mask off… and then Luke puts it on and says, ‘Now I am Vader.’ Surprise!… ‘Now I will go and kill the fleet and I will rule the universe.’” Kasdan: “That’s what I think should happen.” Lucas: “No, no, no.… Come on, this is for kids.” “The whole point of the film… is for you to be real uplifted, emotionally and spiritually, and feel absolutely good about life. That is the greatest thing that we could possibly ever do.” Ford: “George has a predisposition for happy endings.” Hamill finds it “so pat.” Lucas smiles: “So are fairy tales.”

Fox deal: Lucas has tied *Empire* profits up in the ranch, needs ~$30 million, doesn’t have it. Thirty-day ultimatum. Marvin Davis: $20 million loan; Fox share increases to 40 percent after $105 million. 224-page contract. Davis: “Georgie boy, you’re going to make me rich with movies.”

*Raiders* June 1981, near-unanimous raves (Canby, Schickel, Ebert). Kael: “If Lucas… weren’t hooked on the crap of his childhood… George Lucas is in the toy business.” Lucas: “My films are closer to amusement park rides than to a play or novel. You get in line for a second ride.” Slow $8 million opening weekend; Hawaii sandcastle lasts; $160 million in 1981, fourth all-time.

**Jan. 7, 1982, London, with five-month-old adopted daughter Amanda** (held her hours after birth; “just like a bolt of lightning”). “One [goal] was to be independent of Hollywood, the other was to have a family.” He will find it “harder and harder to make his family his priority. And by the time work on the film was complete, his private life would be in shambles.”

“George came and he never left.” Watts; Marquand “couldn’t grasp it.” Reynolds: Marquand was “intimidated by George.” Marquand: like “trying to direct *King Lear*—with Shakespeare in the next room.” Lucas: “ultimately I’ll have the final say.” Insists on seeing Marquand’s private shot list; imposes SW “documentary” multi-camera style. “I imposed my will a little bit.… I hadn’t realized that ultimately it’s probably easier for me to do these things than to farm them out.” Kurtz: “George harassed Richard Marquand into more or less doing what he wanted.”

**Ewoks:** sent back through design to a “cuddly teddy bear–like look.” “Dare to be cute. The worst we could do is get criticized for it.” McQuarrie throws up his hands; “maybe too obvious a marketing idea.” “George loved them. Nobody else did.” Signs eleven-year-old Warwick Davis (Wicket) for a fairy-tale idea that will become *Willow.* Locations under the cover title *Blue Harvest.* Yuma/Jabba’s skiff blown by dune-buggy fans. Sarlacc: Lucas bickers over cost; Fisher calls it “the sand vagina.” Crescent City, patience gone: “I’m not having fun.… The next trilogy will be all someone else’s vision.” (n.118) Principal photography mid-May 1982, on time and on budget; Lucas has lost twenty pounds.

**Marriage collapse, pp. 312–314 (n.120–128).** “It’s been very hard on Marcia, living with somebody who is constantly in agony.” Lippincott: George “would take problems to bed with him.” “For George Lucas, movies would always be *the other woman.*” Marcia: “the bottom line was just that he was all work and no play.… He was very emotionally blocked, incapable of sharing feelings. He wanted to stay on that workaholic track. The empire builder.” He claims the ranch is “just a giant facility to allow my wife to cut film in Marin County” — “too little, too late.” She finds warmth with Tom Rodrigues, nine years younger, hired for the ranch library’s stained-glass dome; she says she has not yet been unfaithful, suggests counseling and a trial separation; he refuses both (therapists “nearly as untrustworthy as Hollywood executives”). Authorizes Dale Pollock’s biography hoping it will remind her of better times; they attend his twentieth high-school reunion, switching name tags. She asks for a divorce; he begs her not to go public until after *Jedi.* She still edits *Jedi* with Dunham and Sean Barton, mostly what Lucas mockingly calls “the dying and crying.” They barely speak; Kazanjian has to ask her himself. Barton: she wants to recut a scene; Lucas snaps, “You can make it different, but you’re not going to make it better.” Marcia later: “I was the more emotional person who came from the heart and George was the more intellectual and visual… But George would never acknowledge that to me.… In his mind, I always stayed the stupid Valley girl. He never felt I had any talent.”

**Vader’s funeral pyre, p. 315 (n.129) — typography locked.** Dunham points out the current cut leaves Vader’s fate unclear: “We just left him there [on the Death Star] and we don’t see him again. Did he blow up in the Death Star or did Luke take him or what happened to him?” Kazanjian agrees audiences may not know whether Vader is actually dead. “George got pretty heated.… obviously that hit a nerve. I said, ‘Okay, sorry I asked.’” Lucas knows they are right: “Mark Hamill and a second-unit crew were quickly dispatched to Skywalker Ranch to film Luke setting fire to Vader’s funeral pyre.”

“Black Friday” at ILM: nearly a hundred effects shots discarded. Marcia, in tears, worries the deadline is keeping her awake; Lucas: “If she was breaking into tears, it probably had nothing to do with the movie.” He approves substandard effects under the deadline; frustrated with the sarlacc-pit battle (more edits than any other scene) and with Boba Fett. Dunham: “Boba didn’t measure up to some standard George had.” **“Just throw him in the pit,” sighed Lucas.** (n.133)

Meanwhile he writes a twenty-page *Indiana Jones and the Temple of Death* treatment (end of May 1982; later *Temple of Doom*); darker, “I wasn’t in a good mood.” Kasdan refuses: “It’s so mean.… I think *Temple of Doom* represents a chaotic period in both their lives.” Huycks write it fast so they don’t “lose” Spielberg. In-jokes (Short Round named for the Huycks’ dog); staff worry it’s too violent. Lucas joins the Sri Lanka shoot April 1983, films the rope-bridge collapse with a second unit; *Rolling Stone* finds him “so gloomy, so unhappy, so downright miserable.” Ralston: “totally fried.”

May 7 employee preview at the Coronet. He still regrets that the Ewoks couldn’t blink (fixed digitally decades later). **Renames the film at practically the last minute:** “*revenge* was a trait unbecoming in a Jedi knight,” substituted *return;* already-printed *Revenge* posters recalled and sold only to the Fan Club.

**Opens Wednesday, May 25, 1983, in 820 theaters** (vs 126 for *Empire*); $6.1 million opening day; break-even $115 million, recouped in less than five weeks, approaching $250 million by year-end. Critics split. *Time* cover; caution about effects and creatures — “a criticism that would be leveled against him and his three *Star Wars* prequels twenty years later.” Johnston: “We were never sure whether the movie was a vehicle for the effects or for the story.” **Kazanjian on Vietnam, p. 319 (n.142):** “You can look at *Jedi* and see the Vietnam War there. You can see the Ewok guerrillas hiding in the jungles, taking on this improper force of mechanized bullies—and winning.” John Simon on *Nightline*: “dehumanizing”; Siskel and Ebert tell him to lighten up.

Four days before release he leaves for two weeks in Honolulu. To *Rolling Stone:* “That dumb screenplay I first wrote ten years ago is at least finished.… there hasn’t been a day in my life where I haven’t gotten up in the morning and said, ‘Gee, I’ve got to worry about this movie.’ Literally. Not one single day.” To *Time:* burned out, “going on momentum,” “I’ve got to get my life back again—before it’s too late.”

**Monday, June 13, all-hands at Skywalker Ranch:** George and Marcia holding hands, tearfully announce the divorce. Staff stunned. Amanda, now two, remains largely in his custody. “I’ve got a daughter now… I don’t want her to grow up to be eighteen and have her say, ‘Hey Dad, where have you been all my life?’” Two-year sabbatical except *Temple of Doom* still shooting. “I put my companies and the making of movies and everything on the side.” Spielberg doesn’t believe he’ll retire. **Close, p. 321 (n.150):** “I look upon the three *Star Wars* films as chapters in one book. Now the book is finished, and I have put it on the shelf.” “As far as he was concerned, he was never taking that particular book down off the shelf again. *Not ever.*”
