## Opening (p. 28)

Lucas: “Basically, I would tell them about a scene, an animal, or a character, and they would do a bunch of designs. I would okay some or modify or change them, and we build; we go to the next thing. And so, every week, they get more to design. Some of these guys are designing costumes; some of them are designing sets; some of them are designing props or cars. There’s just a whole variety of guys up there, and each one does a different thing. And then I’m writing the script.”

Rinzler: “This is the big conceptual push. From mid-October 2002 to Day One of principal photography, life in the art department will hit its zenith in terms of quality, quantity, and hours.” Tiemens: “It’s like going to an art gallery opening every Friday. Here’s ninety-nine new paintings for George to look at.” Church: “It’s the most fun I’ve ever had. Monday or Tuesday, we might go home at seven or eight in the evening; Wednesday, one or two in the morning; Thursday—all night.” (parenthetical: “Fortunately for these acolytes, the kitchen often sends up its leftovers.”) Church: “That was the magic hour, Thursday at 3 A.M.—the three-to-five stretch is the intense, focused work period.” T. J.: “Here you’re working on something you loved as a kid. Everybody is supertalented and everyone has something to add to the mix.”

“As of October 18, 2003, three new artists have been added to this mix: Derek Thompson (creatures); Warren Fu (vehicles/environments); and Alexander Jaeger (environments), bringing their number to twelve.” The date is printed **2003** on a page whose meeting heading is Friday, October 18, **2002**. Record both; do not silently correct.

Fu: “I’d seen these *Art of Star Wars* books, but I’d never made the connection that you could actually make a living doing that kind of work. When I graduated from UC Berkeley, my friends told me about an internship at Lucasfilm. I didn’t even know how to make a portfolio, so I just kinda put everything together in a folder and submitted it late. We had to sneak into my friend’s office and use his mail machine to backdate it! I was rejected the first time, so this is icing on the cake being up here.”

McCaig describes the department as a “jazz ensemble” in which each artist instinctively knows when to “step forward and take a solo” and when to merge back into the group. “This is the biggest art department I’ve ever worked with. And everyone is confident enough in their abilities so that we can jam the way that musicians do—and that’s really rare.” McCallum: “We wanted to make the art department a collective unit—and I wanted the guys to have fun.”

Rinzler: “Throughout this period, the one common thought voiced by those in the ensemble is: *Wouldn’t it be cool if … ?*” Jaeger: “For the first part of the design process, it seemed like George was going from what we were drawing, rather than telling us what to do. A lot of what we did was what we’d really love to see in a *Star Wars* film. We’d draw it, present it, and see if he’d bite.” Church: “George was always appreciative, and might say, ‘Wow. Not for my movie, but that’s a great idea.’ We’d always try something out because—you’ve got George Lucas’s eye, and not many people have that chance.”

Caption, Fu, typography locked: “I was five years old when *The Empire Strikes Back* came out, and after I saw it, it was all over.”

### FRIDAY, OCTOBER 18, 2002

McCaig shows Lucas “two paintings on his computer screen: one of Anakin Skywalker emerging from a volcano, burned but still standing, and another of Anakin on the back of a lizardlike mount. The first prompts Lucas to say that Anakin will probably be carried out of the volcano; the second prompts him to say that the lizard can run up walls and ceilings, which will enable them to create unique fight sequences.” (sentence completes on p. 29)

Elstree art department “is now open for business.” Bocquet’s team has “already produced a skeletal life-sized construct of the Jedi starfighter.” Lucas coming up the stairway: “Fee, fi, fo, fum! Ready or not, here I come!” He sees the Elstree photos: “Already building sets? I may have to start writing the script pretty soon!” After reflection he limits the build: “For God’s sake, I don’t need all that—” as he spots Gregoire, “You can do it, can’t you?” *it* referring to “a digital extension of the practical cockpit.”

If there are “more than seventeen shots for any one environment, they’ll need to build a set of some sort.” Needed: Jedi Council, Palpatine’s office, “probably the Senate,” and “either to return to part of Padmé’s Episode II apartment or to create a new one.” He “suggests reviewing *Return of the Jedi* and taking a look at Coruscant’s plaza/park. Anakin and Padmé might have a love scene somewhere like that—but without greenery.”

Lucas is planning a lot of fighting there, about four to five scenes, and one ‘love scene.’”

## Opening rewritten; “gags”; blank “script” board (p. 30)

“He has also altered the beginning of the film. Instead of starting with seven battles on seven planets, Episode III now opens with a huge space battle. Our heroes crash-land into a ‘bad guy’s ship,’ perhaps Neimoidian, where there will be lots of ‘cliffhangers, from one near-death experience to another—*Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom* aboard a ship.’ The ship might be breaking apart. He asks the artists to work on a ‘grand control room and a grand office.’ The heroes will be trying to rescue a kidnapped character. For their escapades, he asks the artists to come up with dangerous situations, or what are generally referred to as ‘gags.’”

Tiemens: “Turbines?” Lucas: “Turbines are always good. Anything you don’t wanna get near.” “He then reveals that Anakin and Count Dooku will have a huge duel aboard the ship. The entire opening sequence should last about twenty minutes.”

Fay David brings a ship binder. Lucas: “We’re out of our minds,” “Making a movie where the bad guys win, and everyone dies, is not destined to be the most successful movie of all time.” He brakes on battle-scene key frames: “I haven’t gotten that far yet.” A blank board sits between paintings. McCallum: “It’s a copy of the script!”

Lucas prefers “the longer hair worn by Obi-Wan and Qui-Gon. ‘Anakin’s still a Jedi,’ he points out.” McCaig “mumbles something about Trisha needing to get started, and Lucas’s response is similarly indistinct.” Rick: “Is that a yes?” George: “I don’t know, I’m not sure what Iain asked. He’s mumbling.” McCallum is “ecstatic,” “for ‘production is finally beginning.’” “I knew I wasn’t going to get a script, but I had to keep them working. Without a script, our priority then becomes: We know Padmé’s in the film, we know she’s going to be in her bedroom, and we know it’s going to be night—so can we create a nightgown for her?”

## Handwritten Act I notes; “I’ve been thinking about it”; “We have an Emperor this time!” (p. 31)

Rob Coleman is on hand “for the first time.” “Indeed, when Lucas walks in and sees Coleman he does a double take, protesting, ‘What’s he doing here? No animation! You can’t come until June 24.’” Coleman: “I’ll see you at the film’s premiere.” Lucas “quickly offers that he may have to start writing ‘when reality hits.’” McCallum: “You said you’ve been writing.” Lucas: “I’ve been *thinking* about it.”

Story details: “the opening space battle will be fought over Coruscant. Anakin and Obi-Wan in their Jedi starfighters fly into a Separatist cruiser, then split up as the star cruiser slowly breaks in half. Obi-Wan ends up on the bridge after a series of episodic cliffhangers, and manages to land the front half of the ship. Meanwhile, Anakin fights Count Dooku.” “Anakin’s longer hair is approved.” A Mustafarian in red: “We have to be careful he doesn’t look like the Emperor.” “Somebody immediately connects the dots and says, ‘We have an Emperor this time!’”

### FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 8, 2002

Polis Mazta “conceptualized in three different ways across six paintings.” Church: Lucas “instructed them to create an asteroid wasteland, to consult 1960s NASA photos, and to include ‘space-suit guys’—a species that doesn’t have much in the way of faces, based on nonutilized Episode II designs for a Kamino species. He hasn’t specified yet whether they’ll be good guys or bad guys.” Caption: Natalie Portman, returning from *Cold Mountain* in Romania, stops at Elstree for a “full-body cast” / “custom-made dressmaker’s stand.”

## “Good toast factor”; droid general is “not a Sith” (p. 32)

Lucas on Mustafar paintings of Jedi near lava: “there is a certain reality about toast here—even in fantasy.” Thompson has Jedi “using the Force to hurl lava at each other.” Lucas: a “good toast factor.” Drawings of Jedi “being killed in combat on Mygeeto (crystal world), but Lucas objects—the Jedi will be ‘betrayed,’ not cut down in battle.”

“Lucas casually mentions that Episode III’s principal villain could be a Separatist droid general—and everyone begins to take notes.” Lucas: “I won’t limit it at this point to a droid. It could be an alien of some kind. I’m not sure if I want him to be human. It’s the Darth Maul. It’s the Jango Fett, Darth Vader.” Rick: “Male, though?” Lucas: “I don’t care. It’s gotta be iconic. It’s gotta be pretty evil.” McCaig: “Evil. Iconic. Got it.” Lucas: “It’s like, *Uh-oh! This is the bad guy.* Your first reaction is: *Scary.* But it also has to have a lot of personality and be very recognizable. He has to be able to do dialogue scenes.” McCaig: “This is not a Sith, right?” Lucas: “It’s not a Sith.” McCallum: “Age?” (continues p. 33)

## “Villains are ageless”; first general board (p. 33)

Lucas: “Villains are ageless. He can’t be young. Old people are scary. You can all get ahold of that and see if you can come up with a new iconic villain.” Rick pulls Iain aside: “Make that your number-one priority.” Lucas leaving: “Work on the villain. That’s the next real assignment. Now I have to go to a business meeting.” Pause. “In the real world.”

### FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 15, 2002

Two Separatist ship models: “How many cheers for this one?” Moderate. “And for this one?” Louder. “You see? It’s not hard to make decisions around here.” First “icon of evil” board. Thompson: “We all collaborated on the droid general. It was one of the most exhilarating weeks, because we all got to attack one thing.” Lucas is “quiet for about a minute,” then, “seemingly careful not to be discouraging, says, ‘The ones I like the most are . . . ,’ and stamps two for further research.” He doesn’t say anything negative, “except for ‘Too ethnic, too *Alien*-ish . . .’” McCaig: “Do you want to see more?” Lucas: “Some of this is a little too devilish.” Caption: “A Zhu painting of the duel on Mustafar puts into play Lucas’s desire to have the fight and its environements in close proximity to the lava at all times.” — printed typo *environements* locked as is.

## “Careful not to re-create Darth Vader”; Fu’s general (p. 34)

“Full figure?” “Could it be part droid?” Lucas: “We need to see more articulation, so I know exactly what I’m looking at. And we have to be careful not to re-create Darth Vader, since he’s showing up at the end of the movie.” Rinzler: “For those attending, this is a revelation—the first admission from Lucas that Darth Vader will definitely be in Episode III.”

### FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 22, 2002

Propane leak at the Ranch; artists evacuated; some “jogging the circuit” to keep warm. Smaller meeting. Admiral’s room “where Dooku confronts Anakin.” Lucas: “This is the place where Dooku meditates on his grand schemes,” so take out a column that would block his view of space. Church: “Could we do something that revolves?” Lucas: “It’s the executive suite. You gotta say to yourself, *If I had an office, this is what it’d look like.* Remember: We can’t go outside [into space]. Cinematically, it would be inappropriate. We have to stay inside the set.”

Felucia rethought from microphotography. “This is good. Excellent.” “If you go five miles, you run into this. This is odd. I like this. This is really odd. This is weird.” Two basic areas: “jungle and desert.”

Warren Fu’s last-minute general sketch: “Because my priority was environments, I didn’t focus on the droid general until the end of the week—at the last minute. The morning of the meeting, I sketched one, which I didn’t even like that much. But we put it out there, and George goes right up to it and says, ‘That’s the one.’ I mentioned that the general might have organic eyes—George’s eyes lit up and he said, ‘Now that’s interesting.’”

## “I just have to figure out a movie to go with it” (p. 35)

McCaig drawings “that suggest Padmé is sick while pregnant, and having terrible dreams.” Lucas: “That’s just because she was spending too much time with you!” Then: “She does have a thing where she gives birth . . .” Closing the meeting: “All right. Keep up the good work. The stuff was great, guys. I just have to figure out a movie to go with it.”

McCallum back from a three-week Sydney trip; meetings closed to “nonessential personnel.” Sculptor Michael Murnane is “the thirteenth member of the group.” Lucas to McCallum: “I haven’t seen you for a while—I didn’t even know you were alive.” Rick: “I’m not.” They try the binders again. Lucas: “Until I’ve started the script, it’s hard . . .” McCallum: “Oh, God . . .” Lucas: “Take some aspirin.” McCallum: “Aspirin? I need to freebase!” Lucas “loves” the lemur sculpts “and gives them all FABULOUSOS.” Egyptian influence on Polis Mazta: “Okay. I’ll buy that.” “The other film was low-key, twilight. Frame: “Working with George is a scary but exhilarating experience. He comes up here and asks, ‘Is it a good read?’ He keeps it pure and honest. He makes sure that design is in service of the storytelling.”

## December 13 story points; Christmas 2002 writing (p. 36)

Lucas: “Should I go for it?” Rick: “Go for it.” McCallum: “I haven’t figured out if there’s a correlation between more okays and not enough time, but you’re doing great.” Because of hints “that the script may be finished by Christmas,” McCaig: “We have two more weeks to be as outside the box as possible.”

### FRIDAY, DECEMBER 13, 2002

Rain; a leak in the art department; smaller group. Lucas has requested a copy of the binders; Fay David and Stephanie Lostimolo sent copies “to his home, where he is now reportedly working on the script Mondays through Thursdays.” He arrives early, approves two lizards (male and female), tells the doc team: “I snuck up here early. You missed the whole thing. A song, a dance . . .” Padmé dresses: avoid material “that won’t be good for hugs. ‘She’s a victim in this one, but interacts with Anakin a lot.’”

“Once again, without warning, he volunteers some key story points: ‘Mace we see more in the Jedi Temple—at this point, we don’t see him out in the field, though that’s subject to change. We see Bail Organa’s wife, the Queen of Alderaan. She gets one baby. On Utapo, I have a big battle now. It’s where Obi-Wan kills the [droid] general. So we’ll need his henchmen. I’m thinking of doing a droid-dragon chase. I don’t know how Obi-Wan is going to possibly win, but we’ll figure out how to get out of it later.’” Still *Utapo* at this date. “Because it’s a battle and Obi-Wan is chasing the general, the general will need a vehicle. It’s gotta be a big fight. Lasers versus swords; lizards versus droids. Lots of speed, lots of chasing—*lots* of speed—jumping from one vehicle to another. Lizards getting killed, falling off cliffs, things like that.”

McCallum asks which Padmé costumes Biggar can start. Lucas: “It’s too early. I have to get an outline together,” then: “She’s in her apartment twice; she’s in the Senate; she’s on the landing platform.”

### CHRISTMAS 2002

FOX STUDIOS, SYDNEY: McCallum, after knee surgery, arrives December 20; “production begins full-scale operations the day after Christmas.” Ramos: “All of those things need sorting out. He happens to be a really hands-on kind of producer.” McCallum: “What we’re doing is controlling chaos.” Communication “creates the missing foundation, ‘because we’re not going to find the more apparent foundation until we’re well into postproduction—which makes for an interesting paradox.’”

SKYWALKER RANCH: “It’s now the ninth month of preproduction.” Lucas: “Back in August, I started writing this, but the script starts to have its own life. Characters start to tell you what to do—and you end up with problems. By the third film, you have a lot of characters left over from before, and they’re all running around yipping and yelling and saying, ‘What about me?’ And you have to solve these problems, because what you thought was going to happen isn’t happening. I got far enough with the outline to realize that the bridge between Episode III and Episode IV still had them about fifty feet apart. So I had to disassemble Episode III and rethink it, to make it line up with Episode IV. When you actually put it down on paper and start doing it scene by scene—when you really start pulling it apart—you say, ‘Well, I have to have a through-line. And I have to stick with it.’”
