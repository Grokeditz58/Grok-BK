## PROLOGUE: The Crew (p. 66)

Heading typography, as printed: PROLOGUE: The Crew.

“Twenty-seven years separates these two photos, the first (right) taken on the set at EMI Studios in 1976 (Mark Hamill as Luke, Lucas, Carrie Fisher as Leia, and Harrison Ford as Han) and the second—designed to mirror it—in 2003 on the set in Sydney (Christensen, McCallum, Lucas, Portman, and McGregor).” Printed verb *separates* locked as is.

Lucas: “When I did the first *Star Wars*, I was confident primarily because of my inexperience. Now I’m confident because of my experience. When I did the first *Star Wars*, I was on my own in England, with a crew that was partly hostile, partly supportive. I made very close friends with the supportive part, and a lot of those guys went on to do the next two films. On these last three *Star Wars* films, I’ve been working with a group that I started with on *Young Indy*. So I have a lot of people who I’ve worked with for a long time—a talented, professional, experienced group—which makes everything easier, because even under the most stressful of times, this group is very good at working together.”

Core group: McCallum, Bocquet, Tattersall, Biggar, Ramos. Tattersall remembers that in 1992, shooting *Young Indy* at a chateau in France, “Lucas and McCallum were already talking of assembling essential personnel for the next *Star Wars* film.” Later additions from the Special Edition or *The Phantom Menace*: Coleman, Gillard, Knoll, Peter Russell, Ty Teiger, Jayne-Ann Tenggren, “and others.”

McCallum: “I really invest a lot of time trying to create the best atmosphere that I possibly can, especially in my relationship with the people I work with. We’ve spent almost a lifetime together. Kathryn and I just celebrated fifteen years working together—to me, our lives are intertwined.”

“The most sublime state of filmmaking is where everyone is interdependent. No one person on the film can let anyone else down, because it has a ripple effect on the whole picture. It’s incredibly collaborative—most people don’t realize how collaborative it is, how intensely personal, how emotionally draining. You wake up at four-thirty A.M. and you finish around ten P.M. If you’re not in the right physical and mental shape, you can go into a downward spiral.”

## Chapter opening: Sydney and the schedule (pp. 67–68)

Chapter title typography, as printed: Shooting a Foundation.

Lucas on Sydney: “It’s like San Francisco on steroids.” Rinzler: “Sydney was founded in 1778 as an English penal colony” (printed date locked as is; not silently corrected). Fox Studios will house “Episode III’s seventy-one sets.” The first film shot on the premises: *A Daughter of Australia*, “back in 1922”; stages and streets named for Australian film-industry luminaries (Chips Rafferty Lane, Hector Crawford Stage, Ken G. Hall Stage). “But nothing ever hit these premises like *Star Wars*.”

McCallum on choosing the studio and Kim Williams: “I was very reticent at first to shoot in a studio. I’d rather not have to worry about the film that’s already in being late, or the film coming in behind you. It forces you into a situation that I didn’t want our movie or George ever to be in. But after spending a week with Kim, I knew that he could deliver the studio—that it would be ready for us on time and that it was going to be a great place to work. He was such a decent man that I knew we’d have enough time on each side of our booking to be safe.”

Christensen: “I’ve been here since the beginning of May, getting in shape and learning the lightsaber fights.” McGregor: “Hayden was here much earlier than I was, but I had about three weeks of fighting, five days a week, for half-a-day at a time. It’s much harder work now. On Episode I, I’d fight all day long, whereas now I’m like, ‘Okay, guys, I think I’ve reached my limit.’ It’s repetition, repetition—but it’s got to become fluid.” McCallum: “I closed some of the actors’ deals just days before we started shooting. I just didn’t know how long we’d need them, until the script was done and the schedule set.”

Keepers of the shooting schedule: Lucas, McCallum, and 1st AD Colin Fletcher. Fletcher: “George answered all my questions. We had about an hour a day. A lot of directors hate going through the schedule because it means facing reality, but George would just go through it as many times as I wanted.” A technical read-through of the entire script is held in the cafeteria a few days ahead of Day One. Crew of “nearly a thousand,” “many of whom just finished working on the two *Matrix* sequels”; “a projected sixty-three-day shoot.” McCallum’s “overriding priority”: “There should be no hierarchy on the film, except that you have a writer-director who has an idea—and everything we do has to enable him to get to the purest state of realizing that idea. At the end of the day, that’s what filmmaking is.”

Lucas (p. 73): “I have a tendency to be a quiet person on the set. I don’t do a lot of yelling. I try to keep everybody happy and focused on what it is they’re supposed to be doing, which is a challenge because there’s always a lot of raw emotions on a movie set. But I’ve been doing this for so long that I’ve seen just about every crisis you can possibly imagine—and gotten through it.” “In working with the actors, I basically go over the scene with them and suggest what to do. They know beforehand where we’re going and what we’re shooting. It’s just a matter of getting that on tape. It’s the same thing with the cameramen. I just assume we won’t shoot till nine A.M., and then, after lunch, until three P.M., with forty-five minutes per setup. You have your wish list, then you have the realities.” Fletcher: “George sets himself challenges every day. What he’s going to do, where he’s going to be by lunchtime. He’s like . . . incredible.”

Video village: two Panasonic TH-42PHD5 monitors; two HDC-F950 Sony digital cameras; Fujinon lenses. Chris Neil, recommended by Francis Coppola: “The most challenging thing for me is coming in and trying to fit myself into the mix, to fill a role that hadn’t been created on the other two films. “It’s a very different suspense structure. Part of the fun for me was completely flipping upside down the dramatic track of the original movies. If you watch it the way it was released, IV, V, VI, I, II, III—you get one kind of movie. If you watch I through VI, you get a completely different movie. One or two generations have seen it one way, and the next generations will see it a completely different way.”

“It’s extremely modern, almost interactive moviemaking. You take blocks and move them around, and you come out with different emotional states.” LOCATION WRAP: 19:45. SETUPS: 21. SCRIPT SHOT TODAY: 2m 49s.

## SHOOT DAYS 14–15: Anakin becomes Darth Vader; Plagueis at the opera (pp. 93–94)

### SHOOT DAY 14: THURSDAY, JULY 17, 2003

Stage 7, Int. Palpatine’s office. SCENES SHOT: 99pt (Anakin becomes Darth Vader). VFX NOTES: Anakin cuts off Mace’s hand. Elsey puts “the final touches on Ian McDiarmid as Darth Sidious,” blue lights on his face “to simulate the blue lightning coming from his fingers, which will be added in postproduction.” Jackson, wearing glasses, “yells his lines from off camera over the noise of a wind machine.” Lucas to Christensen, watching the fight: “The thing to remember is there’s a huge display of craziness going on here.” Christensen: “As much as I’m watching what’s going on, I think I’m really searching myself to see what I’m going to do here.” “I’m listening to them but I’m also in my own head at the same time.” Lucas: “We still have to get the audience into what you’re thinking. When you get into these scenes, you have to be visually reacting, too.” Jackson, on Palpatine begging for help: “I’m bending those lightning bolts right back on him. I mean, that’s why he’s begging—because I got him. ‘Yeeeeah! Who’s your daddy now?!’”

“In the next scene, Anakin becomes Darth Vader. Christensen kneels before McDiarmid, rehearsing his dialogue to himself.” Christensen: “Anakin’s relationship with Palpatine eclipses his relationship with Obi-Wan. But he doesn’t really have a clear devotion to one or the other. Anakin as he will be played is—I don’t want to say naïve, but his belief system is still open. He isn’t really exactly set in his devotion to the Jedi or to Palpatine. He is looking to see how he can get more power, but his ideas of good and evil are not black and white.” “And . . . action!” “After a few takes, with Hayden kneeling among a dozen crew, Anakin’s transition to the dark side is complete.” LOCATION WRAP: 20:30. SETUPS: 42. SCRIPT SHOT TODAY: 4m 31s. NOTES: McDiarmid “had suffered a considerable amount of discomfort with his contact lenses”; nurse Helen Johnstone “gave him an eye bath and checked for any damage.” Caption, Elsey: “I love the Emperor. Re-creating that makeup was both a challenge and an honor.”

### SHOOT DAY 15: FRIDAY, JULY 18, 2003

Stages 7 & 4. Int. Palpatine’s private office; Int. Coruscant ballet. SCENES SHOT: 99 (Palpatine fights with Mace Windu), 67pt (Palpatine tells Anakin the Jedi Council wants to control the Republic). Lucas to Fletcher: “If we can get through this day, we’ll be okay.” On the third day of the Mace–Palpatine fight, wires attach Jackson so stuntmen can pull him “backward to simulate Windu being pummeled by a particularly vicious blast of dark side lightning.” Lucas to Jackson: “Your first reaction is surprise—your hand’s been cut off—and then you’re blasted away.” After a rehearsal, Jackson is “floating above the set, pulled there by wires yanked by stuntmen jumping off a ladder.” Jackson: “It’s almost like I’ve practiced for this death all my life. Last summer, when I was in San Francisco doing another film [*Twisted*, 2004], George and I had lunch and discussed what was going to happen in this film. And he said, ‘Well you’re pretty much the only important character that I can actually kill off, so it has to be a pretty spectacular death.’” For close-ups of Anakin cutting off Mace’s hand, Lucas to Christensen: “It’s good if it’s a clean move,” then the scene “will go quiet afterward, with ‘low strings and scary music.’”

Next: the opera, “where Palpatine continues his seduction of Anakin, recounting the story of Darth Plagueis.” Lucas to McDiarmid: “A little political plotting at the ballet is just the thing after a hard day’s sword-fighting.” Makeup artists touch up Sandi Finlay (Sly Moore), David Bowers (Mas Amedda), and McDiarmid, “who are seated, staring out at a performance that doesn’t yet exist.” Lucas: “It’ll take us six months to figure out what they’re watching.” “Ian then recounts to Anakin the story of Plagueis several times in several setups, each delivery flawless and chilling.” Christensen: “Scenes where Ian did most of the talking, I just had to sit there and listen. I literally just sort of nod my head and agree with the story he’s telling me—and those scenes were my favorites, because he’s an amazing storyteller. I was so in awe of what he was doing in each scene, I’d find myself at times almost breaking out of character.” McDiarmid remembers a Dundee variety show at age four, “rather scary and, at the same time, seductive.” Lucas, wrapping: “That’s a wrap.” To McDiarmid: “That was amazing, absolutely amazing. Everyone was glued to the monitors.” Daniels arrives with birthday cake for Fletcher; Fletcher: “I’m blown over.” Lucas to Ian: “You’ve got one more fight with a little green frog, but that shouldn’t be too hard.” LOCATION WRAP: 20:45. SETUPS: 40. SCRIPT SHOT TODAY: 6m 45s. He’s diabolical in every sense . . . his daughter kills him by driving nails through his eyes.’”
