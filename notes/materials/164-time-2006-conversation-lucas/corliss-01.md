## Corliss introduction (TIME PDF pp. 1–3)

- "Movie history can be divided, without much forcing of the issue, into two eras: before Star Wars and after." Pre-1977 Hollywood: adult American cinema, few "box office megamillions." Popular films made "for everyone" (*The Love Bug*, *Airport*, *The Poseidon Adventure*, *The Godfather*, *The Sting*, and *Jaws*). Science-fiction was "kids' stuff"; even *Jaws* relied on a shark "that kept short-circuiting off the coast of Martha's Vineyard waters."
- "*Star Wars* changed everything." It "quickly became the top-grossing movie in the 65-year history of feature films (replacing *The Sound of Music*)"; "the first cult-movie megahit"; merchandising "that equaled, and then surpassed, the Disney cartoon features" ("That revenue, not Lucas' share of the film's take, was what made him a billionaire."); "the first Hollywood epic, at least so far as I know, that was conceived as a trilogy." And, "as Lucas mentioned in an interview I had with him two weeks ago," *Star Wars* was "one of the hits whose profits, shared by the theater owners, financed the multiplexing of America."
- Corliss typo: in 1971 Lucas "hatched the stainless-steel-cool, THX138." Warners' "bafflement and meddling" "stirred in Lucas a resolve to be a truly independent filmmaker." *American Graffiti* (1973). Out of *Star Wars* came ILM, THX, "and a little studio, specializing in digital animation, that became Pixar. (Lucas sold that one to fellow visionary capitalist Steve Jobs.)"
- **Corliss, not Lucas, on extending the saga (TIME PDF pp. 2–3):** "Most surprising, perhaps, was Lucas' fidelity to the fantasy world he'd dreamed up. He could have gone back, or on, to making the gnarly little independent movies he has talked about, to increasingly incredulous listeners, for 35 years. Yet he has extended the original Star Wars trilogy not just to the three episodes he made in the past decade but to an Ewoks TV show and, now, a Star Wars cartoon series for cable TV and a rerelease of all six chapters in 3D. He is a father who feels obliged to raise the children he sired. Luke and Anakin... ...and Indiana Jones, another Saturday matinee hero, the icon of the three-film collaboration with Spielberg that promises another installment one of these years." Also a DVD release of *The Young Indiana Jones* TV series, "with a one-hour documentary on the subject of each episode."
- At 61, "Lucas refers to himself as 'retired'—meaning, he explains, he has no more Star Wars movies to make." The pieces that follow are "excerpts from our two-hour phone conversation." Some minutes were lost on both recorders (Corliss's and Lynne Hale's, Lucasfilm Director of Communications). "I'm amused that neither the world's largest media company nor the galaxy's preeminent group of movie futurists could pull off recording the entire conversation." Corliss's sign-off for the intro: "a peek into the mind of Lucas Skywalker. —R..C." (double period in this reprint).

## HOW BIG MOVIES MAKE LITTLE MOVIES (TIME PDF p. 3)

- **Lucas:** "It was the money from Star Wars and Jaws that allowed the theaters to build their multiplexes, which allowed an opening up of screens. The money that Star Wars made, half of it goes to the theater owners. The theater owners said, 'Let's do some expansion. Let's build this idea of a multiplex,' which was sort of floating around. So they started building multiplexes, they had all these screens, they needed to fill them. So all the little Miramaxes came up and said, 'We'll help you fill those.'" Studios then started "a little company that does nothing but distribute little movies."

## THE DOYEN OF DIGITAL (TIME PDF pp. 3–4)

- Corliss (as interviewer): digital will make cinema "more democratic," "more like literature or painting."
- **Lucas:** "The problem is, Making a big movie, a Harry Potter or a Spider-man, you're spending $20 to $30 million for the prints, just to strike them and ship them to the theaters." If you don't have to spend money on prints, "You can go directly to the theater and say 'Hey I got a movie. Will you book this for three weeks?'"
- Conversion scheme: "It costs about $1,200 for a print and about $200 for a digital print." Charge the distributor the same $1,200, put $1,000 "into a pot that eventually pays for all the projectors"; "In about five years you would basically reconvert the entire industry." "No one's bought in yet. But they will. It's just a matter of time."
- On studio/exhibitor delay: "This is a new world; it doesn't work the way the old world worked." "One of the real problems has been that everybody's trying to figure out a way to control everything." "It has to do with trying to be greedy and control it." "it's one of the problems we have in this system we've created called win at all costs. And the idea of cooperating and everybody wins is not in the cards." (A speaker label "R.L." appears once in this section; the interviewer is Corliss.)

## DAY AND DATE (TIME PDF p. 5)

- **Lucas:** Day-and-date "is inevitable. It doesn't have anything to do with DVDs, it has to do with online." "that's the only way you're going to beat pirates, that is the only way because you can already download anything you want and it goes to the pirates instantly. The day it's released." Why go to theaters: "Because it's a social experience." Football-in-the-cold analogy: "it doesn't effect stadium attendance at all" ("effect" as printed). "If you haven't built a fan-base or you're not selling something that people want, then the attendance is going to drop. But if you have a good product that you're putting into the theater, then they're going to always go there."

## BIG SCREEN, LITTLE SCREEN (TIME PDF pp. 5–6)

- **Lucas:** "I compose it for the big screen, I don't worry about the little screen. Even when we did our TV series (Young Indiana Jones), I said, 'Make this as if it were going into a big theater', because I knew that eventually the screens would be rather large." "Yeah, you don't get the full scope of Lawrence of Arabia on a small screen. But you still understand it. And it's still just as emotional."
- "I am a giant proponent of giant screens. But I accept the fact that most of my movies are going to be seen on phones. Because that's what's going to happen. People can get whatever they want out of it on a phone. If they do, then that's great. I don't recommend it, but I certainly don't say don't do that." "if you really love films... there's a huge difference between watching something on a small screen with a mediocre sound system and watching it on a giant screen in a giant theater with a huge beautiful sound system. I mean the difference is electric."

## 2D, 3D (TIME PDF p. 6)

- Pixar films "made brilliantly creative movies, but they looked different." "When you see a 3D movie, you assume it's a higher-quality movie and it's something you don't see on television. Now the television show I'm working on, the Star Wars television show, is 3D." He means "the old-fashioned 3D," "with glasses and everything." He "had no idea" while making the movies. "it used to be a cheap trick... Now it's a movie, but it happens to be in 3D." "I was very much against 3D until I saw this new process and said, hey, this actually works in a way that it should work, which is it doesn't call attention to itself, you forget that you're watching in 3D, it's just a nicer process." "now they've got better glasses."

## RENEGADE AND RETRO (TIME PDF pp. 6–8)

- Corliss tries the thesis that *Star Wars* was "pioneering in its technology but retro in its content" and "validate[d] the Saturday-matinee impulse," leaving "a lot fewer exciting films by the best filmmakers that were made for adults."
- **Lucas:** "Well, I don't agree with that. Because if you look at the Academy Awards, and the top ten lists of the critics, every single year there are some amazing, artistic adult movies." Experimentation in form "happened in the 20s with Eisenstein"; in the 1960s "especially here in San Francisco with Bruce Connor and all those guys. But it was too far out of the mainstream." "to do that, you can't be too experimental, because most people aren't going to be attracted to that. They're going to be attracted to storytelling—storytelling in a way that they're used to."
- "The area I'm interested in now is to go do some form-experimenting—to try and figure out different ways of telling movies. I grew up in the Godard, Fellini world and all that. To me that's where my heart is. But I realize that's not commercial. That's why I can say I managed to do something that everybody wants to do—all those guys wanted to do—which was to get a pile of money so I can sort of waste it, burn through it. It's like a government subsidy... I have my own little government subsidy that I've built myself, and now I can go and do stupid things with it. I mean, I'm old enough and I'm kind of retired..."
- **Retired, TIME PDF p. 7 — key line:** Corliss: "What do you mean, you're kind of retired?" Lucas: "In that I don't have to do Star Wars anymore. I don't have to make money any more. I can just waste it. I call it hobby filmmaking, where you just get to do what you want to do, and you don't have to worry about what anyone thinks about it." After the TV series, "I'm going to do my own little movies." "Basically, you have to accept the fact that it's going to be the land of THX (the movie), and worse." He ruminates, paints a little, will make "reasonably low-budget movies" and try ideas "when I started." "I'm more interested in the avant-garde underground kind of moviemaking." "the average movie costs $80 million, anything under $20 million is pretty cheap. Anything under $10 million is almost impossible. And anything under $5 million is Roger Corman."

## INDY 4 (TIME PDF p. 8)

- **Lucas:** "Well, I've been working on Indy 4 for ten years. So I've been more involved, so no matter how you count it on this one I'll be more involved than I'll have ever been on the other three put together. It's taken forever to get a script of it. That's my part of it." The thing "is designed for" an older Harrison Ford. "the problem there, which is not a problem, is that we don't have to make that movie. All we can do is hurt ourselves, all it's going to do is get criticized. I mean it's basically Phantom Menace we're making. No matter how you do it, no matter what you do, it won't be what the other ones were in terms of the impact or the way people remember them." "We just had a great time making those movies. And if we can have a great time doing this one and we can enjoy ourselves, and make something that's entertaining to us, no matter what the world thinks, let's just do it."
- Film vs. digital: Spielberg "will win. He's the director." "If he he wants to shoot it on film and cut it on a Movieola... Hey, he's got a great editor. Michael Kahn can cut faster on a Movieola than anybody can cut on an Avid." ("he he" and "Movieola" as printed.) "he has relented after all these years to maybe cutting it digitally. We'll see what happens."

## FAVORITE TOYS (TIME PDF pp. 9–10)

- Next home gizmo: "larger servers, storage units... like a safe." Downloaded quality "It's actually better" than DVD; "that's where Hi-Def is going to run into problems" versus "Blue Ray" (as printed).
- ILM, THX, and the Pixar-era companies "were designed really to make the process of making films easier and at the same time make the quality higher." "ILM was there because there were no real special effects companies at the time."
- Pre-vis: "It's very quick, almost like a video game: you can make movies very quickly and shoot them and them put them together. It's just basically a moving storyboard." "Directors can just sit down like a writer and direct their movie on a desk." Studios might abuse it, "But if you ignore the essential problem of the corporate overlords, it's actually a great way to make movies, because you can really see what your movie is, structurally, before you go out and shoot it." Lucas "come[s] out of documentary filmmaking"; Spielberg "conceptualizes and storyboards it and shoots exactly what he needs. But I have to see it moving."
- **"Sith" as a film title only, TIME PDF p. 10:** "I let him do some pre-vis on the last film, on Sith. And he loved it, and he did it on War of the Worlds. So he has accepted that part now because it's so much better." A simpler digital editing interface is also in the works, "the kind of interface we had on edit-to-write."
- Closing Lucas line: "Unfortunately, we live in a new world where all the fun things are gone. Everything is virtual."
- Corliss thanks Clayton Neuman, "who quickly and accurately transcribed the long conversation. He's the Han Solo of this journalistic adventure. —R.C."
