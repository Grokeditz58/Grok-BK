# Shields, *George Lucas* (2002): notes part 3 — chs. 7–8 and back matter (pp. 71–93 plus chronology through jacket)

Source: owner's MEGA PDF `UWkUVT6Q`. Citations are printed page numbers.

## Ch. 7: George Lucas, the Industry (pp. 71–83)

- Epigraph, Lucas: "I've never focused on making the money. I've always focused on not losing the money."
- *Return of the Jedi* (1983) "brought to an end an important period." That year he filed for divorce from Marcia "after 18 years" (they married 22 February 1969; this is about 14 years — a slip). "Eventually sharing joint custody of their three adopted children" — only Amanda (1981) was adopted during the marriage; Katie and Jett came later. 1984: he announces he will step down from actively managing Lucasfilm for two years. **Lucas, p. 72:** "I've put up with Star Wars taking over, pushing itself into first position, for too long." He tells friends he wants more time with his children. John Baxter: managers asked for authority to direct company films; "Lucas told them there was room for only one filmmaker in his organization."
- Skywalker Ranch in Marin: 150,000-square-foot post-production and music facility, R&D offices. Divorce finalized 1985, "$100 million" to Marcia (Shields's figure). ILM computer division splits into Lucasfilm Games and Pixar (1983 in Shields's telling). Pixar sold to "Steven Jobs" in 1985 for $10 million; Lucas "decided he wouldn't make a computer-generated Star Wars." **Lucas, p. 73:** "ILM is exactly like Pixar, except we don't do animated movies. We do animated pieces in feature films. I didn't need two companies that were doing the same thing." Shields dates *Toy Story* to 1997 (the film is 1995) and says Lucas later had to buy Renderman from Pixar. THX System pioneered for the *Jedi* premiere in two special theaters, 1983; Home THX research 1986.
- Company drift: toy sales $150 million (1981) to $50 million (1986); 800 employees; Ranch "$20 million a year." Lucas returns in 1986. **Lucas, p. 73:** "I'd go off to make movies and come back two years later and find everybody had hired an assistant." Doug Norby becomes president; layoffs (25 of 450 on-site at the Ranch; payroll down to 400).
- *Captain Eo* (1986, Coppola, Michael Jackson); *Star Tours*. Michael Cloply in the *Wall Street Journal*: Lucas as an "un-businesslike businessman," a "cult of personality that threatens to stifle creativity and excellence." *Howard the Duck* (1986) a bomb; *Willow* (1988) "fared little better." Michael McAllister (ILM): if *Willow* had been someone else's movie, Lucasfilm would never have signed on, "But this was for the boss and the boss gets to work however he wants" (p. 75). Morphing; Scott Ross urged a patent; "He still slaps his forehead talking about the morph phenomenon," Ross in 2001 — ILM "missed the market opportunity," and someone else's morphing program "sells off-the-shelf for $95."
- ILM triples under Ross (*Roger Rabbit*, *The Abyss*, *Last Crusade*). Ross wants in-house original films; "sources say" he met Lucas only "half-a-dozen times." Lucasfilm Commercial Productions becomes the most profitable division. *Last Crusade* "$100 million… in only two weeks" (1989) spurs the George Lucas Educational Foundation and *Young Indiana Jones* (Lawrence, Freud, Picasso, Archduke Ferdinand; study guides; 117 weeks, 20+ countries, 15,000 camera set-ups, 10,000 schools). The series "never caught on": it "couldn't commit historical inaccuracies" and real historical figures didn't really interact with Indy, who becomes "a bystander" (p. 77).
- John "Lassetter" (Lasseter): "You'd kill yourself on effects, but no one remembered the films." Ross quits; his name is "mentioned only once" in the company history. Norby and other executives quit in 1992. Irving Thalberg Award, 1992, presented by Spielberg.
- Seclusion at the Ranch. Reagan had requested a tour; Lucas, "angry that he had been legally unable to prevent Reagan from referring to his Strategic Missile Defensive Initiative in speeches as “Star Wars,” refused a visit" (pp. 77–78). Not directed since 1976. *Radioland Murders* (1994) flops. *American Graffiti* named to the National Film Registry, 1995.
- Digital technology as a rescue from "the kinds of dreadful personal experiences he suffered directing the first Star Wars episode in Tunisia." **Lucas, p. 79:** "Digital technology is the same revolution as addiqg sound to pictures and the same revolution as adding color to pictures." ("addiqg" is a PDF typo.) 1997 MTV Lifetime Achievement Award to a Chewbacca-costumed actor, presented by Carrie Fisher, is framed as "closing the door on that kind of filmmaking." Shields then says "*Star Wars: Episode I: The Phantom Menace* went into production" that year. Special Edition trilogy / 1998 remaster; AFI 100 includes *Star Wars*, *Raiders*, and *American Graffiti*. 30,000 fans in Denver, 1999, Official Fan Club Celebration. *TPM* as "one of the last of Lucas' movies to employ film."
- *Attack of the Clones* (2002): "all-pixel, “film free” production with real actors." **Lucas, p. 81:** "For the cast, it didn't make much difference except that they didn't get interrupted as often. You don't want a lot of time between takes; if you have to stop to reload the camera, it has a tendency to dissipate a lot of the energy that an actor's developing to get the performance right. [For the crew] the biggest issue was that we could see dailies while we were shooting." On digital projectors and THX: "But there's no way that we're going to threaten the movie theaters. That would be suicidal."
- Ranch described at length: 4,700 acres, "$200 million" poured in; stables, softball, three restaurants, vineyard, fire department; LucasArts, Lucas Digital (ILM + Skywalker Sound), Lucas Licensing, Lucas Learning. Not visible from the road; no photographs; Victorian ranch house; staircase redone three times; library under a 40-foot stained-glass dome; solarium; basement screening room (15 recliners, burgundy fabric, THX). Guest cottages named for Welles, Huston, Eisenstein. Planned move of several companies to the Presidio: "$250 million, 1,500-employee corporate campus," museum, seven-acre "Great Lawn." Added Ranch construction listed (archives building, digital film production facility, day-care, etc.).

## Ch. 8: The Future of Filmmaking (pp. 85–93)

- Epigraph, Lucas: "I think I've made some changes in the way the film industry works, and I think there will be more dramatic changes to come. I'm enthusiastic about that and I have a feeling that will be part of my legacy."
- Digital is "the big move." **Lucas to the Academy of Achievement, pp. 85–86:** "I think crossing into the digital age is the big move for the industry. I think it will be the biggest thing that's happened while I've been making movies ... I don't see any other major technical process coming along and changing that." **To *Time*, p. 86:** "They're just very cautious; they'll get around to it in time. It's happening whether anybody likes it or not. I have some friends, like Marty [Scorsese] and Steven [Spielberg], and they're not going to change over. They love film. I think Jim Cameron and Francis [Ford Coppola] changed over. It's like the beginning of color. Some people still wanted to use black and white, and that's great. It's an addition to what's already existing."
- Freer artistic expression: "So now you can tell much more interesting stories and you can express yourself more clearly… that's why all artists are constantly pushing the technology, the medium—to widen the range, so that they can use their imagination" (p. 86). Live actors "will never be completely eliminated," but more realistic aliens "encouraged him to resume the Star Wars saga." **Lucas, p. 86, on *TPM* creatures:** "I've gotten much better performances out of my aliens this time." Practical benefits: no reload waits, instant review instead of evening dailies.
- Democratization / the Internet. **Lucas, pp. 87–88:** "I think there are going to be some social changes that take place due to the Internet, and the availability of the tools to more and more people. I think you are going to find a lot of people recutting movies and changing them, making them into their own movies, things that are hard to contemplate at this point. And there will be delivery systems that are way, way different." "I think the biggest issue is going to be to be how the movies get into the marketplace and what happens to them once they are there… I don't think it's going to be a ‘sit down, hands off' situation anymore. I think it's going to be people sort of reinventing the movies once they're out there. How this works for the artist, I don't know. And what it does to the marketplace, I don't know. We're living in very exciting times and I look forward to seeing how this whole thing evolves." ILM once "charged $25 million per film"; *The American President* House of Representatives example; hope that "two filmmakers in a garage" can make a feature "for just few thousand dollars."
- **Lucas, p. 88**, denying that this is just love of tech: "I know I have a reputation for being this technical guy, but I'm not. All I know is I need to tell a story, and I'm most interested in quality ... so the audience can enjoy the film the same way we do when we sit in the answer print screening and see it under the most prime conditions. The idea in digital projection is that you get a high quality image for the run of the film." "The craft of movies is almost completely technical, as opposed to writing a book or something, which is only partially technical . . . whether we add sound, or whether we add color, or whether we use digital technology, is simply a way of broadening the canvas, so that we have more colors to work with" (pp. 88–90).
- Independence. **Lucas, long quotation, p. 90:** "For me it's very important. Most creative people don't like others looking over their shoulder saying, ‘Why don't you make that green? Why don't you make that blue? Why are you doing this?' .... So you try to get yourself into a situation where you only have to answer to yourself… There's nothing worse than the frustration of having somebody who doesn't get what you're doing trying to turn it into something else. It's a very frustrating thing and I never wanted to go through it. . . .That attitude comes out of film school, I think, where the primacy of the creative process in making a film is what you live for. It's not a business. It's trying to create something interesting that you're proud of, and try out creative ideas that may seem really off the wall, may work or may not work."
- **Lucas, pp. 90–91:** "Sometimes people are surprised to learn that most of the films I've made don't work… They ve been released but nobody has ever seen them. Maybe 40 percent of them are very successful. When my films that don't work it's usually because I tried some very experimental idea. I tried new ideas and they just didn't work, as opposed to trying to do something conventional and having it be so conventional nobody wanted to see it." ("They ve" is a dropped apostrophe in the PDF.)
- To film students, p. 91: "I want to tell film students: ‘If you've gone into films to make money, fame and fortune, just forget it! The odds are way against you. Go into the stock market! Go into something else. You're never going to find money in the film business. Because it's too hard' . . . I've got just about as much money as you can get, and I was just as happy 30 years ago. As long as I could make a movie, I was happy. And as long as I still can make movies, I'll still be happy."
- Time closing in, p. 91: "[F]or me it's just a matter of ‘How can I get through all the stories in the amount of time I have left?' My dream is that I get to do it. That was my dream when I was younger, too. ‘Will I get to make the movies? Will I get to do what I want?'"
- Instincts, pp. 91–92: "I've always just followed my own course, whatever I found the most interesting to me at the moment. I've never had a real plan of, ‘I want to get from here to there, and I've got to do this.' The underlying plan to everything is, ‘I've got a bunch of movies to tell, and this is the one I'm going to do now, and this is the one I'm going to do next.' And then I focus on the one at hand."
- Proud that he "helped move cinema from a chemical-based medium to a digital-based medium" (p. 92). History will decide which works last: "You may make something you don't think is very important during your lifetime and it'll last for a thousand years. You can't really focus too much on that part of it, because you don't know what history is going to throw at you in terms of what's important and what's not important" (p. 93). The chapter epigraph is restated as the closer.

## Chronology, filmography, bibliography, websites, index, jacket

- Chronology (pp. 94–95): 14 May 1944 birth; 1962 crash three days before graduation; 1964 Modesto JC AA; 1966 USC shorts / *Electronic Labyrinth: THX-1138: 4EB* first prize at the 1967–68 National Student Film Festival; 1966 BFA; 1967 Warners scholarship; 1969 Zoetrope, begins *THX*, marries Marcia 22 February; 1971 Lucasfilm incorporated, *THX* 11 March; 1973 *Graffiti* 2 August; 1974 begins writing *Star Wars*; 1975 ILM and Sprocket Systems; 1977 *Star Wars* 25 May; 1980 *Empire* 21 May, Ranch construction begins; 1981 *Raiders*; 1983 *Jedi* 25 May, divorces Marcia; 1984 *Temple of Doom*; 1986 *Captain Eo*, sells Pixar, co-produces *Labyrinth*; 1988 *Willow* and *Tucker*; 1989 *Last Crusade*, LucasArts; 1992 Thalberg; 1997 Special Editions; 1999 *TPM* 19 May; 2002 *Attack of the Clones*.
- Filmography (pp. 96–97) includes *The Rain People* (1969), *Kagemusha* (1980), *Body Heat* (1981), *Twice Upon a Time* (1983), Ewok TV movies and series, *Droids*, *Labyrinth*, *Captain Eo*, *Howard the Duck*, *Star Tours*, *Tucker*, *The Land Before Time*, *Willow*, *Young Indiana Jones*, *Radioland Murders*, and the saga films through *Attack of the Clones*. Credit roles are not itemized (producer vs. director vs. executive producer).
- Bibliography (p. 98): Baxter *Mythmaker* (1999); Brosnan *The Primal Screen*; Champlin *The Creative Impulse* (1992); Goldberg et al.; Henderson *The Magic of Myth* (1997); Jenkins *Empire Building* (1997/1999); Pollock *Skywalking* (1983); Salewicz *George Lucas: The Making of His Movies* (1998); Dana White A&E Biography (1999); Andrew Woog, Lucent (2000). Several of these are also on the MEGA Lucas-material catalog as later to-reads.
- Websites (p. 99): Academy of Achievement; ILM; Skywalker Sound; starwars.com; E! Online fact sheet (URLs as of 2002).
- Index (pp. 100–102): "the Force" is indexed to p. 21. Darth Vader is not given his own heading in the index (he is discussed in the Star Wars entries). Slip: Alec Guinness is listed under *American Graffiti* as well as *Star Wars*.
- Picture credits p. 103 area; about the author: Charles J. Shields, 12 Chelsea House books, former English-department chair at Homewood-Flossmoor High School, Illinois.
- Jacket: "George Lucas wanted to be a racecar driver when he was a teenager, but a terrible car accident made him rethink his dreams…" Mentions the "ruined" serious-filmmaking charge and "no denying Lucas' huge impact." ISBN 0-7910-6712-2.
