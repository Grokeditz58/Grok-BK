# Shields, *George Lucas* (2002): notes part 2 — chs. 4–6 (pp. 33–69)

Source: owner's MEGA PDF `UWkUVT6Q`. Citations are printed page numbers.

## Ch. 4: American Zoetrope and THX-1138 (pp. 33–43)

- Epigraph, Lucas on *THX-1138*: "[THX-l 138] was a very personal kind of film and I didn't think [Warner Brothers] had the right to come in and just arbitrarily chop it up at their own whim."
- 23, fresh out of school; Warners floundering after Jack Warner left; *Finian's Rainbow* the only picture on the lot; Coppola only four years older. Contrast of temperaments. Coppola wants to be an *auteur*. They "shared the same vision" of independence (p. 35). Coppola promotes Lucas from gofer to director's assistant; promises Warners backing for a feature *THX* if Lucas helps on *The Rain People*.
- Crew complaints about Lucas's aloofness on *Finian's Rainbow*. Coppola: "You gotta learn how to do this" — interacting with the crew (p. 36).
- Europe equipment-buying spree; Lucas's "pleas to slow down" ignored (pp. 36–38). Warehouse in San Francisco; American Zoetrope named for an early motion-picture device. Lucas marries Marcia Griffin "on February 22" (year given in the chronology as 1969).
- Lucas as a technician on the Maysles' *Gimme Shelter* (1970); Shields says he "kept the cameras rolling" during the Altamont killing (p. 38).
- Feature *THX-1138*: Duvall as THX; plot summary (sex outlawed, sedatives, LUH pregnant, White Limbo, escape to the surface). **Lucas, p. 39:** "I liked non-story, non-character tone poems that were being done in San Francisco at that time." Spielberg on Lucas's cutting: "George makes his visuals come to life with montage. That makes him unique in our generation, since most of us do it instead with composition and camera placement." 40-day Bay Area shoot; "entire Technicolor film for only $777,000."
- Warners outraged; recut over Lucas's protests; little fanfare, 1971. The epigraph recut quote is restated in the body, p. 40, without the bracketed studio name: "It was a very personal kind of film and I didn't think they had the right to come in and just arbitrarily chop it up at their own whim."
- Shields's "blueprint" reading of later work (pp. 40–42): (1) escape from repression through responsibility; humanity and machines; hard work — "one of Lucas's personal mottoes is “Talent without hard work doesn't get you very far.”" (2) good vs. evil / Western coloring: "black-armored Darth Vader confronts Luke Skywalker and Princess Leia, both garbed in white"; THX defies a malevolent government as Luke's companions take on the Empire. (3) optimism. Charles Champlin, *George Lucas: The Creative Impulse*, is quoted on "the essential warmth of [Lucas'] prevailing view." **Lucas, pp. 41–42:** "If we're not careful about the way we conduct our lives, we're going to live in a society devoid of pain and risk, but insulated, emotionless, and much more unpleasant." Sunrise as freedom; Lucasfilm, Ltd. incorporated as a "statement." Zoetrope collapses; Warners pulls financing and leaves Coppola with a $300,000 personal debt; Coppola takes *The Godfather*. Lucas turns toward a more mainstream picture: *American Graffiti*.

## Ch. 5: American Graffiti (pp. 45–53)

- Epigraph, Lucas: "[American Graffiti] was about my life as I grew up, so I cared about it a lot."
- 1971 context (Vietnam ending, nostalgia). Treatment: **Lucas, p. 46:** "I've always been interested in that theme of leaving an environment or facing change, and how kids do it, and that would be at the heart of the movie." Four boys choosing dependence on the past vs. adult responsibility. Money sought in Europe / Cannes. "my ‘I don't want to be a writer syndrome'" (p. 47). Friends who had said they'd write the script are busy; a producer takes the raised money and delivers a screenplay Lucas rejects: "the screenplay was completely different from the story treatment. It was more like ‘hotrods to hell.' It was very fantasy-like, with playing chicken and things that kids didn't really do. I wanted something that was more like the way I grew up."
- He writes it himself as the first start-to-finish Lucasfilm picture. Long **Academy of Achievement** quotation, pp. 48–49, on "a very dark period": offers of "a lot of money" for "terrible projects"; "I had to constantly turn down vast sums of money while I was starving, writing a screenplay for free that I didn't like to write, because I hated writing .... [but] you simply have to say, ‘This is what I want to do. I want to make my movie. I don't want to take the money.'" Three weeks, eight in the morning until eight at night. He was trying to write a "B movie" in the style of AIP "teenage hot rod movies… the lowest rung of the movie ladder," but with professional quality. The epigraph line is repeated in the body, p. 48/49.
- Four interlocking stories on one end-of-summer night; USC classmates polish the script (humor, character). AIP almost signs, then backs off; Universal buys it. Filming in Marin and Sonoma, late 1972; Howard, Dreyfuss, Ford; 28 days, $775,000; editing/sound at Zoetrope in San Francisco. Plot sketch of Steve, Curt, John, Toad. Premiere August 1973; "grossed a whopping $145 million, or $50 for every dollar invested—a ratio that not even any of the Star Wars films would equal" (p. 51). Nominated for Best Picture and Best Original Screenplay.
- **Lucas, p. 51:** "[It] was a big moment for me." "Okay, now I am a director. Now I know I can get a job. I can work in this industry, and apply my trade, and express my ideas on things, and be creative in a way that I enjoy. . . . There wasn't anything in my life that was going to stop me from making movies." Beats his "millionaire before 30" prediction to his father: "By 28, he was a millionaire." Profits into Lucasfilm, THX, and ILM (Jurassic Park / Lost World are named as later ILM work).
- Already at work on a serial set "in the far future and a distant galaxy," first episode "actually… the middle of the series." Working title "Star Wars." Friends: "Boy! Watch out… When that one hits you're really going be thrown for a loop." **Lucas, p. 53:** "Oh, no-no. I went through American Graffiti. I can handle this."

## Ch. 6: The Saga (pp. 55–69)

- **Chapter epigraph, George Lucas, p. 55 — primary-watch statement:** "I would rather see us be a positive force in the universe than a cancer. We have the knowledge to be either one. That, in essence, is what Star Wars is about. We are both good and evil, and we have a choice."
- *Graffiti* convinced him audiences still wanted to feel good, against 1970s realist/violent "serious" Hollywood. **Lucas, p. 56:** "You can learn from cynicism, but you can't build on it." Serious themes "embedded in a highly entertaining film." Inspiration: Flash Gordon and "Adventure Theater," plus seriousness.
- Heroes from fairy tales and mythology vs. "high-tech monsters and machines" — "sort of “Jason and the Argonauts versus the Martians.”" Hires writers, is dissatisfied, writes it himself. Still "fine-tuning Star Wars: Episode IV… two-and-a-half years later, right up to its release." **Lucas, p. 57:** "I come up from the filmmakers' school of doing movies, which means I do everything myself." Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. He sticks to "the folk side of things," avoiding sex and violence. **Lucas, p. 57:** "My films aren't that violent or that sexy. Instead, I'm dealing with the need for humans to have friendships, to be compassionate, to band together to help each other and to join together against what is negative." On what a filmmaker needs: "focus on learning as much about life… First. Then learn the techniques of making a movie, because that stuff you can pick up pretty quickly. But having a really good understanding of histor>-, literature, psychology, sciences is very, very important" (p. 57–58; "histor>-" is a PDF typo).
- Plot boiled down "like an child-appropriate fairy tale" (book grammar): Luke assembles Han and the droids to rescue Leia from "the evil Empire, represented its enforcer Darth Vader. Change Luke and Han to knights on horseback, and Darth Vader to a dragon, and most children would recognize the basic storyline" (p. 58). **Lucas, p. 58:** he "didn't bother to “flesh out the storyline ... I had a rough of idea of what happened and who the major characters were, but I didn't include a scene-by-scene scenario of what happened in my treatment.”" Enough material "not for one movie, but several."
- Studio resistance: SF "old hat" except *Fantastic Voyage*, *2001*, *Planet of the Apes*; robots and a Wookiee; "aimed at children." **Lucas remembering the complaint, pp. 58–59:** "Look, that's Disney's. Disney does that. The rest of us can't do that, so we don't want to get into that area." Too long for one film. Fox signs for one movie only. **Lucas, to himself, pp. 59–60:** "Okay. I'll get rid of the last two thirds of it, and I'll just do the first act. I can make that into one movie. . . . I'm not going to give this up. I won't just put this on the shelf and forget it. I'll make this into three movies and I will make all three movies." Turns down a director's salary; 40 percent of the box-office gross plus merchandising; snaps up sequel rights.
- **Lucas, p. 60**, on adversity: "Film is not an easy occupation… It's not a matter of how well can you make a movie. It's how well can you make it under the circumstances, because there's always circumstances and you cannot use that as an excuse. . . . You simply have to show them the movie and it has got to work, and there are no excuses."
- ILM, 1975; Sprocket Systems (Skywalker Sound, 1989). Mid-1976, Tunisia "without the cast" (Shields's wording). Morale: a technician says Lucas knew what he wanted "but “the problem was that none of us knew what that was.”" **Mark Hamill, p. 61:** "I have a sneaking suspicion that if there were a way to make movies without actors, George would do it." Leia's "foul stench" line as an example of stiff dialogue. **Harrison Ford, p. 61:** "You can type this [expletive] George, but you sure can't say it." **Lucas, p. 61:** "I had to direct 500 people on Star Wars, and I hated it. I accept the power to do whatever I like with my camera. But I refuse to command other people."
- Ten days in Tunisia on schedule; a British editor he "hardly knew" cuts early scenes. **Lucas, pp. 61–62:** "they just weren't working, and-1 was very down about the whole situation." He recuts on Sundays; the editor had stopped "about a foot a film before he would have made a cut." British unions, tea, early wrap; Fox "pull the plug" and tells him to collapse two weeks into three days; triple crews; "pale and ill." Special-effects unit has blown half of a $2 million budget on three shots. Chest pains, Marin General, exhaustion. **Lucas to Dale Pollock, p. 62:** "That's when I really confirmed to myself that I was going to change. I wasn't going to make more films, I wasn't going to direct anymore. I was going to get my life a bit more under control."
- Ben Burtt, "organic" soundtrack; Burtt in *Film Sound Today* on rust, dents, real motors. Walkers (punch press + bicycle chains), TIE (altered elephant bellow), Wookiee (walrus and other animals), laser (antenna guy wire), lightsaber (TV + 35mm projector), landspeeder (LA Harbor Freeway through a vacuum-cleaner pipe). Hardware from "$30,000 worth of scrap metal and junk"; aliens from "Uglies, Ltd." in London.
- Release as "*Star Wars: Episode IV: A New Hope* (known simply as Star Wars)," 25 May 1977. Ebert excerpted (p. 64). Frame rates, optical zooms, 70mm. Budget "$10 million," gross "$400 million in its initial run." Merchandising almost unknown except Disney. **Lucas, p. 65:** "As it turned out, [Star Wars] was so successful we were able to make toy deals and we began to start the whole idea of action figures, of tie-ins, of toys that go along with movies. Over the years that's one of the things that's helped me stay independent and finance my own movies and stay in business." 2000: *Star Wars* action figures top-selling toys for boys, second overall to Barbie; original ~$3 figures "now valued at $1,400 and over."
- Six Oscars plus Special Achievement for sound effects, spring 1978. Pauline Kael (*New Yorker*): "The loudness, the smash-and-grab editing, the relentless pacing drive every idea from your head." "has no emotional grip." John Baxter (later a Lucas biographer): "God, it was so mindless." Jason Ankeny (2001) long excerpt: *Star Wars* "effectively ended a renaissance in American filmmaking," shifting from Coppola/Scorsese/Altman to "action-packed, special effects-powered events," "breakneck pacing," a generation "weaned on the rapid pace of television." Charles Taylor, Salon, "How ‘Star Wars' Ruined American Movies": "the infantilization of movies." **Lucas, p. 67**, "about his influence in putting spectacle over ideas": "I'm a filmmaker, not a director. I like the physical part of making movies. I might be a toymaker if I wasn't a filmmaker."
- *Empire*: Lucas has "no interest in directing it because of his unhappy experiences with the previous movie." Irvin Kershner. Shields: "Widely considered the best of the Star Wars movies so far, Kershner treats the characters as human beings, not as mythology types. Chewbacca wails as he holds C-3PO's head in his hands; Luke is clearly afraid when he confronts Darth Vader alone." Dark cinematography; 1980 hit; cliffhanger into *Return of the Jedi* (1983). *Raiders* (1981), *Temple of Doom* (1984) and the PG-13 rating (Britain: 25 cuts for under-16s); Steven Schiff in *Vanity Fair* on *Temple of Doom* upsetting children. *Last Crusade* 1989.
- *Jedi* 1983; 1997 Special Editions, one episode every other month that spring. *The Phantom Menace* (1999): Neeson, McGregor, Portman; charity premieres $5.6 million; $400 million North American ticket sales; "front-page hype… lukewarm response for audiences and critics." "The will be six Star Wars films in all" (book typo). *Attack of the Clones* 2002; Episode III "isn't expected till 2005."
