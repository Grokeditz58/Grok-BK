# Norwich, *I Am George Lucas* (2013): notes part 2 — chs. 6–9 (pp. 60–97)

Source: owner's MEGA PDF `sXtEkBLa`. Citations are printed footer numbers ("Norwich p. N"). Typography locked from the JPEGs. Formula: printed page P = PDF page − 2.

## Ch. 6: *Star Wars* Is Born (pp. 60–73) — priority chapter

p. 60: chapter-art of Lucas dreaming of a space city / robot.

- **pp. 61–62, typography locked, attributed:** "Here you've spent your whole life just begging, and using every means at your disposal to get one person or two people to say 'yes' to your project or to say, 'Yes, I'll do this. Yes,' you know? And then suddenly everybody says yes. Suddenly everybody wants you to do everything and anything you want."
- **Authors' wording (not a first-person quote):** "he was interested in creating a modern myth that dealt with the forces of good and evil. These two ideas were the beginning of a movie called *Star Wars*."
- *Graffiti* proceeds bought a Victorian in Marin as the new Lucasfilm Ltd. headquarters. Eight hours a day, five days a week, back room; researched science fiction and fantasy, Flash Gordon comics, "world religions, from Buddhism to Christianity," and "the American anthropologist Joseph Campbell, who wrote extensively about mythology."
- **pp. 65–66, typography locked. Crops `quotes/q01-p65-force-definition.jpg` and `quotes/q02-p66-kids-film-morality.jpg`.** Authors: "As George wrote and rewrote, the notion of the "Force" came into sharper focus. In the screenplay, the character of Obi-Wan Kenobi defines it as "an energy field created by all living things. It surrounds us and penetrates us; it binds the galaxy together."" Semicolon, not "and binds." Authors continue: "The Force can be used for good or evil. This tension was central to what George was trying to create with *Star Wars*." Then, attributed: "As he later explained, "I wanted to make a kids' film that would strengthen contemporary mythology and introduce a basic kind of morality. Nobody's saying the very basic things. They're dealing in the abstract. Everybody's forgetting to tell the kids, 'Hey, this is right and this is wrong.'""
- May 1974 first draft after a year; another year of "sweat and tears" and "No. 2 hard pencils"; hired Ralph McQuarrie. Many studios passed; Fox greenlit. Divided the script into three parts (authors' wording; also 10 MORE #4). Settled for a $150,000 director's fee instead of the $500,000 he easily could have demanded, in exchange for sequel and merchandising rights. Fresh faces: Hamill (relatively unknown TV actor) as Luke; Fisher as Leia; Ford as Han; Guinness as Ben "Obi-Wan" Kenobi to satisfy the studio. Tunisia for "openings scenes" (typo as printed) on Tatooine. "computer-generated special effects" for much of the other scenes (anachronism/simplification — 1977 was mostly optical/model work; flag as the book's wording). Opened his own special effects studio, Industrial Light & Magic (ILM). Nearly three hundred and sixty special effects.
- **p. 71 typo as printed:** "technical whizzes who knew who how to operate computer-controlled cameras."
- Sidebar "Danger on the Set!": a sandstorm knocked over sets and caused R2-D2's electronic controls to fail; a stormtrooper suffered a concussion; George wrote to Marcia, "I forget how impossible making movies really is. I get so depressed, but I guess I'll get through it somehow."
- George and Marcia edited; opened on forty-three screens May 25, 1977; "ten Academy Awards nominations and earning $775 million worldwide" (the Time Line said "winning seven Academy Awards" — flag both; the Oscar illustration on p. 72 shows seven statues). "there were still two more installments of the *Star Wars* trilogy to make."
- Film Notes p. 67: "Chewbacca the Wookiee" and "R2-D2" spelled correctly; "*Star Wars* is actually Episode IV in Lucas's epic series, though it was the first one made." James Earl Jones as the voice of Darth Vader.

## Ch. 7: The Challenge of the Sequel (pp. 74–81)

*More American Graffiti*: George not that interested; executive producer, not director; Vietnam themes; 1979 flop.

- **p. 76, typography locked, authors:** "part two of the original *Star Wars* trilogy would give the upper hand to the "Dark Side" in the epic battle between good and evil." ("Dark Side" in quotes, title case.)
- **p. 77:** George admits, "I was nervous when I started the second film." Brought in another writer (unnamed here — Kasdan is named later for *Return of the Jedi*). Lando Calrissian; "One of the few criticisms of *Star Wars* was that it didn't have enough minority actors. So George decided to go with Billy Dee Williams."
- Sidebar "Creating Yoda": "two-foot-tall swamp creature who trains Luke Skywalker in the ways of the Jedi Knights"; puppet; "his scenes were enhanced with the use of computer animation" (simplification/slip — *Empire* Yoda was a puppet, no CGI; flag). Frank Oz performed and provided the voice. George decided not to direct.
- **pp. 78–80, typography locked:** "As a director, I wanted to do everything," he explained. "It's very hard for me to delegate things to other people. Well, the best way to do that is to take one more step back and be forced to delegate everything. And see if I could stand it."
- **p. 80 typo as printed:** "George turned the directing reigns over to" Irvin Kershner (should be "reins"). Clashed (George liked a fast pace; Irvin preferred to spend time with each scene) but made it work; George still controlled editing and effects; "spent about $33 million of his own money." Opened May 21, 1980; first weekend almost $5 million; "more than $530 million it would eventually earn worldwide."
- Film Notes p. 79: "The movie ends with a startling discovery by Luke about Darth Vader's true identity" (does not explicitly spoil "I am your father"). "capture of Han Solo, Chewbacca, and Princess Leia" is a simplification.

## Ch. 8: A Guy Named Indy (pp. 82–89)

Archaeology classes at MJC "may have" planted the seed. 1975 discussed the idea with Philip Kaufman, who suggested the Ark of the Covenant.

- **Slip, p. 84:** "In the Bible, the covenant is a lost chest that contains the stone tablets with the Ten Commandments on them." (The Ark is the chest; the covenant is the agreement — flag.)
- **Beach-vacation habit, typography locked:** "so I miss all the craziness that goes on, all the hoopla, and the hype and the success, and how much it's making, or whether it's doing good or whether it's doing bad." Hawaii 1977 with Spielberg; *Raiders*. George wanted cliff-hanger TV; Spielberg preferred nonstop thrills. "George wanted Indiana to be a maverick, but ultimately a good guy, kind of like Han Solo. Spielberg saw him as more of a rebel." Tom Selleck / *Magnum, P.I.*; Spielberg suggested Ford; George resisted at first, then agreed. Shot in seventy-three days, finishing two weeks ahead of schedule; editor Michael Kahn.
- **p. 88, typography locked:** "*Raiders of the Lost Ark* opened on June 14, 1981." (Common U.S. date is June 12 — record as the book's date, also on the Time Line.) First weekend more than $8 million; nearly $400 million. *Temple of Doom* 1984, George producing and Spielberg directing, "more than $330 million worldwide." "More movies would follow, as well as several TV spinoffs."
- Film Notes p. 87: "Rene Belloq" without an accent in the body, "René Belloq" in the star list; Karen Allen as Marion Ravenwood; John Rhys-Davies as Sallah. George's job: Executive producer and Co-writer.

## Ch. 9: Back to the Empire (pp. 90–97)

p. 90: chapter-art of Luke versus Vader in a lightsaber duel (Cloud City-like setting). Feeling the heat on the final installment. Executive producer; Lawrence Kasdan to help with the screenplay; Richard Marquand to direct, "though George would be on set every day." Speederbike scenes on Endor filmed with Steadicam; "Some scenes were shot in California's Redwood National Park, with the actors later added in with computers" (another CGI anachronism/simplification — optical compositing; flag). Ewoks "teddy bear-like creatures."

- **p. 94, typography locked:** "Though audience members might have thought the Ewoks were computer generated, they were actually played by little people dressed in elaborate costumes." "Jabba the Hutt, the gangster-like creature who is talked about, but never seen, in the original *Star Wars*" (then immediately the Special Edition added him).
- *Return of the Jedi* opened May 25, 1983, "six years to the day after the release of the original"; opening weekend about $30 million ("nearly made its money back"); more than $475 million worldwide.
- **p. 95, typography locked, authors:** "*Return of the Jedi* concluded the second trilogy of the *Star Wars* epic." Then "about fifteen years later." *Howard the Duck* as unsuccessful. Special Edition: restored deteriorated films, "shot new scenes and even added back scenes that had been cut," including Jabba "as he'd originally intended."
- **p. 96, typography locked:** *Star Wars* had been "about forty percent of what I wanted it to be," he had said. (Rau 1999 had "about sixty percent" — record both as their books' wording.) Rereleases "nearly as big as the original releases"; all three premiered in the number one spot; "the momentum booster George needed to start in on the first trilogy of the *Star Wars* series."
- **Film Notes p. 93, typography locked:** "Episode VI of the series opens with the building of a new Death Star by Darth Vader and the Galactic Empire." (Vader "building" is a simplification.) "Luke then returns to Master Yoda to complete his Jedi training, and learns more about his relationship with Darth Vader, as well as his connection to Princess Leia." "Luke returns to destroy the Death Star, where he engages in a final battle between good and evil with Darth Vader. The force of good prevails, and the rebels go on to destroy the Death Star and defeat the evil Empire once and for all." (Lowercase "force.") Ian McDiarmid as the Emperor; Peter Mayhew as Chewbacca the Wookiee.
