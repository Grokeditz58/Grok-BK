# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Chapter II: The Star Wars (pp. 45–83)

Fully read. Citations: Kaminski p. N; Kaminski Notes n. Y (Ch. II notes start at n.100). Kaminski glosses flagged. Staff/crew/cast statements labeled by speaker.

## Gestation, Flash Gordon rights, optimism (pp. 45–48)

**Gary Kurtz** (n.100), typography as Kaminski prints it, dinner during *American Graffiti* editing: “George and I had dinner one night, and we were looking through the paper while we were editing American Graffiti… Discussion came around to Flash Gordon, and wouldn’t it be great to have a Flash Gordon kind of science fiction movie–that would be great. We’d love to see that. That’s sort of the gestation of Star Wars–and that was based on something that we wanted to see, that we would pay to go see! And no one was making it.”

UA registered the title “The Star Wars” with the MPAA on August 1st, 1971 (n.101). Lucas (n.102): “I had thought about doing what became Star Wars long before THX 1138.”

Kaminski footnote, p. 46: dating of the Flash Gordon rights attempt is “somewhat ambiguous; accounts place it anywhere from 1973 all the way back to before THX 1138,” and **Jonathan Rinzler**, in *The Making of Star Wars*, “is very tactful not to assign it to any specific time period.” Kaminski’s preferred date: New York, May 1971, on the way to Cannes, visiting UA and the Flash Gordon copyright holders; King Features demanded more than he had; Dino DeLaurentis was courting Fellini.

**Francis Coppola** (n.104): “I remember having lunch with George at the Palm restaurant in New York… and he was very depressed because he had just come back and they wouldn’t sell him Flash Gordon. And he says, ‘Well, I’ll just invent my own.’ ”

Lucas block, typography locked (n.105): “I loved the Flash Gordon comic books…I loved the Universal serials with Buster Crabbe. After THX 1138 I wanted to do Flash Gordon and tried to buy the rights to it from King Features, but they wanted a lot of money for it, more than I could afford then. They didn’t want to part with their rights—they wanted Fellini to do Flash Gordon. I realized that I could make up a character as easily as Alex Raymond, who took his character from Edgar Rice Burroughs. It’s your basic superhero in outer space. I realised that what I really wanted to do was a contemporary action fantasy.” (Kaminski’s extract mixes American “realized” and British “realised” in the same block.)

Lucas (n.106): “When I made the deal I had to give it a name… but it wasn’t until I finished Graffiti in ’73 that I started writing it.” In place of Emperor Ming, “he would place the aptly-named Emperor, and eventually in later drafts his henchman Darth Vader” — that “eventually… later drafts” timing is Kaminski’s.

Lucas, 1974, typography locked (n.107), on *THX* then *Graffiti*, a lesson Kaminski says carries into *Star Wars*: “I realised after THX that people don’t care about how the country’s being ruined. All that movie did was make people more pessimistic… So I decided that this time I would make a more optimistic film… It’s too easy to make films about Watergate… I’m a very bad cynic. But we’ve got to regenerate optimism… It’s all that hokey stuff about being a good neighbor, and the American spirit and all that crap. There is something in it.”

Lucas (n.108): after *Graffiti*’s effect on high-school kids, “I started thinking about ten and twelve year olds… They don’t have westerns; they don’t have pirate movies… I felt, then, that Star Wars would be a natural and give kids a fantasy life that they really needed to have. I wanted to make a romantic space fantasy, the sort of thing that existed before the hard, cold side of science took over the genre in the fifties.”

Lucas to *Starlog,* 1981 (n.109): “Star Wars was a little bit different… because by that time I’d decided that it was useless to try to get someone else to write my screenplays…I finally gave up!”

Souvenir program: story work in January 1973, around the January 28th Northpoint screening (n.110). Lucas (n.111): “It was January 1973. I had been paid $20,000 for Graffiti, it had taken two years, I was $15,000 in debt and Universal hated the film so much they were contemplating selling it as a TV Movie of the Week. I had to start paying back some of this movie so I thought, ‘I’ll whip up that treatment, my second deal at United Artists, my little space thing.’ ”

## Journal of the Whills, name lists, Berg (pp. 48–49)

Free-association name list (n.112): “Emperor Ford Xerxes XII,” then “Xenos, Thorpe, Roland, Monroe, Lars, Kane, Hayden, Crispin, Leila, Zena, Owen, Mace, Wan, Star, Bail, Biggs, Bligh, Cain, Clegg, Fleet, Valorum.” Combined: Alexander Xerxes XII “Emperor of Decarte,” Owen Lars “Imperial General,” **Han Solo is “leader of the Hubble people,”** Mace Windy a “Jedi-Bendu,” C.2. Thorpe a space pilot, Anakin Starkiller “King of Bebers,” Luke Skywalker “Prince of Bebers.” Planets: Yoshiro and Aquilae (desert), Norton III (ice), Yavin (jungle, native eight-foot-tall Wookies — Kaminski’s spelling here).

Two-page handwritten *Journal of the Whills.* Opening line as Kaminski prints it: “This is the story of Mace Windy, a revered Jedi-Bendu of Opuchi, as related to us by C.J. Thorpe, padawaan learner to the famed Jedi.” **J.W. Rinzler** (n.113) transcribes the rest: C.J./C.2. = “Chuiee Two Thorpe of Kissel. My father is Han Dardell Thorpe, chief pilot of the renown galactic cruiser Tarnack.” Sixteen, Intersystems Academy, padawaan to Mace Windy, “Warlord to the Chairman of the Alliance of Independent Systems…Some felt that he was more powerful than the Imperial leader of the Galactic Empire…Ironically, it was his own comrades’ fear…that led to his replacement…and expulsion from the royal forces.” Part II: four years later, “guardians on a shipment of fusion portables to Yavin,” summoned to “the desolate second planet of Yoshiro” — then “Lucas’s first space-fantasy narrative trails off…”

Kaminski: little relation to the final film, but phonetics of “Chuiee” (“Chewie”), a pilot named Han, a galactic Empire, a space academy, Jedi. Recalls E.E. Smith / *Lensmen*.

**Jeff Berg**, Lucas’s agent (n.114): “I knew more about the story based on what George had told me than what was in that brief treatment.” Recommended something simpler.

## *The Hidden Fortress* treatment, May 1973 (pp. 49–57)

Lucas discovered Kurosawa via **John Milius** at film school. Kaminski’s synthesis argument, p. 50, typography locked in the 1975 Lucas line (n.115): “I’m trying to make everything look very natural, a casual almost I’ve-seen-this-before look… You look at that painting of Tusken Raiders and the banthas, and you say, ‘Oh yeah, Bedouins…’ Then you look at it some more and say, ‘Wait a minute, that’s not right…’ Like the X-wing and TIE fighter battle, you say, ‘I’ve seen that, it’s World War II—but wait a minute—that isn’t any kind of jet I’ve ever seen before.’ I want the whole film to have that quality!”

Kaminski: first treatment is “essentially *The Hidden Fortress* set on another planet. There are no Jedi or Sith, or even the Force.” Lucas “contemplated purchasing the rights” (n.116) and “flat-out remade it,” copying passages from Donald Richie’s 1965 *The Films of Akira Kurosawa* plot summary. Lucas (n.117): “Hidden Fortress was an influence on Star Wars right from the beginning… I was searching around for a story. I had some scenes—the cantina scene and the space battle scene—but I couldn’t think of a basic plot. Originally, the film was a good concept in search of a story. And then I thought of Hidden Fortress, which I’d seen again in 1972 or ’73, and so the first plots were very much like it.”

Kurosawa (n.118–119): “A film should appeal to sophisticated, profound-thinking people while at the same time entertaining simplistic people.” “A truly good movie is really enjoyable too… A truly good movie is interesting and easy to understand.”

Treatment begun April 17th, handwritten ten pages, typed fourteen, completed first week of May 1973 (n.120–121). Titled *The Star Wars*: a General leads a princess through enemy lands. Kaminski footnote: *Journal of the Whills* is “a proto-Star-Wars that was abandoned”; “the treatment” hereafter means the May fourteen-page version. Appendix A is flagged for the holy-grail “Journal of the Whills” document conceptually separate from this plot summary.

**Jan Helander**, “The Development of Star Wars as Seen Through the Scripts by George Lucas” (n.122), summary of the synopsis locked in notes (civil war; two bickering Imperial bureaucrats crash on Aquilae; rebel princess and general Luke Skywalker; religious temple / young boy rebels; cantina “lazer sword”; asteroid field; forbidden Yavin; furry aliens sell princess; farmer; city-planet Alderaan, capitol of the Empire; Ophuchi reward ceremony; princess reveals “true goddess-like self”).

pp. 53–54: Kaminski prints parallel passages — Lucas’s synopsis vs Kurosawa/Richie — almost sentence-for-sentence (thirty-third vs sixteenth century; “aura spice” vs royal gold; two Imperial bureaucrats vs two peasants; land speeders vs horses; space port Gordon; Imperial patrol of twelve vs four). Later sections add primitive aliens and young boys beyond Kurosawa’s scope. Helander (n.123) reprints the same opening comparison and notes General Skywalker’s spear fight with an alien as also in *Hidden Fortress*.

Rabbit hole, pp. 55–57: *Hidden Fortress* said to remake Kurosawa’s 1945 *They Who Tread on the Tiger’s Tail*, from the Noh *Ataka* / Kabuki *Kanjincho*, from the 12th-century Minamoto Yoshitsune / Benkei story in *Tale of the Heike*. Kaminski: “this mythologized historical character… is in some distant way responsible for Star Wars existing as it does.”

**Kaminski thesis for this chapter, typography locked, p. 57:** General Luke Skywalker in the treatment “bears the same name as the protagonist of the final film, [but] his character has more in common with Obi Wan, that of an elderly Jedi master—although the Jedi do not yet exist… the character instead being a mere General, a port of the General Makabe character.” “Interestingly enough, the Luke Skywalker of the final film does not exist yet, nor does Darth Vader—neither of them were any part of Lucas’ original concept. Nor are the Jedi, Sith or even the Force—his original story was simply a futuristic adventure tale, a self-contained story about an elderly General leading a princess to safety and a rebellion against a dictatorship… an elaborated version of Kurosawa’s Hidden Fortress set in outer space. Considering Lucas’ claims of telling a tale about Vader and Luke, or some sort of laboriously-researched and brilliantly-confected ‘modern mythology,’ this is a truly interesting uncovering.”

Lucas “much more honest in the 1970’s” (n.129): “I had the Star Wars project in mind even before I started my last picture, American Graffiti, and as soon as I finished I began writing Star Wars in January 1973.... In fact, I wrote four entirely different screenplays for Star Wars, searching for just the right ingredients, characters and storyline. It's always been what you might call a good idea in search of a story.”

## Escapist fantasy, residual *THX*/*Graffiti*, *Apocalypse Now*, *Dune* (pp. 57–62)

Lucas on the Western and Vietnam-era culture (n.131). 1974 (n.132–133): “Some of my friends are more concerned about art and being considered a Fellini or an Orson Wells… I’m more drawn to Flash Gordon. I like action adventure, chases, things blowing up, and I have strong feelings about science fiction and comic books and that sort of world.” “I don’t care if I make a piece of art or a piece of shit.” 1977 (n.134): “My main reason for making it was to give young people an honest, wholesome fantasy life, the kind my generation had… Now they have The Six Million Dollar Man and Kojak.” Same year (n.135): “I wasted four years of my life cruising like the kids in American Graffiti and now I'm on an intergalactic dream of heroism. In Star Wars I'm telling the story of me. It's my fantasy… Young people today don't have a fantasy life anymore… All they've got is Kojak and Dirty Harry.”

*THX* residuals, p. 58: robotic policemen → stormtroopers; proto-Jawas as “shell dwellers”; holograms; “used universe”; cloaked spectre resembling the Emperor; man vs machine; opens with a Buck Rogers clip.

Lucas on *Graffiti* continuity (n.136): “[Star Wars was] my next movie after American Graffiti… It’s about a young boy leaving his world and going off into the unknown to a great adventure. American Graffiti focuses on that final night when that decision is made. Star Wars carries that story on to what happens after you leave.” This “would not come to prominence until… the second draft” — Kaminski’s timing.

**Harrison Ford**, 1977 (n.137): “A lot of the elements of Han Solo are a lot like Bob Falfa in American Graffiti. But I don’t—I hope—they’re not the same person.”

**Walter Murch** (n.138): “Star Wars is George’s version of Apocalypse Now, rewritten in an otherworldly context… The Rebels in Star Wars are the Vietnamese, and the Empire is the United States.”

Lucas (n.139): “A lot of my interest in Apocalypse Now was carried over into Star Wars. I figured that I couldn’t make that film because it was about the Vietnam War, so I would essentially deal with some of the same interesting concepts… convert them into space fantasy, so you’d have essentially a large technological empire going after a small group of freedom fighters or human beings.”

*Dune* (pp. 59–60): desert/Tatooine not yet in the synopsis (first appears in the rough draft as desert planet Utapau). Spice; rebel-boys subplot paralleled to Paul Atreides training Fremen. Second source of the rebel-boys scene: Kurosawa’s *Sanjuro* (1962). Cantina arm-cutting: *Yojimbo* (1961). Furry aliens: possibly H. Beam Piper’s *Fuzzy* stories (n.140). Asimov *Foundation* “minimal on the 1973 synopsis”; possible “city-planet” Alderaan // Trantor. E.E. Smith’s *Lensmen* “would come to mold the coming drafts.”

Kaminski, p. 62: the 1973 synopsis “does not tell the tale of someone, especially a youngster, taking their first steps into some kind of larger world,” so Lucas began thinking about making it more “superhero in outer space.”

## Deal, studios, Parkhouse, rough-draft genesis (pp. 62–71)

New Hollywood context; Kaminski: *Star Wars* would “essentially end the American New Wave.” First published concept, fall 1973 (n.141): “Star Wars is a mixture of Lawrence of Arabia, the James Bond films and 2001. The space aliens are the heroes, and the Homo Sapiens naturally the villains. Nobody has ever done anything like this since Flash Gordon Conquers the Universe in 1942.” Footnote: this description “shows how much more outrageous and comic-book-like he was making it.”

UA and Universal passed, summer 1973 (n.142). Kaminski footnote quotes both rejection letters at length — they liked the concept but found it risky: UA, “The innocence of the story, plus the sophistication of the world [Lucas] will depict makes for the best kind of motion picture… a film for children of all ages,” but “too much cost involved for this kind of juvenile story.” Universal internal memo: “If the movie works, we might have a wonderful, humorous and exciting adventure-fantasy… The question, in the end, is how much faith we have in Mr Lucas’s ability to pull it all off.”

**Tom Pollock**, Lucas’s lawyer (n.143): “We did not want to go with Universal.” Ten days after Universal passed, Fox / **Alan Ladd Jr.** took it; Ladd had been smuggled an advance print of *Graffiti*. Kaminski: the synopsis Ladd signed “could not possibly have been filmed—Lucas didn’t tell him that it was a remake of Hidden Fortress and that he hadn’t secured the rights from Toho.” Ladd (n.144): “When he said, ‘This sequence is going to be like The Sea Hawk or this like Captain Blood or this like Flash Gordon,’ I knew exactly what he was saying… That gave me confidence that he was going to pull it off.”

June 1973 deal: Lucas $50,000 to write, $100,000 to direct, merchandising and sequel rights; Kurtz $50,000 producing. Fall: sell Mill Valley, buy Victorian office at 52 Parkway, San Anselmo. **Kurtz** (n.145) on Parkhouse zoning. Lucasfilm created 1971 at Pollock’s suggestion; first employees **Bunny Alsup** (Kurtz’s sister-in-law, personal secretary) and **Lucy Wilson** (financial book-keeper) (n.146). **Hal Barwood** (n.147) on rented rooms and lunches. Kurtz (n.148) on Lucas buying neighboring houses “just to keep them out of peoples hands.” Lucas (n.149): “When I was writing Star Wars, for the first year, there was an infinite number of distractions… Graffiti was a huge hit, plus I was restoring my office… Building a screening room kept me going for nine, ten months.”

Around September 1973, revision of the synopsis into a script (n.150–151).

## Rough draft, May 1974: four ingredients of later Vader (pp. 65–76)

Four versions of the script plus the treatment (five); *Journal of the Whills* summary may be a sixth. Rough draft introduces Jedi-Bendu and Sith. Elderly General Skywalker shifted to supporting role; protagonist becomes eighteen-year-old **Annikin Starkiller** (n.152). Brother Biggs killed by a Sith in the opening (later drafts use Deak). Father **Kane Starkiller** and friend General Luke Skywalker are the only two surviving Jedi. Black Knight of the Sith / commander is **Prince Valorum**, assisted by his General, **a man named Darth Vader**.

Lucas (n.153): “If someone tells me an interesting story, I can easily transform it into a screenplay. But to be the initiator of the idea, that’s very difficult.” Notebook in the shower / at night (n.154–155), including writing on a Hawaii vacation after *Graffiti*.

**Dale Pollock** (n.156) on the notebook: first page notation during *Graffiti* sound mix — **Walter Murch** asked for “R2, D2 (Reel 2, Dialog 2),” and Lucas liked the abbreviated sound of R2-D2. Weekend armfuls of SF magazines and comics; Marcia wondered what was going on; “he was making a movie that ten-year-old boys would love.” Research from *Buck Rogers* and *Flash Gordon* to *2001*; Ming as a model for the emperor; *Iron Men of Mongo* five-foot copper metal man; Burroughs banthas and flying birds; *Forbidden Planet*, *The Day the World Ended*, *Dune*, *Lensmen*.

Lucas to *Starlog,* 1981 (n.157): “I have to admit that I read Starlog. Starting in 1973 I was very much focused on science fiction—the genre people, the conventions, the magazines, every fantasy thing I could get my hands on—to see where everybody’s head was.” **Martin Scorsese** later states Lucas screened *Adventures of Robin Hood* (n.158). Lucas: the “research” “gave him feelings for themes and motifs” (n.159) but “[Analysing it] becomes academic, and when I was doing it it wasn’t academic” (n.160).

Dogfight vision (n.161): “One of the key visions I had of the film when I started was of a dogfight in outer space with spaceships—two ships flying through space shooting each other. That was my original idea… In Star Trek it was always one ship sitting here and another ship sitting there… It wasn’t really a dogfight where they were racing around in space firing.”

Name lists (n.162–163): “Kane Highsinger/ Jedi friend; Leia Aquilae/ Princess; General Vader/ Imperial Commander; Han Solo/ friend.” “Seethreepio” and “Artwo Deetwo” as “workmen,” then “two workmen as robots? One dwarft/one Metropolis style.” Later note: “Make film more point-of-view of robots.”

**Rinzler** transcribes late-1973 notes (n.164), typography as printed: “Theme: Aquilae is a small independent country like North Vietnam threatened by a neighbour or provincial rebellion, instigated by gangsters aided by empire… The empire is like America ten years from now, after gangsters assassinated the Emperor and were elevated to power in a rigged election… We are at a turning point: fascism or revolution… Han very old (150 years)… Establish impossibility of Death Star… A conflict between freedom and conformity… Tell at least two stories: Starkiller becomes a man (not good enough); Valorum wakes up (morally speaking)… Valorum like Green Beret who realises wrong of Empire… thundersaber…”

Jedi as samurai/military, “Imperial Space Force”; Force does not exist yet; no super-human powers. Three main characters: “the general, the princess, the boy (Starkiller).” Kane Highsinger becomes Kane Starkiller, father of Annikin (n.165). Younger brother Deak added. History more like Asimov’s *Foundation*: no Republic, only a once-benevolent Empire; Jedi as bodyguards of the emperor for “one hundred thousand years,” then hunted by “THE KNIGHTS OF SITH.” Opening roll-up of the rough draft printed in full, p. 70.

Hyoe Tadokoro from *Hidden Fortress* split into Prince Valorum (warrior/Sith) and General Darth Vader (military henchman).

Pollock on the writing room (n.166): eight hours a day, Eisenstein photo, *THX* poster, 1941 Wurlitzer, no music until the daily page count; Cronkite as quitting time. Lucas: “You beat your head against the wall and say, ‘Why can’t I make this work? Why aren’t I smarter? Why can’t I do what everybody else can do?’ ” “I don’t let out my anger and fears very well.” Stomach and chest pains, headaches. **Lucy Wilson** (n.167): “I came in one day and his wastebasket had tons of hair in it!” — Lucas cut his hair with scissors when frustrated. Lucas work-ethic block (n.168): calendar quotas, five pages a day, “I’ve always got about one page done by four o’clock.”

Early 1974: Marcia editing Scorsese’s *Alice Doesn’t Live Here Anymore* in Arizona; Lucas joins her in a hotel. **Scorsese** (n.169): “I remember George was writing Star Wars at the time. He had all these books with him, like Isaac Asimov’s Guide to the Bible, and he was envisioning this fantasy epic. He did explain that he wanted to tap into the collective unconscious of fairy tales. And he screened certain movies, like Howard Hawks’ Air Force [1943] and Michael Curtiz’s Robin Hood [1938].”

Rough draft dated May 1974, titled “The Star Wars.” Helander summary (n.170) locked in substance: Utapau fourth moon; Kane, Annikin, Deak; Sith kills Deak; Kane’s body “a concoction of artificial limbs”; Luke Skywalker becomes Annikin’s teacher; Clieg Whitsun; General Darth Vader and Governor Crispin Hoedaack; death star; Artwo Detwo and See Threepio; cantina lasersword; Kane rips a power unit from his body and dies; Vader and Prince Valorum (black Knight of the Sith); asteroid field; Yavin; Wookees including Prince Chewbacca; Annikin captured and tortured by General Vader; **Valorum realises the Imperials are “completely without honour and codes,” turns, frees Annikin and Leia, garbage chute; Wookees destroy the death star, “killing both Vader and Governor Hoedaack”**; Queen Leia honours the heroes including Valorum; Annikin appointed Lord Protector of Aquilae.

## Lucas’s later “split the saga” claims vs the actual draft (pp. 72–74)

Kaminski: this is the script Lucas later calls too long to be one movie, supposedly cut into a third with the other two thirds for *Empire* and *Jedi*. “the story bears no resemblance to any of the subsequent films.” “Lucas would later claim that the basic concept for the entire Star Wars ‘Saga’ was outlined in this but it is not true in the least.”

Lucas, 2002 (n.171): “When I started to write it, it got to be too big, it got to be 250, 300 pages… I said, well, I can't do this. The studio will never allow this. I will take the first half, make a movie out of that, and then I was determined to come back and finish the other three, or other two stories.” Some versions “explicitly describing how his ‘original script’… ended with the forest and Death Star battles, indicating that he is indeed referencing the rough draft of 1974” (n.172).

**Kaminski rebuttal, typography locked:** “there is little truth to this statement, one which he has been repeating since the 1980’s. Aside from the fact that the rough draft was only 132 pages—making it twenty-four pages shorter than the final screenplay, and not the exaggerated behemoth he claims—the basic plot is remarkably similar to the final film” (desolate planet, mentor, cantina, Han, rescue princess from “death star,” one-man fighters, mentor earlier killed, medals). Asteroid and Wookiee-forest beats “hardly constitutes blueprinting the entire trilogy.”

Lucas, 2005, revised to account for the prequels (n.173): “You have to remember; originally Star Wars was intended to be one movie, at the cinema for a Saturday night. You never saw what came before, you never saw what came after. It was designed to be the saga of Darth Vader; it starts with a monster coming through the door, throwing everyone around. Halfway through you realise that he's actually a man, and that the hero is his son. It was meant to be one movie, but I broke it up ’cause I didn’t have the money to make that movie, as it would have been a 5-hour movie. The tragedy of Darth Vader was diminished – it was hard to see that it was actually a story about a guy who becomes redeemed.”

**Kaminski, p. 74:** “This claim is even more preposterous than the first one… nothing of the sort was ever intended. Especially since Darth Vader was not even part of his initial fourteen-page treatment (nor was Luke)… Darth Vader as we know him would not come to be created for many years, and in fact would not be finalized until the 1980’s. His character is hardly in this first script, and is nothing more than a minor villain General, nor is he monstrous, memorable or even a Sith Lord; instead he is a slimy Imperial, a spineless General who likes to give orders and collect the spoils of war.” Prints rough-draft scene 66, Library / Palace of Lite / Aquilae: Vader at a desk, welcomes Prince Valorum, “Your exploits are legendary… If there is any way I can assist you, my entire command is at your bidding.” Valorum wants computer/comms access. Vader: “Do you really believe he's a Jedi?” Valorum: “If he was not a Jedi, I wouldn't be here.”

Lucas, 1983 (n.174), which Kaminski flags as Lucas “catch[ing] us off guard” by admitting no script contained the whole story: “There are four or five scripts for Star Wars, and you can see as you flip through them where certain ideas germinated and how the story developed. There was never a script completed that had the entire story as it exists now… As the stories unfolded, I would take certain ideas and save them; I'd put them aside in notebooks… I just kept taking out stuff, and finally with Star Wars I felt I had one little incident that introduced the characters. So for the last six years [1977-1983] I've been trying to get rid of all the ideas I generated and felt so bad about throwing out in the first place.”

## Four separate elements later combined into “Saga” Vader (pp. 74–77)

Stay in 1977-only headspace, Kaminski instructs.

1. **Imperial bad guy named Darth Vader** — “a ‘tall grim-looking’ one according to the rough draft, but merely a man… a relatively minor one, acting as more of a bodyguard or muscleman… and he dies along with Governor Hoedaack when the giant ‘Space Fortress’ is blown up by the Wookie attack ships. He doesn’t wear his trademark costume or mask—those don’t appear until the second draft and wouldn’t even become permanent fixtures until the final. General Darth Vader is merely a flunkey, a henchman, and he would remain in this status all the way until the final film.” “this character bears little relation to the one we are familiar with, and is simply a human General who shares his name (Lucas shuffled names around freely).”

2. **A Sith Lord redeeming himself and turning to good** — not Vader, but **Prince Valorum**, a knight of the Sith, expert at exterminating Jedi. Sees Vader torture Annikin, realises the Imperials lack the samurai-like code, turns, helps the heroes escape. Port of General Hyoe Tadokoro from *Hidden Fortress* (scarred after losing a duel; princess remarks his master is cruel; he frees the heroes). “Valorum himself also displays many of the future Vader mannerisms, such as being a feared Sith warrior and dressing in black.” Renunciation “vaguely reminiscent” of *Jedi*’s finale but “wholly and distinctly separate”; Sith here “more like a mercenary band of pirates.”

3. **A family of Jedi** — Annikin son of Kane; Kane one of two surviving Jedi, dying, cannot train his sons; after Deak is killed, Kane asks friend Luke Skywalker to train Annikin. Name map, typography locked: “Kane would become Anakin Skywalker, Annikin would become Luke Skywalker, and Luke would become Obi Wan.” “To get a clearer picture of where the story was headed: Father Skywalker (aka Anakin), a Jedi who has become half machine in his battles, takes his son Luke to his Jedi friend Obi Wan Kenobi to train him in the Jedi ways.”

4. **A father who is becoming a machine** — Kane has all limbs and even parts of organs replaced, is dying, later rips a power unit from his body so the others can use suspended animation and avoid Imperial scanners.

“Together, these elements would be later recycled and combined by Lucas when attempting to resolve the Father Skywalker issue for his sequel to the final film.”

## “Father Skywalker” as the 1977 film actually states it (p. 77)

Kaminski’s reconstruction from *Star Wars* alone, typography locked: “Luke had a father that was a great Jedi. We call this father character as simply Father Skywalker, since he remained nameless. It is implied that Father Skywalker lived on Tatooine with his friend, Ben Kenobi (ref: ‘[Owen] thought [Father Skywalker] should have stayed here [on Tatooine] and not gotten involved’). There, he lived a dull farm life with his brother Owen, but yearned for a more adventurous experience (ref: ‘Luke’s just not a farmer, Owen; he has too much of his father in him.’). When the Clone Wars began, Kenobi convinced him to go off and fight, but Owen thought that he should stay on Tatooine and mind his farming. Father Skywalker was already a great pilot and naturally became a great hero in the war, and Kenobi became a General as well. Together, the two became Jedi knights of the Republic, and fought side by side… Kenobi began training others to be Jedi, but one of them, a man named Darth Vader, fell to the darkside of the Force. When the Emperor took over, Darth Vader joined his forces, hunting down the Jedi and murdering Father Skywalker. Kenobi escaped extinction and returned to Tatooine, living as a hermit. This is all the information that can be extrapolated from Star Wars.”

Kane in the first draft “is very much a similar character to the final Father Skywalker… So throughout all the drafts, the character of Father Skywalker remained essentially the same, with the only difference being his death pushed further into history and a more detailed origin developed.”

## The Force not yet a power; Castaneda claim rejected; 21-87 echo (pp. 77–80)

Force “is not existent in this draft—the phrase ‘may the force of others be with you’ is used, but it is merely a generic ‘good luck’ phrase… Most agree that it is a play off of the Christian phrase ‘May God be with you.’” Second draft would turn Jedi-Bendu into *Lensmen*-style super-powered warriors, with a Kiber crystal increasing abilities. Parallels: Kirby’s *New Gods* “The Source”; Smith’s “The Cosmic All.”

**Mark Hamill** to *Preview,* 1983 (n.175): “I asked him about the origin of the idea, and he said it's in about 450 old science fiction novels… He’s the first to admit it’s not an original concept. It’s nice how George presented the idea so everyone can get as much or as little out of it as they want. Some see it as a very religious thing.”

New Age / Ki / Qi / Ka cultural context, 1970s San Francisco. Popular “fact” that Lucas got “the force of others” from Carlos Castaneda’s *Tales of Power*: **Kaminski rejects this.** *Tales of Power* published 1975, “many months after Lucas would have already invented the basic premise… many months after Lucas had completed the second draft.” *The Eagle’s Gift* (1981) is “very obviously similar” in details but “published far too late”; “the influence may have been the complete reverse—Star Wars’ ‘the Force’ may have influenced Castaneda.” Footnote: Lucas was “obviously familiar” with Castaneda and “would later make references to it”; Don Juan “would have an impact on a certain Star Wars character, as we will later see.”

Lucas (n.176): “The ‘Force of others’ is what all basic religions are based on, especially the Eastern religions… which is, essentially, that there is a force, God, whatever you want to call it.”

**Arthur Lipsett’s *21-87***: audio of Warren S. McCulloch vs cinematographer **Roman Kroitor**. Kroitor: “Many people feel that in the contemplation of nature and in communication with other living things, they become aware of some kind of force, or something, behind this apparent mask which we see in front of us, and they call it God.” **Steve Silberman**, *Wired,* 2005 (n.177), brought the similarity to Lucas; Lucas said his use of the term was “an echo of that phrase in 21-87.” **This is the 21-87 / Kroitor / “echo” line that Chapter I did not yet quote.** Kaminski also prints a first-draft *THX 1138* exchange (n.178): THX: “…there must be something independent; a force, reality.” SRT: “You mean OMM.” THX: “Not like OMM as we know him, but the reality behind the illusion of OMM.”

## First-draft name swap, 1974 interviews, friends, side projects (pp. 80–83)

July 1974 first draft: only names change — Kane Starkiller → Akira Valor, Deak → Bink Valor, Annikin → Justin Valor, Prince Valorum → General Dodona, Jedi Bendu → Dai Noga, Sith → Legions of Lettow. Script otherwise the same; fourteen pages longer but no new scenes. Lucas later reverted to May 1974 rough-draft names. Second draft finished January 1975.

Rare 1974 **Larry Sturhahn** interview (n.179): “it’s science fiction—Flash Gordon genre; 2001 meets James Bond…” “I’m a real fan of Flash Gordon, and this is a much more plotted, structured film than the other two… Take the first two and combine them with another side of me that hasn’t been seen yet… American Graffiti wasn’t that hard to write. I did it in 3 weeks, but I’ve been working on this one for 6 months… Maybe that has to do with having to make it up… I’ll look at it and if I’m not entirely satisfied, I’ll hire somebody to do a re-write.”

*Film Quarterly,* spring 1974 (n.180): “[The Star Wars] is a space opera in the tradition of Flash Gordon and Buck Rogers. It's James Bond and 2001 combined - super fantasy, capes and swords and laser guns and spaceships shooting each other… But it's not camp. It's meant to be an exciting action adventure film.”

Parkhouse barbecues: Kurtz, Robbins, Barwood, Murch, Ritchie, Korty, sometimes the Huycks; Lucas reads the script and tape-records reactions (n.181). Footnote, Rinzler p. 24: notes survive that Lucas gave the draft to eight people — **Matthew Robbins, Hal Barwood, Bill Huyck, Gloria Katz, John Milius, Haskell Wexler, Francis Ford Coppola, Phil Kaufman.** **Michael Ritchie** (n.182): “It was very difficult to tell what the man was talking about.”

**Coppola**, 1999 conversation with Lucas (n.183): “You finished the script and then you gave it to me… I thought it was terrific. And then you totally changed it! And I kept saying ‘Why are you changing it?’ ” He liked “more outrageous ideas, such as having Princess Leia as a fourteen-year-old girl (an inspiration from Hidden Fortress).” Coppola (n.184): “George became frightened of some of his own good ideas… I think he shied away from his innovations somewhat.”

Lucas on the writing circle (n.185): Huycks and Milius “fabulous”; “having been an editor for a long time, I usually help them on their editing and they help me on my scriptwriting.”

Spielberg / Jaws mechanical-shark anecdote with Milius (n.186): Lucas pokes his head in the jaws; they stick shut; they flee. *Radioland Murders* with the Huycks, first draft July 1974, just as Lucas finished his first *Star Wars* draft (n.187). “The Adventures of Indiana Smith” with **Philip Kaufman**, 1975, then shelved (n.188); influence visible in the second draft “where Luke is introduced as an aspiring archaeologist.” *Apocalypse Now* nearly at Columbia after *Graffiti* (n.189); deal collapsed when Columbia wanted Zoetrope’s rights (n.190): “And when that deal collapsed, I started working on Star Wars.” “With this, Lucas’ Apocalypse Now was channelled into Star Wars’ rough draft.” Chapter ends: “Star Wars refused to leave Lucas’ mind and he pushed ahead with a second draft.”
