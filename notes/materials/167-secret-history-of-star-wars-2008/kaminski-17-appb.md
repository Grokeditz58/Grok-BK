# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Appendix B: Of Heroines, Wookies and Little People (pp. 468–472)

Fully read. Appendix footnotes restart at 1; cite as Kaminski App. B n. Y or Kaminski p. N. Kaminski’s “never committed to paper” conclusions are flagged as such. Staff labeled. The appendix collects “lost draft” anecdotes (female Luke, imprisoned father, mother-Wookiee storybook frame, little people, all-Japanese / Black Han) and argues they were verbal or artwork experiments, not missing scripts.

## Female Luke; “rescue the father”; Wookiee storybook (pp. 468–470)

**Mark Hamill**, *Starlog*, October 1980: “Way back in the early days…I saw some preproduction sketches and was amazed. My character was cowering behind the Princess. Originally, she was the lead character, trying to save her brother, who was on the Death Star. Then they reversed the roles.” None of the rough through fourth drafts have this plot. Second draft is close (Deak imprisoned) but the protagonist is male Luke Starkiller; Leia is a brief Utapau cameo. Rinzler: the concept “never even made it to script form.” Lucas on March 1975 (App. B n.1, *Making of Star Wars* p. 40): “The second draft didn’t really have any girls in it at all. I was very disturbed about that. I didn’t want to make a movie without any women in it. So I struggled with that, and at one point Luke was a girl. I just changed the main character from a guy to a girl.” Kaminski: McQuarrie artwork based on the second draft with Luke female; Lucas returned to a male lead when the princess came back in draft three.

Lucas to **Alan Arnold**, 1979 (App. B n.2, p. 223): “Originally, the story was about an older brother coming to find his younger brother, who’s living on a farm, so that together they can rescue their father, an old Jedi. The older brother is a battle-hardened warrior. This character evolved into Han, the other side of Luke and an older brother figure. Ben Kenobi developed from the father figure into a friend of the father.” Kaminski: “clearly confusing his own drafts”—second draft has Deak and Clieg going to Utapau to contact Luke; father (The Starkiller) summoned them for the Kiber crystal; Vader kills Clieg, imprisons Deak; Luke rescues Deak; “the father was never imprisoned.” Draft one: General Skywalker (the Kenobi figure) is imprisoned with the heroes after Annikin’s real father is killed—“perhaps this is the basis for Lucas’ confusion.” “It is not a lost draft—Lucas simply made a bit of a gaff.”

Hamill, *Starburst* 24, 1980 (App. B n.3), typography locked: “One of my favourite earlier versions of the Star Wars screenplay had a clever device to off-set the technology… It started with a helicopter shot of an enchanted forest and they push the camera through the window of a tree and you see a mother Wookiee trying to breast feed this squealing baby Wookiee… She takes the book off the shelf and we see it's titled Star Wars. She opens the book and that’s when the ship comes overhead and the film we know starts… Then, at the end, after we get our medals, we bow and it cuts back to the baby Wookiee asleep - hopefully not like the audience. And the mother closes the book and puts the baby to bed. And that would have got across that it was intended to be a fairy story.” Resemblance to the 1978 *Holiday Special* (Lucas-designed, Baxter p. 264, App. B n.4). Not in any available draft, *Annotated Screenplays*, or Rinzler. Kaminski: Hamill “does not state that he himself read it”; Lucas “never contained explicit camera movement description”; probably a verbal idea later reused as the *Holiday Special* basis. Flag as Kaminski.

## Little people, *Willow*, Japanese / Black cast (pp. 470–472)

Hamill, *SF Movieland*, 1978: “George wanted to make Star Wars before American Graffiti. Star Wars, he told me, generated from the sequel to THX-1138. The part I play is Robert Duvall. But it evolved in a different way. At one time Luke Skywalker was a girl and the princess was her brother that she was rescuing. Gary Kurtz told me that for a couple of days George wanted Luke to be played by a midget and my aunt and uncle would be midgets so that when he goes to another world they're all giants. At one time he wanted to use all Japanese actors. It goes through a lot of changes."

Lucas, 2004, *Characters of Star Wars* featurette (App. B n.5): “There was a point where Luke Skywalker was going to be a midget. And all of the people on the farm and everything, the aunt and the uncle and that whole group were all going to be little people.” Rinzler: brought up in December 1975 casting (App. B n.6, p. 102): “When I was in New York, I had done some screen tests for little people… I think that idea was a little influenced by Lord of the Rings.” Kaminski: this “morphed into 1988’s *Willow*,” “story by George Lucas.” Lucas, *Rolling Stone*, November 5th, 1987 (App. B n.7): “Then, Ron Howard is directing Willow…It’s something I’ve been working on for about ten years; I’ve [finally] managed to get all the pieces put together.” Places *Willow*’s birth “precisely around” the little-people *Star Wars* notion.

Early idea: film *THX 1138* in Japan with Japanese actors, rejected for costs (App. B n.8). Rinzler p. 69 (App. B n.9): “Lucas also considered doing the whole film in Japanese with subtitles,” for “exotic disorientation.” Scaled back to Toshiro Mifune as Obi-Wan (from General Makabe / “Mukabe” in *Hidden Fortress*), which would have made Leia Japanese. Then a Black Han Solo, “being particularly interested in Glynn Turman” (App. B n.10, Rinzler p. 124). **Dianne Crittenden**, casting director (App. B n.11): “There was talk at one point about having the princess and Ben Kenobi in Japanese, which led George into thinking Han Solo might be black.” Lucas (App. B n.12): “This was actually when I was looking for Ben Kenobi…I was going to use Toshiro Mifune; we even made a preliminary inquiry. If I’d gotten Mifune, I would’ve also used a Japanese princess, and then I would have probably cast a black Han Solo. At the same time, I was investigating Alec Guinness.”

Close: “all of these legend-like ‘Lost Drafts’ they never were actually committed to paper, as far as evidence suggests, and rather were concepts toyed with.”
