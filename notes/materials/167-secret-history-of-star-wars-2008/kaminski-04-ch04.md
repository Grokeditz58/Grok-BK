# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Chapter IV: Purgatory and Beyond (pp. 131–153)

Fully read. Citations: Kaminski p. N; Kaminski Notes n. Y (Ch. IV notes start at n.270). Kaminski glosses flagged. Staff/crew/cast statements labeled by speaker. Production, release, *Splinter*, the twelve-then-nine episode plan, and the decision to write *Empire*.

## Sequel rights, a thin Luke-trilogy arc, and a standalone fairy tale (pp. 131–136)

Second draft’s “Episode I” and end-teaser are “the first sign of potential evidence,” possibly still a serial narrative device. But contracts negotiated just before this (n.270) gave Lucas sequel rights and two more films for **Mark Hamill** and **Carrie Fisher** — “in total, he had three contractual films.” By 1976 the available threads, as Kaminski lists them, were the Han/Luke/Leia love triangle, Luke’s continuing Jedi training (a new teacher needed once Ben was rewritten to die), the Rebels vs Imperials, “Darth Vader’s revenge, Father Skywalker and Kenobi’s past, the mysterious Emperor.”

Lucas “states that he only thought about doing sequels after the film became a hit” (n.271) but also “mentions that he had sketched out a vague arc for Luke… as Luke is trained and becomes a Jedi master” (n.272). Lucas: “By the time I finished the first Star Wars, the basic ideas and plots for Empire and Jedi were also done.” (n.273) Kaminski treats this as a thin arc assembled around draft three/four background notes (n.274): new Rebel base, Luke trains and falls in love with Leia, climactic hero/villain and Rebels/Empire clash. Credence: Lucas “had to invent Yoda unexpectedly since that role was originally intended for Kenobi but Kenobi had been killed off in the first film when it was thought that Lucas might never get a chance to make subsequent entries” (n.275). December 1975 meeting with **Alan Dean Foster** outlined second and third books (examined later in the chapter) (n.276).

Vader’s survival in draft three as serial “I’ll get you yet!” (n.277, already in Ch. III via Foster) also saved the cut Luke-vs-Vader surface duel (n.278) for a sequel; *Splinter of the Mind’s Eye* “did indeed reprise this.” Kaminski’s own model: the original film’s three acts remade as a trilogy (Tatooine initiation; training + first trial vs Vader, a draw, Luke becomes a full adult/Jedi; return and slay Vader // fall of the Empire; Luke and Leia fall in love), at first within first-film or smaller budgets.

Lucas, late 1976 (n.279): “I know I’ve got a better one in me, one that is more refined… you have to walk around the world you’ve created a little bit before you can begin to know what to do in it.” At that time sequels were “to be done only as novels” (n.280), with a film option deferred until after the hit.

**Revised fourth draft made the film more self-contained.** New Death Star scene: senate dissolved; Tarkin, “Fear will keep the local systems in line—fear of this battle station.” Destroying the Death Star would collapse Imperial infrastructure. “A long time ago” tag; “basically, it’s a fairy tale now” (n.281, already in Ch. III). Luke completes his arc; at the end of the (unrevised) fourth draft **Ben announces Luke is now a Jedi Knight: “You have stepped into your father’s shoes.”** (n.282) Kenobi then killed in the revision “for dramatic arc of the film as a standalone movie.” Rinzler: production trouble forced practical rethinking (n.283). Footnote: the sequel contract still had not been drawn up while Lucas was writing the fourth draft; legally he only had the single film.

**“Episode” dilemma.** Lucas claims he always wanted the episode subtitle and the studio nixed it as confusing; “This story seems believable—though Rinzler’s exhaustive Making of Star Wars conspicuously has no documentation that this occurred, and no early draft title crawl ever carried an episode listing.” “what episode was Star Wars originally supposed to be? Episode IV, you say? Lucas himself often makes this claim, but don’t be so sure.” Backstory “wasn’t meant to be a movie” (n.284). Draft two, with similar backstory minus the father’s murder, was Episode I. The 1977 sequel was initially “Chapter II,” then the draft Lucas himself wrote “Episode II” (n.285). **The 1979 *Art of Star Wars* published script “was forged, retitled as Episode IV: A New Hope.”** The filmed revised fourth draft, March 15, 1976, is titled: “The Adventures of Luke Starkiller as taken from the ‘Journal of the Whills’ by George Lucas (Saga I) STAR WARS.” Series name = *The Adventures of Luke Starkiller/Skywalker*; “Star Wars” is the entry, not the series. *In media res.* Lucas (n.286): Saturday matinee serials; “You know, you’re in the middle of the thing and that would be the end of it. It was one movie, it just grew to be three movies, unfortunately, because I wrote more than I expected.”

Title kept as insurance (n.287–289): “something like $8 million worth of science fiction freaks… will go see absolutely anything with a title like Star Wars.” **Mark Hamill** (n.290): Fox memo that “women between the ages of 18 and 36 do not like films with the word ‘wars’ in the title”; a set contest to rename the movie produced nothing better.

## Production, Marcia’s cut, De Palma, release (pp. 136–144)

Late 1975, still un-greenlit: Lucas spent his own *Graffiti* money on storyboards, art department, cast, English stages, ILM. **Gary Kurtz** (n.291): “We decided to go ahead with the picture whether they financed it or not… It forced them to make some quick decisions.” Cameras March 22nd, 1976, Tunisia (n.292). Lucas, 1977, typography locked (n.293): “I struggled through this movie. I had a terrible time; it was very unpleasant… I was running the corporation… On Star Wars we had over 950 people… I spent all my time yelling and screaming at people, and I have never had to do that before.” Unhappy with lighting; chose elderly **Gil Taylor** (*Dr. Strangelove*, *A Hard Day’s Night*) (n.294–295). “The robots never worked. We faked the whole thing and a lot of it was done editorially.”

**John Jympson** fired; **Marcia Lucas** took over, starting the cut from scratch. Christmas: still recutting the trench run when **Martin Scorsese** called — his *New York, New York* editor had died. **Willard Huyck** (n.296): “For George the whole thing was that Marcia was going off to this den of iniquity… Marty was wild and he took a lot of drugs and he stayed up all night, had lots of girlfriends. George was a family homebody… George would fume because Marcia was running with these people. She loved being with Marty.” Late-spring exec/friends screening: no applause; Marcia burst into tears. “It’s the At Long Last Love of science fiction… It’s awful!” **Gloria Katz**: “Shhh! Laddie’s watching… Marcia, just look cheery.” (n.297) **Brian De Palma** “viciously criticized and mocked Lucas” at dinner; **Spielberg** reassured him it was a modern classic. **Paul Hirsch** and **Richard Chew** finished after Marcia left; she returned for a week before release.

Props/costumes/sets/models held for inexpensive sequels. Low-budget sequel concept: leftover Kiber crystal + *Raiders* plot (outlined with **Philip Kaufman** since 1975, n.298) — jungle treasure hunt, Luke and Leia vs Vader for the crystal; climax: falling stones injure Luke, Leia battles Vader and is badly injured, Luke duels him and sends him down a bottomless well.

**Alan Dean Foster** and *Splinter of the Mind’s Eye*, “From the further adventures of Luke Skywalker,” second story in the contractual trilogy; third book planned (n.299). Foster to **Lou Tambone**, 2002 (n.300): Lucas wanted a story filmable on a low budget, hence a fog-shrouded planet; book completed before release so fans would have material if the film succeeded; Lucas cut an elaborate opening space battle and requested a few minor changes. Later Foster interviews claim he invented most of the story (n.301); an earlier interview (n.302) says “We sat down to consciously design a book which could be filmable on a low budget.” Kaminski: *Raiders* similarities and the Kiber crystal suggest more Lucas influence than later Foster remembers. *Splinter* also prefigures *Empire*/*Jedi*: Vader kills a commanding officer; swamp planet like Dagobah; cave/spirit fight like the Empire cave; arm reconstructed; climax duel, Vader throws stones, fights Leia one-handed and taunts her, throws an energy bolt (Lightning / later Emperor). Also reprises 1973/1974 jungle-native spear-fight / train-the-natives beat later used in *Jedi*. **Harrison Ford** and **Alec Guinness** not contracted, hence no Han or Ben. Luke and Leia “obviously in love… sexual tension is a major part.” Vader’s arm cut off, “hardly any blood,” he pries the saber from the severed arm — “strongly implies that Vader is not completely natural” but still ambiguous. Book released February 1978 (n.303).

**December 1975 Lucas-to-Foster meeting, typography locked (n.304):** “I want to have Luke kiss the princess in the second book. The second book will be Gone With the Wind in Outer Space. She likes Luke, but Han is Clarke Gable. Well, she may appear to get Luke, because in the end I want Han to leave. Han splits at the end of the second book and we learn who Darth Vader is…In the third book, I want the story to be just the soap opera of the Skywalker family, which ends with the destruction of the Empire. Then someday I want to do the backstory of Kenobi as a young man—a story of the Jedi and how the Emperor eventually takes over and turns the whole thing from a Republic into an Empire, and tricks all the Jedi and kills them. The whole battle where Luke’s father gets killed. That would be impossible to do, but it’s great to dream about.” Footnote: “If this strikes you as suspiciously prophetic Appendix G has an elaboration.” Kaminski’s immediate gloss: by 1977 Ford wasn’t prepared to appear, so Han was written out of book two and the romance made straightforward; “Vader’s past, hinted at being explored in book two, was then moved into Star Wars itself” — in December 1975 the film did not yet reveal Vader as the father’s murderer; that entered the March 1976 revised fourth draft (n.305).

May 25th, 1977 release; $54 million in eight weeks. Lucas (n.306) forgot opening day while mixing foreign versions; Marcia mixing *New York, New York* at night; they ran across from the Chinese Theatre and saw the line. **Jay Cocks** / **Jeremy Kagen** anecdote: a dishevelled Ford, shirt half ripped: “I went into Tower Records to buy an album and these people jumped on me.” (n.307) Hawaii with Marcia; Ladd calls nightly grosses; Spielberg and Amy join them; sandcastle, *Raiders* offered. Frozen yogurt franchise musing (n.308). Lucas: success let him pursue “the dream for American Zoetrope” (n.309). Reviews excerpted (*Time*, Ebert, *L.A. Times*, *Variety*). **Coppola** (n.310): “the complete tapestry… was very compelling.” **Tom Pollock** (n.311–312): “The experience is not like any experience I have had since I was a child.” **Saul Zaentz** open letter in *Variety*. **Lucy Wilson** (n.313): overheard restaurant talk, magazine covers. **Harrison Ford** retracts the script jab (n.314): “I told George: ‘You can’t say that stuff. You can only type it.’ But I was wrong. It worked.”

## After the hit: Vader the unexpected star, prequels as afterthought, twelve then nine (pp. 144–151)

Lucas (n.315): “At first I was contemplating selling the whole thing to Fox… I’d just take my percentage and go home and never think about Star Wars again. But the truth of it is I got captivated by the thing. It’s in me now.” **Kurtz** (n.316): decision to go ahead “a month after Star Wars was released.” Low-budget *Splinter* shelved, book three cancelled, more extravagant stories; sequel rights would expire if production didn’t start within two years, so work began “just over six months after the release” (n.317). Kurtz, 1999 (n.318): Fox floated shooting “part of another movie” cheaply at the tail end of *Star Wars*; no script, they refused; after the opening, “find a writer, and do a proper job on this treatment material and odd notes.”

**Vader’s unexpected popularity, typography locked, pp. 145–146.** “no character was more loved than Darth Vader.” Audiences wondered what was underneath; “many believed him to be a robot or an alien, and some even that he was a beautiful man underneath the mask with gorgeous long blond hair.” Lucas: “Darth Vader became such an icon in the first film… That icon of evil sort of took over everything, much more than I intended.” (n.319) “Darth Vader was supposed to be a memorable villain, yes, but not as important as audiences made him out to be.” Hence more screentime and expanded mythos in the sequel. “This is why Darth is merely a rather minor henchman in Star Wars but becomes an all-powerful central character for the sequel, one who seemingly controls the Empire and is feared by all (as opposed to Star Wars, where he is talked down to by everyone from Princess Leia to Imperial officers and has only nine minutes of combined screentime).”

Prequel notes existed (Old Republic, Jedi, Emperor’s rise, Vader’s betrayal of Kenobi and Father Skywalker) but **“Contrary to popular belief, Star Wars was not designed with these prequels in mind, but rather they were added as afterthoughts.”** Lucas (n.320): “After the first film came out, and suddenly it was a giant hit, I said, ‘Oh, I get to do these two movies.’ Everyone said, ‘What [else] are you going to do?’ I said, ‘Gee, I could do these back stories too. That would be interesting.’ That's where the [eventual idea of] starting in episode four came [from], because I said, ‘Well, maybe I could make three out of this back story.’ That evolved right around the time the film was released, after I knew it was a success.” First public consideration of a “young days of Ben Kenobi” sequel, *Rolling Stone,* August 1977 (n.321): sequel agreements with “All the actors except Alec Guinness. We may use his voice as The Force – I don't know. One of the sequels we are thinking of is the young days of Ben Kenobi. It would probably be all different actors.” Too expensive/new-world for an immediate follow-up.

Lucas, 1977, on sequel flexibility (n.322): “if I put enough people in it and it was designed carefully enough I could make a sequel about anything. Or if any of the actors gave me lot of trouble… I could always make a sequel without one.” *Star Wars* “about 25% of what I wanted it to be” (n.323); footnote, *Charlie Rose* 2004: “In fact, it was probably 75%… but to me it felt emotionally like it was 25%.” Hospital, hypertension/exhaustion. *Rolling Stone* 1980 (n.324): “I hate directing… It’s like fighting a fifteen-round heavyweight bout with a new opponent every day.” James Bond–style franchise, other directors, he would direct the last one (n.328). **Kurtz** in the 1977 *Making of Star Wars* TV special (n.325): “different adventures rather than direct sequels.” **Hamill**, June 1978 (n.326): “It won't be a direct sequel to the first story; it'll be a series of adventures… If the Star Wars series runs as long as I think they're going to run, I will be Ben Kenobi's age when I do the last one!” **Alan Arnold**, 1978 (n.327): Kurtz described it as “a new chapter in the Star Wars saga,” “future George Lucas stories do not have chronological sequence.” Hamill, 1978 *Gossip* (n.329): “I am definitely going to do two more and they have asked me to do a fourth one… I haven't signed for it, yet.”

**Twelve-film plan, then nine.** *Time,* 1978 (n.330): “Star Wars Corp. will make STAR WARS II, and then, count them, ten other planned sequels.” Same article: eleven sequels, twenty-three years, completion 2001. Why twelve: 1930s serials were usually twelve episodes (n.331). Official *Bantha Tracks* issue 2 (n.332): “Based on the second of twelve stories in George Lucas’ Adventures of Luke Skywalker series, the first draft of the screenplay [of Empire Strikes Back] was written by Leigh Brackett.” Issue 3 (n.333): “the second of twelve stories George Lucas wrote in the Adventures of Luke Skywalker.” Last official twelve-part mention, issue 6, autumn 1979 (n.334): Kershner and Lucas keeping “to his vision for the entire twelve part saga.” Next official number, *Bantha Tracks* 8, spring 1980, Lucas himself (n.335): “I cut that number down to nine because the other three were tangential to the saga. Star Wars was the fourth story in the saga… after the third film, we’ll go back and make the first trilogy, which deals with the young Ben Kenobi and the young Darth Vader.” **Kaminski, typography locked, p. 151:** “In reality, Lucas never had a real story arc until he fused the characters of Darth Vader and Father Skywalker, thus creating the prequel trilogy and setting Star Wars up as the fourth film in a nine-film series. This will be the focus of the next chapter.”

## Ranch, Force as metaphor vs superpower, Marcia kills Ben, Brackett, treatment dated (pp. 151–153)

Late 1977: **Lawrence Kasdan** hired by Spielberg to script *Raiders* (n.336). **Irvin Kershner** (n.337): Lucas showed him Skywalker Ranch plans. “He said, ‘This is why we’re making the second [Star Wars film]. If it works, I’ll build this… If it doesn’t work, it’s over.” Lucas to Alan Arnold, 1979 (n.338): Ranch as a “funding machine” so he can return to abstract student-film work “regardless of their commerciality.”

**Obi-Wan’s death and the Force, 1977 account (n.339), typography locked:** “I was struggling with the problem that I had this sort of dramatic scene that had no climax about two-thirds of the way through the film… As I originally wrote it, Ben Kenobi and Vader had a sword fight and Ben hits a door and the door slams closed and they all run away and Vader is left standing there with egg on his face… I was rewriting, I was struggling with that plot problem when my wife suggested that I kill off Ben, which she thought was a pretty outrageous idea, and I said, ‘Well, that is an interesting idea, and I had been thinking about it.’ Her first idea was to have Threepio get shot, and I said impossible because I wanted to start and end the film with the robots… But then the more I thought about Ben getting killed the more I liked the idea because, one, it made the threat of Vader greater and that tied in with The Force and the fact that he could use the dark side. Both Alec Guinness and I came up with the thing of having Ben go on afterward as part of The Force.” In the 1977 film the voice is something “only Luke can hear—in fact, he himself is not even sure if he is hearing it.” *Rolling Stone* 1977 already quoted: “We may use his voice as the Force—I don’t know.”

Kaminski on the 1977 Force, typography locked: “cleverly written so that anyone and everyone could make it into whatever they wanted, and even Lucas did not seem to have a totally concrete conception of what it was… Essentially, it was a metaphor for believing in yourself—Luke succeeds because he believes he can.” Footnote: “In opposition to the sequels, there is no physical dimension to the Force in Star Wars—it exists only in the mind… The only uses of it as a ‘power’ are mental ones: the mind trick… while Vader’s strangulation is conveyed by a visual—the pinching of his thumb and index finger, that almost suggests that he is making the officer believe he is being choked… in Empire Vader would simply look at a person and they would keel over unexpectedly.” “When Luke reached out his hand in the opening sequence of Star Wars II… and his lightsaber… leapt into his grasp, a new threshold had been crossed in which the Force became a material superpower.”

**Leigh Brackett** hired after a friend handed Lucas an old SF novel and said, “Here is someone who wrote the cantina scene in Star Wars better than you did.” (n.340) Husband **Edmond Hamilton**; “Kaldar, Planet of Antares” (1933/1965) “lightsword” sometimes cited as a lightsaber influence. Lucas thought he was hiring a pulp writer; only in the room learned she had written *Rio Bravo*, *El Dorado*, *The Big Sleep* with Faulkner (n.341).

**November 28th, 1977:** Lucas hand-wrote the *Star Wars II* story treatment. “The film was titled *The Empire Strikes Back.*”
