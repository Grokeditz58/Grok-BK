# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Appendix G: The Tales of Jonathan Rinzler (pp. 519–525)

Fully read. Appendix footnotes restart at 1; cite as Kaminski App. G n. Y or Kaminski p. N. Kaminski’s readings of four apparent “contradictions” in Rinzler 2007 are flagged as Kaminski. Staff labeled. The appendix argues that *The Making of Star Wars*—built on Charles Lippincott’s 1975–78 “Lost Interviews” plus archives—contains no “Tragedy of Darth Vader” script and no “make Vader Luke’s father?” note, and that four passages that look like early saga-planning are later insertions or orthodox-backstory statements.

## What Rinzler adds, and what he does not find (pp. 519–520)

May 2007, thirtieth anniversary. “literally thousands of pages of interview transcriptions” Lippincott gathered 1975–1978 for an unreleased making-of. “candid and refreshing in that it reflects the original perspective of the film, untainted by success or sequels, and as a result often goes against much of the modern Lucasfilm double-speak.” Also unveiled private notes and draft conferences. Kaminski: “no major incompatibilities” with this book; new material includes “the all-important third draft outline” and “an in-depth summary of the original Journal of the Whills.” “of course there was no such fabled ‘Tragedy of Darth Vader’ script unearthed, nor any personal note of Lucas’ that pondered ‘make Vader Luke’s father?’” “this mammoth text may be the very indisputable proof against any Vader-centric story ambitions, for it details specifically and repeatedly how Lucas’ story was Luke’s and how Vader was inconsequential to it.” Notes mostly on Luke or Republic politics, to a lesser extent Han, Kenobi, and the droids; stream-of-consciousness such as “make workmen robots?” “Vader, in fact, is hardly ever mentioned at all… nothing of significance revolving around Vader, except the last draft where he kills Luke’s father,” talked about “quite a few times,” “well before the film was even released.” Private Foster meeting still outlines the sequel without a Father Vader.

## Four apparent contradictions (pp. 520–525)

**1. “Luke and the princess are twins” (Rinzler p. 42, App. G n.1).** Between second and third drafts, a six-page synopsis dated May 1, 1975; then a new outline; Luke changed from a girl back to a boy, princess resuscitated. “‘It was at that moment,’ says the writer, ‘that I came up with the idea that Luke and the princess are twins. I simply divided the character in two.’” Kaminski: “glaringly out of place”; they are still developed independently; “Lucas never once spoke of Leia as a twin before 1981,” and the line “has a very retrospective, modern-Lucas sound.” Rinzler “spoke to Lucas three times in 2006. Undoubtedly these three interviews produced this statement.” Specific counters: revised fourth draft, typography locked: "LUKE STARKILLER, a farm boy with heroic aspirations who looks much younger than his twenty years"; publicity later nineteen or eighteen; Leia is sixteen. **Dianne Crittenden** (Rinzler p. 68, n.2): “The princess was supposed to be about sixteen, Luke was about eighteen, and Han was in his early twenties.” Underage union laws required both Luke and Leia over eighteen; Ford’s casting made Han late twenties rather than an early-twenties “James Dean.” Lucas also says Leia has two younger brothers, ages four and seven (Rinzler p. 351). Sequel talk in 1975 is a Luke–Leia romance, *Gone With the Wind* in Outer Space; Foster’s *Splinter* is “rife with sexual tension”; *Empire* treatment and first draft still have Luke in love with Leia; open-mouth kiss in the film. The statement “does have some merit” only as metaphor. Lucas to Arnold, 1979 (n.4, pp. 222–23): “All the characters came out of one composite—Luke… At one point I was going to have a girl in the center. Luke Skywalker might never have been; he might have been a heroine. Leia came out of Luke, so to speak, just as Han did, as the opposite of Luke. Han Solo evolved from my wanting to have a cynical foil for the innocent Luke.” **John Mollo** (Rinzler p. 192, n.5): “George said, ‘No, I think [Luke] ought to look a bit more like Han [for the medal scene].’ It was a very last minute thing, but we concocted an outfit like Han’s in different colors.” Lucas (Worrell p. 182, n.6): “Han Solo and Luke are like twin brothers, the spiritual brother and the warrior brother with the devil-may care attitude.”

**2–3. Foster, December 1975 (Rinzler p. 107, n.3, n.7), typography locked:** “I want to have Luke kiss the princess in the second book. The second book will be Gone With the Wind in Outer Space. She likes Luke, but Han is Clarke Gable. Well, she may appear to get Luke, because in the end I want Han to leave. Han splits at the end of the second book and we learn who Darth Vader is...In the third book, I want the story to be just about the soap opera of the Skywalker family, which ends with the destruction of the Empire. Then someday I want to do the backstory of Kenobi as a young man—a story of the Jedi and how the Emperor eventually takes over and turns the whole thing from the Republic into an Empire, and tricks all the Jedi and kills them. The whole battle where Luke’s father gets killed. That’s impossible to do, but it’s great to dream.” Kaminski: “we learn who Vader is” is not a father reveal—it is Vader’s origin as Kenobi’s student who betrayed the Jedi and killed Luke’s father, “in the same breath” as “Luke’s father is killed.” Private meeting with the sequel author; he even spoils that Luke, not Han, gets Leia. At the moment of the remark, the father “just dies anonymously in battle”; the “we learn who Darth Vader is” beat may have been the December 1975 plan to reveal Vader as the killer in the sequel, then moved into March’s revised fourth draft “because he might only make the first film” (these “sequels” were Foster novels; the Fox sequel contract was not yet final). *Splinter*, the late-1977 *Empire* treatment, and Brackett’s draft have no Father Vader. Speculation, flagged: the unused third-draft-outline Death Star duel was saved for a sequel so Vader could reveal he killed the father; once that fact entered the first film, “the Vader confrontation in Star Wars II had much less weight… thus Lucas gave Vader another revelation for Luke about his father.” “Soap opera of the Skywalker family” = Luke avenging Father Skywalker with his father’s saber; reinforced in *Empire* draft one and *Splinter*. Ghost-father and 1977 twin-sister may or may not have been in mind in 1975; “nothing to suggest as much.”

**4. The Duel / iron lung (Rinzler p. 111, n.8), typography locked:** “The backstory is about Ben and Luke's father and Vader, when they are young Jedi Knights. Vader kills Luke's father, then Ben and Vader have a confrontation, just like they have in Star Wars, and Ben almost kills Vader. As a matter of fact, he falls into a volcanic pit and gets fried and is one destroyed being. That's why he has to wear the suit with a mask, because it's a breathing mask. It's like a walking iron lung. His face is all horrible inside. I was going to have a close-up of Vader where you could see the inside of his face, but then we said, ‘No, no, it would destroy the mystique of the whole thing.’” Kaminski: “This, however, is a word for word copy from Paul Scanlon's August 25th, 1977 interview with Lucas for Rolling Stone”; Rinzler lists that interview as a source. “there still is no evidence of it being in place before 1976.” Rinzler placed it here because Ben ceases to be mechanical in the fourth draft (so Vader becomes the mechanoid) and because Lucas says he didn’t develop Vader until the fourth draft—but that last point “is in reference to his past”: Jedi student and betrayer already in the third draft; murderer of Luke’s father only in the revised fourth.

Close: aside from these three or four statements, Rinzler’s book is “honest, thoroughly researched, expertly written.”
