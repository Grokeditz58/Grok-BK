# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Chapter V: Revelations (pp. 154–217)

Fully read. Citations: Kaminski p. N; Kaminski Notes n. Y (Ch. V notes start at n.342). Kaminski glosses flagged. Staff/crew/cast statements labeled by speaker. This is the book’s central chapter: five numbered “revelations,” the April 1978 father fusion, the Emperor’s transformation, the twelve-to-nine plan, the forged *Art of Star Wars* script, and the “master plan” as a security blanket.

Kaminski’s five revelations, typography locked in substance:
1. There was no prequel trilogy yet. *Empire* was Chapter II / Episode II, not V. (pp. 164–165)
2. In Brackett’s first draft (Feb 23, 1978), the ghost of Father Skywalker appears on Dagobah. Vader and the father are still separate. (p. 165)
3. In Lucas’s April 1978 second draft, Vader says “I am your father.” This merger creates the prequel trilogy and later changes *Empire* to Episode V. (pp. 166–169)
4. Leia was never to be Luke’s sister. The “Other” line is a Sequel Trilogy setup, later plugged with Leia. (pp. 184–194)
5. Vader was not yet to be redeemed; the third film’s original title, *Revenge of the Jedi*, implied vengeance. (p. 193)

## Story conferences, treatment, Brackett draft: father still separate (pp. 154–165)

Unlike *Star Wars*, early *Empire* drafts share the same construction: Hoth, split heroes, Yoda training, Bespin duel. Conferences with **Leigh Brackett**, Nov 28–Dec 2, 1977. Kaminski, citing a “personal report” of the Magic of Myth Smithsonian exhibit: the dated November 28 treatment is “a culmination of the story conferences,” starting as handwritten notes from the first day. Main evidence: **Laurent Bouzereau**, *The Annotated Screenplays* — Yoda first proposed in conferences as “the critter,” named “Minch Yoda” only in the treatment, so the treatment came second. Opening scene discussed at length (n.342): helicopter shot, two men on a snow lizard, beast kills one; Lucas then turned them into Luke and Han and dated that opening Nov 28, withholding the rest until later.

Conferences decided Emperor and the Force as the two opposing issues. Emperor “virtually non-existent in Star Wars,” now to be dealt with in person, **still a Nixonian bureaucrat**, “a Wizard of Oz-type person,” a master manipulator (n.343). Vader to be shown in a black castle surrounded by lava, gargoyles and gremlins (n.344) — “a far cry from the petty bickering with Tarkin.” Emperor on a communication screen, “caped and hooded in a cloth of gold,” has felt a disturbance because of Luke’s training. **Joe Johnston** sketched the castle; Lucas said he would save it for future episodes (n.345).

Yoda (n.346): “an Indian desert type, very childlike… at first should be repulsive and slimy… a crazy little nitwit that goes around scurrying like a rat.” Sounds, Kaminski notes, like third-draft Ben. Lucas on the necessity of Yoda (n.347): Kenobi “really didn’t serve any useful function after the point he fights with Darth Vader… I had Luke being trained by Ben only I had killed Ben off, so now I had to come up with another Jedi, who was older and wiser and shorter than Ben to train Luke.” Appearance (n.348): about twenty-eight inches, slightly froglike, slick skin, wide mouth, no nose, bulbous eyes, thin spidery arms, floppy webbed feet, “personality of a Muppet,” still called “The Critter.” Lessons as “proverbs or commandments” (n.349). Force discussion (n.350), typography locked: “the dark side of the Force… should be the real villain of the story… The danger, the jeopardy is that Luke will become Vader… If he uses too much of the Force, it will start using him. For example, to lift objects Luke has to use the bad side of the Force… When Luke fights, he has to use the dark side, but he is also using the good side for protection… by the end of the film Luke should have become Ben.” Other planets brainstormed: water planet, garden planet, “city planet” as Imperial home (n.351).

**Han backstory in conferences (n.352), typography locked:** “Han is an orphan and was raised by Wookies on their planet. He left, flunked out of the Space Academy, and then met some kind of Ernest Hemingway character, a very powerful trader in the galaxy who took Han under his wing until they had a falling out. Han swore he’d never talk to him again.” The Alliance needs this man, who “controls all nonmilitary transports… head of some sort of transport guild”; Leia says he’ll talk only to Han, or Han alone knows where he is hiding. Han at first refuses, then agrees; the mission is aborted when the Empire attacks. Footnote: being raised by Wookiees “had existed since the first film (see Rinzler)”; the trader figure is new. Left out of Lucas’s treatment; Brackett put it in her first draft.

**Lando** first discussed as “a new Han Solo character” (n.353): “slick, riverboat gambler dude… elegant, sort of like James Bond”; possible girl/alien sidekick, or gambler running a store on the Wookiee planet (n.354). Clone-Wars beat attached to Lando (n.355): “Leia doesn’t trust him because of the war that practically wiped out his species. He could be one of the last clones… He came from a planet of clones; the planet had maybe seven hundred different countries, and each country was composed of a clone clan and he was the ruler of one of the clans.” In treatment and first draft, then dropped.

Climax ideas (n.356–357): play the fight “like a seduction, a temptation; the audience knows that Luke is not going to die, so the ultimate hook is the fear that Luke might turn to the darkside.” Vader’s telekinesis created here; worry about why he didn’t use it on Ben in the first film, “easily explained by the fact that Ben was probably stronger than Vader.” Force levels: “maybe Ben was a six, Vader was a four, and Luke is now at level two.” Pipe-bending duel idea.

Nineteen-page handwritten treatment (n.358), “crude and briskly developed (with many spelling and grammar mistakes).” Opening printed pp. 159–160 (n.359): Hoth, taun taun, walkie-talkie, snow creature “bashes Luke in the face.” Hamill 1977 car-accident / plastic-surgery theory for the scarring is doubted because Lucas had the opening in mind before it was attached to Luke. Han tells Leia he must leave on a mission; “comes on to Leia,” she rejects him “as a rogue” but “gets a sparkle in her eye.” Motivation still vague on page two — probably written before the trader-mentor subplot was invented. Luke in the lair “fumbling with the use of the force”; fingers “a talsman around his neck” that belonged to Ben, with markings naming a planet; no Force-ghost appearance of Ben. **Luke/Leia romance “much more pronounced… further reinforcing the notion that they were obviously not supposed to be related.”** Luke proclaims his love; Leia says a relationship is impossible because of her duties (Kaminski: Lucas would write a similar scene for *Attack of the Clones*); survives to the shooting script. Vader later uses Leia as bait, “telling her that he knows Luke is in love with her.” Recovery-room kiss printed p. 160.

Yoda (n.360): Ben gave Luke the talisman so he could find him; won’t give starving Luke food until he starts learning. Vader’s grim black-iron castle on a rock in a crimson sea, feeding gargoyles from a golden bowl, feels a disturbance — in the first draft, not the treatment (n.361). Han remembers friend **Lando Kadar** on a gas planet; Leia doesn’t trust him (later learned he is a clone). Luke dreams of Vader, leaves as a Jedi knight, locates Leia by the Force; tempted, refuses, jumps into a debris chute, rescued by the Falcon. Ending on a beautiful jungle garden planet (n.362): Han, Chewie, and Lando leave; “Han gives Leia a long kiss”; Falcon into twin suns behind Luke, Leia, and the robots. “The adventure would continue in the next chapter…”

**Kaminski, typography locked, p. 161:** “You will notice that there is no ‘I am your father revelation’ in Lucas’ outline. Nor would there be in the first draft screenplay. This is the most crucial development in all of Star Wars’ story history.” Tone still closer to *Star Wars* than the sepulchral final *Empire*; relatively light, resolved ending; “a self-contained adventure film.”

Foundation: Kurosawa’s *Dersu Uzala* (1975, Best Foreign Language Film) for Hoth/Dagobah and Yoda (tiny Asiatic hermit, childlike, broken backwards language, spiritual guide; blizzard rescue). Recycled: *Splinter* Luke-vs-Vader; 1974 rough-draft asteroid chase; earlier-draft cloud-city prison of Alderaan as Lando’s Cloud City; *Gone With the Wind* romance, “even containing identical dialog from Margaret Mitchell’s novel” (n.363). Primary theme still “Luke facing his destiny, and the ultimate conquering and slaying of Vader would represent his final triumph as a Jedi and the just retribution of his father’s murder.” Treatment: Vader reaches into the Force to choke Luke as he flees Hoth; Luke jumps to hyperspace.

Clone Wars: first attached to Lando’s clone planet, then dropped; alternate, “an army from a remote part of the galaxy that attacked the Republic,” tied to **Boba Fett**. *Bantha Tracks*, summer 1979 (n.364): “He wears part of the uniform of the Imperial Shocktroopers, warriors from the olden time. Shocktroopers came from the far side of the galaxy and there aren’t many of them left. They were wiped out by the Jedi knights during the Clone Wars.” *Empire* novelisation, **Donald F. Glut** (n.365): Fett in “the kind [of armored spacesuit] worn by a group of evil warriors defeated by the Jedi Knights during the Clone Wars.” Stormtroopers later revealed as clones in 1981’s *The World of Star Wars* (n.366): “A cloned man… He has no mother; only his trainers… A clone has no human rights and no name. He is the property of the Emperor.” Contradicts 1977 implication of academy recruits; Lucas, 1977 (n.367): “Some of the stormtroopers are women, but there weren’t that many women assigned to the Death Star.” Kaminski’s circa-1979 reconstruction: Shocktroopers including Fett invade from far away; Republic creates its own clone stormtrooper army led by Jedi; survivors including Fett become bounty hunters. Possible first hint of moving the Clone Wars later so Fett and (after 1981, a roughly 65-year-old) Vader can both participate; original timeline placed the wars “roughly fifty years prior.” *AotC* “Boba Fett might have been a stormtrooper” is, Kaminski says, an EU overlay (Jaster Mereel), not the 1970s shocktrooper concept (n.368).

Brackett finishes first draft February 23rd, 1978; dies of cancer March 18th. “essentially identical to the treatment with a few notable elaborations.”

**Revelation 1, typography locked, pp. 164–165:** “there was no prequel trilogy. Star Wars was not the fourth entry but the first… During story meetings between Brackett and Lucas, the film was identified as ‘Chapter II, The Empire Strikes Back’ (n.369), and by the time the second draft was finished, the familiar episode listing was in place. However, it was not Episode V… the opening crawl read ‘Episode II The Empire Strikes Back.’ ” (n.370) Footnote: possible change from “Chapter” to “Episode” because Neil Simon’s *Chapter Two* (1979). After the second draft the film would be known as Episode V. Kenobi backstory films “would either continue in the episode listing, which was not necessarily progressing in chronological sequence at the time of the first draft as Gary Kurtz explained (n.371), or they would not be part of the ‘Star Wars’ saga, perhaps simply a spin-off.” Lucas’s “One of the sequels” wording “appears to be a single, standalone movie.” “It is abundantly clear that at this time Darth Vader was still not Father Skywalker.” Marvel annual: Obi-Wan, Vader, and Father Skywalker together as a threesome. They remain separate in the treatment, conferences, and first draft. Claim that Lucas invented the fusion in 1977 is “easily refuted (see appendix ‘Dark Father’).” Genesis: after reading Brackett’s late-February draft, before finishing his own second draft in April — “around March.”

**Revelation 2, typography locked, p. 165:** “in the first draft of Empire Strikes Back, Father Skywalker’s ghost appears to Luke! … The ghost of Luke’s dead Jedi father appears while Luke is training on Dagobah and gives Luke advice. Naturally, when Luke finally faces Darth there is no ‘father revelation’—he beckons Luke to join the darkside, Luke refuses, Vader attacks Luke and Luke jumps off the ledge; the point of Luke’s confrontation with Darth is that he refuses the darkside. Father Skywalker is described as ‘a tall, fine looking man,’ and is referred to only as ‘Skywalker.’ Luke takes the oath of the Jedi from his father.”

## April 1978: “I am your father”; prequels formed; Emperor becomes a Sith (pp. 166–180)

Lucas “was not happy with the script.” “During story conferences with Leigh, my thoughts weren’t full formed and I felt that her script went in a completely different direction.” (n.372) **Lawrence Kasdan**: “It was sort of old-fashioned… The character’s all had the right names, but the story’s spirit was different.” **Kurtz** (*Starlog*, July 1987): changes “rather minor”; “She was going to do two drafts and a polish.” Lucas called in early March; she was already in hospital; died a few days later (n.373). Lucas (n.374): “I didn’t like the first draft script, but I gave Leigh credit because I liked her a lot. She was sick at the time she wrote the script, and she really tried her best.” Easter 1978 (March 26) Mexico vacation with **Michael Ritchie** and his wife; Lucas holed up in the hotel writing (n.375–377): “I found it much easier than I'd expected, almost enjoyable.”

**Revelation 3.** In this draft the father’s ghost does not appear. On the 128th page of the handwritten second draft, typography as printed (n.378):

Luke: “Enough! He said you killed him.”
Vader: “I am your father.”
“The two have been battling all through this dialog. Luke pulls away at this revelation. The two warriors stand staring at one another; father and son.”
Luke: “That’s impossible. It’s not true.”
Vader: “Search your feelings you already know it to be true. Join me”

**Kaminski’s explanation, typography locked, pp. 167–169:** “it was the easiest story solution… based more on convenience, and was in many ways a natural progression.” Brackett’s draft made the redundancy material “by the introduction of Ben Kenobi and Father Skywalker together as ghosts.” “Look at the history of Darth Vader and Father Skywalker: there are enormous parallels! Both were friends with Ben Kenobi, both were Jedi, both have a mysterious past and one kills the other… Similarly, both Ben Kenobi and Father Skywalker were Jedi, both are father figures to Luke, both want to mentor him, both were friends with each other and both were killed by Darth Vader… Obi Wan was created to replace Father Skywalker.” “make Father Skywalker and Darth Vader the same person! It was a great twist, and it was so obvious, just sitting there waiting to happen.” Footnote: Lucas admits a merger “on one rare occasion” in the 2004 DVD featurette “The Characters of Star Wars,” but “reverts back to the usual story, implying that this occurred in early drafts for the first film”: “The good father was Annikin Starkiller and the bad villain was Darth Vader… Ultimately they merged into being one character.” He then wrote the second, revised second, and third drafts all in April 1978, “compare that with the year-long struggles he endured for Star Wars.” New history: “Obi Wan and Father Skywalker were master-student, Skywalker fell to the darkside, battled his former master and was horribly wounded before being resurrected as Darth Vader.” Shift “from the Flash Gordon-type ‘Serial’ style to a more epic Dune-type ‘Saga,’ from a storybook-like tale of good versus evil to a complicated chronicle of temptation and redemption. With the second draft of Empire Strikes Back, George Lucas created the basis for the Star Wars Saga.” Lucas (n.379): “the story itself takes over and the characters take over… it sends you down some very funny paths.” Comic-book and myth father-villain precedents listed (Green Goblin, Fu Manchu, Darkseid, Perseus, Oedipus, Cronus). “This is when the prequel trilogy was formed… which prompted Lucas to change Empire Strikes Back from Episode II to Episode V.” New tale: master-student, Clone Wars, Republic falls, Skywalker succumbs, is wounded and resurrected as Vader, exterminates the Jedi; Obi-Wan hides the son; Yoda and Kenobi in exile.

**Emperor transformed with the merger.** In the original film: Nixon-like politician, corrupt, bribing senators, rigging elections; Vader joined as a minion along with the rest of the Sith. 1976 novelisation “even implied that the weasely Emperor was no long in control, and was instead being manipulated behind the scenes by his own advisors” — some attribute this to Foster, but Kaminski notes a remnant in *TPM* (Valorum controlled by bureaucrats; Palpatine: “Enter the bureaucrats, the true rulers of the Republic”). Novelisation Kenobi (n.380): “Vader used the training I gave him and the force within him for evil, to help the later corrupt Emperors.” Implies multiple, once-good Emperors; roots in the first *Star Wars* draft (benign Empire, Cos Dashit “a thin, grey looking man, with an evil moustache”). Elected ruler also called Emperor, analogised to Queen Amidala; novel prologue also says Palpatine was elected President; Lucas by mid-1977 “had privately begun to call the Republic ruler a ‘chancellor’” (n.381). Plural “Emperors” would mean Palpatine “was no longer the Emperor and was likely killed nearly two decades earlier,” subsequent ones “figurehead puppets.” Prologue printed in full, p. 172 (n.382), “From the First Saga / Journal of the Whills” — “this larger chronicle of course does not exist… See Appendix A.” Novel Ben on Owen/father (n.383): father a Tatooine farmer who ran off on an “idealistic crusade”; “I’m afraid there wasn’t much of a farmer in your father.” **Geoffrey McKinney** (n.384): 1977 crawl/film logic that destroying the Death Star “restored freedom to the galaxy” by collapsing Imperial infrastructure after the senate was dissolved.

Theory that the Emperor was already a Sith in draft two (unseen Valorum = unseen Emperor) “holds little weight”; Valorum lives in the Alderaan dungeon, Emperor on the capital. Second-draft opening: “Ruthless trader barons… have replaced enlightenment with oppression…” Senate corruption, not a single planned takeover; “Holy Rebellion of ’06.” After the hit, Lucas privately shifts the Emperor from figurehead to puppet master who manipulates the bureaucracy, and Vader from one of a Sith group (SS analogy) to “the Emperor’s emissary” (n.385). Long August 1977 talk to Lucasfilm’s **Carol Titleman** printed pp. 176 (n.386): 24,372 systems; Chancellor one four-year term; one Chancellor buys a second term “because of a crisis,” then is made Emperor for life; bureaucracy blackmailed into line; uncorrupted half of the Senate tries to impeach him; “Many of the Senators who were fighting the Emperor at that time mysteriously died… when the Jedi finally rallied and tried to restore order, they were betrayed and eventually killed by Darth Vader.” Brackett draft still “envisioned as a bureaucrat, Nixonian in his outlook and sort of a Wizard of Oz-type person” (n.387), gold cloak, but he can feel a disturbance. **“he doesn’t become the shadowy, black-hooded sorcerer until the second draft of Empire Strikes Back—when Father Skywalker was merged with Darth Vader.”**

Why the father turned: earlier Vader was one-dimensional, “a bit of a bad seed,” no seduction (Sith come to the Emperor). Now the Emperor must lure him, “necessitated that the Emperor himself was a Sith Lord.” Lucas “brought back the Sith Master character of Valorum… combining him with the Emperor” — later creating the Sidious/Palpatine dual-persona problem. *Time,* 1980 (n.388): “A sorcerer, a bad counterpart of Yoda, blocked all opposition and declared himself Emperor… The Emperor subverts Darth Vader to his side, and together he and Vader betray the other Knights, nearly all of whom are killed in their trap. Ben Kenobi escapes, and after a fierce struggle he does such injury to Vader that forever after Vader must wear a mask and that noisy life-support system. The fall of the republic and the rise of the empire will form the first of Lucas' three trilogies.” 1977: “at the height of the Jedi, there were several hundred thousand” (n.389); they tried to re-group and were massacred by Vader and elite special forces (n.390). Possible *Dune* / Templars 1307 echoes.

Issues created: is Owen really the uncle? mother? does Vader know he has a son? Fitting it into the 1977 film: Owen/Beru “worked even better” as fear of Luke turning evil and despising Obi-Wan as a failed teacher. Ben’s tale: “Lucas decided that he could make it seem as though Ben was lying to protect Luke, and that in a sense, Father Skywalker metaphorically ‘died’ when he became Darth Vader. It was a bit of a stretch… The confusion… could also create suspense… It also gave Lucas the option of backing out if he changed his mind after completing the film.”

Lucas, 1981, on the prequels (n.391): “the next trilogy—the first one—since it’s about Ben Kenobi as a young man… Luke ends up in the third film of the first trilogy just three-and-a-half years old… The first trilogy will not be as much of an action adventure kind of thing… much more humorless… more Machiavellian—it’s all plotting—more of a mystery. I think we’ll try, on the next one, to write all three scripts at once.” **Hamill** (n.392): “I did ask what happened to my parents during Star Wars. If I remember, he gave me a really detailed answer which turned out to be completely different when I got the Empire script... He told there was a great duel between Vader and Obi-Wan, and that Vader had fallen into a volcanic pit and was hideously burned beyond recognition... But, I always wondered if it was true that he really had all nine parts written out…” Second-draft scarring variant, Bouzereau (n.393): “Ben fought Vader and pushed him down a nuclear reactor shaft. One of his arms was severed… Vader survived and became a mutant.” “the familiar volcano duel was eventually reinstated” in *Jedi* (cut from the final film). Psychologists advised Lucas that kids who couldn’t accept the revelation would believe Vader is lying. **Irvin Kershner** (n.394): children six to eight say “Darth Vader’s not really his father, is he”; from about nine they accept it.

Lucas also freezes Han in carbonite (previous draft: Han escaped and left in search of his adoptive stepfather). “These two story points all led into the next film.” The film becomes “a totally different animal”: ensemble split, droids broken up, “Han got the girl instead of Luke,” Emperor a sorcerer, “Obi Wan became a questionable liar,” Vader “seemingly controlled the universe,” downer ending. **Kasdan** on Lucas’s draft (n.395): “a very rough first draft, really somewhere between an outline and a first draft. The structure of the story was all there—it was the skeleton for a movie. What was needed was the flesh and the muscle.” Pollock footnote (n. * p. 181): editorial cross-cutting, physical humour, Falcon hyperdrive, bickering Han and Leia added in Lucas’s draft.

## Kasdan, Kershner, the “Other,” twelve to nine, five more revelations (pp. 181–194)

**Kershner** selected late 1977 from Kurtz’s list; Lucas promised, “It’ll be your film.” (n.396) Kasdan hired after turning in *Raiders*, before Lucas had even read it: “I was desperate. I didn’t have anybody else.” (n.397) Kasdan, twentieth anniversary (n.398): Lucas, “Look, Leigh Brackett has died, and I want you to write Empire.” Kasdan: “Don’t you think you ought to read Raiders first?” Lucas: “Well, if I read it tonight and I hate it, I’ll withdraw the offer.” He liked it. “They were under the gun… already building the monsters… I wrote it really fast.” November 1978 Parkhouse conferences, Pollock (n.399): tape recorder, two weeks, script “no more than 105 pages: ‘short and tight.’” Lucas’s two rules: “speed and clarity.” “The trick is to know what you can leave to the audience’s imagination.” Kasdan’s main criticism: Lucas “glossed over the emotional content” (n.400). Lucas: “Well, if we have enough action, nobody will notice.” (n.401) Kurtz: “He’s afraid of going too slow.” (n.402) Pollock: poor dialog, thin characterisation (n.403); example, Han to Leia: “Don’t worry, I’m not going to kiss you here. You see, I’m quite selfish about my pleasure, and it wouldn’t be much fun for me now.” Kasdan: “There were sections… ‘I can’t believe George wrote this scene. It’s terrible.’ ” (n.404) First twenty-five pages “promptly tore apart” (n.405). Zen overtones “did not become as pronounced until the Buddhists Kasdan and Kershner began reshaping the material.” Kershner (n.406): “I wanted them to see the expression of a lot of their anxieties, fears, and nightmares and offer a way to deal with them.” Kasdan on styles (n.407): Lucas “doesn't care about the relationships between people beyond the broad strokes… What he’s interested in is moving the plot forward.” Kershner (n.408): “I kept thinking in terms of character, George was thinking more in terms of the actual story, and then Larry was thinking of dialog.”

*Future* magazine, 1978, both drafts apparently leaked (n.409): “One version… a young, handsome Darth turning rogue Jedi, killing Luke Skywalker's father and being pushed into a pool of molten lava by avenging angel Ben Kenobi… The second version portrays Darth as being, in reality, Luke Skywalker's father.”

**Twin sister in Brackett draft only.** Father’s ghost tells Luke he has a twin sister, won’t reveal where, lest Vader find her. Conferences (n.410): father took one child to an uncle and the other “to a remote place on the other side of the universe”; she might be in Jedi training; “in another episode, the two of them would fight side by side… perhaps avenging their slain father.” Echo of draft-two’s many sons and the briefly female Luke. **“in the infamous second draft there is no mention of a sister or even a mysterious ‘Other.’”** Revised second: Yoda, “Now we must find another”; Obi-Wan, “He is our only hope.” Kaminski rejects the common report that a twin-sister-from-across-the-galaxy plot ran 1977–80 and was hastily replaced by Leia in Episode VI. Lucas on the later “Other” line (n.411): “I was trying to set up subliminally in the audience’s mind that something is going on here, that [Luke] could fail. And if he does fail, ‘there is another hope.’” 2004 DVD commentary: “It sets up the fact that, in this series, Luke could be expendable at this point… ‘He’s not the important one—there is another.’ It’s a cheap trick but it works.” Third draft: “No…we must search for another.” Fourth/fifth: “No, there is another” — “someone already out there, available and ready.” Kaminski: Lucas “didn’t even know whom Yoda was referring to,” but the line “had to have been meant for a sequel set-up”; later denial that sequels existed is “as ridiculous a claim as it may be.” Most logical: another Force-user only Yoda knew of, perhaps the Sequel Trilogy protagonist, “perhaps even a youngster at the time of the middle trilogy.”

**Twelve to nine.** First draft era still twelve (*Time*, March 1978, around the first draft). By July 1979, on set, nine-film “trilogy of trilogies” with twenty-year gaps (n.412–413). Practical: Fisher and Hamill three-film contracts; Ford and Guinness movie-by-movie; the Rebels-vs-Empire story “had to resolve itself by the third film.” **Kurtz’s later “hexology” (Luke IV–IX, sister in VII/VIII, Emperor in IX, then crammed into VI after *Empire*’s troubles) “never existed”** — “a confused recollection of disparate and separate concepts. See appendix Tales of Gary Kurtz.” Counter-evidence: while *Empire* was still filming, Lucas tells **Alan Arnold**, July 19, 1979 (n.414), and elaborates October 29 after wrap (n.415), typography locked: “There are essentially nine films in a series of three trilogies. The first trilogy is about the young Ben Kenobi and the early life of Luke’s father when Luke was a little boy… About a year or two passes between each story of the trilogy and about twenty years pass between the trilogies. The entire saga spans about fifty-five years… After the success of Star Wars I added another trilogy… The next chapter is called ‘Revenge of the Jedi.’ It’s the end of this particular trilogy, the conclusion of the conflict begun in Star Wars between Luke and Darth Vader. It resolves the situation once and for all.” July 19 claim that “The first script was one of six original stories I had written in the form of two trilogies… The original two trilogies were conceived of as six films of which the first film was number four” is flagged as mixed with falsehoods. Sequel Trilogy “did exist, for sure in 1979 and 1980 and likely in 1978”; plot “very little—it is likely that Lucas himself only had vague ideas” (n.416); “the re-building of the Republic,” “the necessity for moral choices” (n.417); “passing on what you have learned” (n.418). “Contrary to popular belief, the Sequel Trilogy was not supposed to follow a grey-haired Luke and company with the aged original cast” — that was “a later addition used as an alternate cover-up.” Fuller treatment deferred to Appendix D.

Timeline of major revisions printed pp. 190–192 (pre-1977 standalone with optional sequels / Foster books; summer 1977 infinite franchise + young Kenobi sequel; Nov 1977 Chapter II treatment; Feb 23 Brackett draft; March 6 *Time* twelve episodes, March 18 Brackett dies; April 1978 father fusion, no sister, still possibly Episode II on artwork until after the fourth draft, n.419; Oct 1978 / Feb 1979 fourth and fifth drafts, fifth is first confirmed “Episode V,” n.420, “No, there is another”; July 19 / Oct 29 1979 nine-film statements; Nov 1979 forged *Art of Star Wars* script).

Third-film goals as of 1979: Luke defeats Vader; Emperor revealed and dealt with; Rebels victorious; Dagobah “does appear in the next film” (n.422) though Lucas’s initial draft did not feature it. If Ford refused, Han could die and Lando, already in Solo’s costume piloting the Falcon, could replace him. Hamill to Arnold, 1979 (n.424): “George has left him in limbo… ‘He’s in a state of perfect hibernation.’ So George has given himself an option.” Kurtz’s later “Han dies and Leia becomes the Queen of her people” (n.423) “not to be taken as gospel.” Droids in all three trilogies (n.425). Hamill approached years earlier about more than three, “for the much different conception,” not renewed after *Empire* wrapped (n.426). 1983: Hamill “could play an elderly version of himself if he looked old enough” (n.427), or recast with older actors (n.428). Interviews around this time: Luke as a three-year-old in the third prequel (n.429), “perhaps implying… Vader may have known he had a son from the very beginning and had raised him for a few years before his turn.”

**Revelation 4, typography locked, p. 193:** “Leia was never to be Luke’s sister.” The “Other” became a lingering plot point once the Sequel Trilogy was eliminated and “Lucas simply plugged Leia into the role because there wasn’t anyone else.”

**Revelation 5, typography locked, p. 193:** “despite the fact that Darth Vader was now revealed to be Luke’s father, it appears he was not to be redeemed… Vader was to be destroyed, along with the Empire, and it wasn’t in a redeeming way; as the original title implied, Revenge of the Jedi, Luke would have his vengeance. Vader’s eventual redemption would stem from a by-product of the resolution of his paternity… This will be elaborated upon in the chapter on Return of the Jedi.” Fatherhood “could be played either way… supposedly, Lucas considered this.” Han’s fate, Vader’s parentage, and the “Other” all left open-ended. “Once again, Lucas had covered all his bases…just in case.”

## Forged script, “master plan,” Campbell, “that’s the way it always was” (pp. 194–217)

By February 1979 fifth draft, *Empire* is Episode V. 1979 subtitle *A New Hope*, Episode IV; series renamed from *The Adventures of Luke Skywalker* to *Star Wars*. First public introduction: November 1979 *Art of Star Wars*. **The published script “is an outright forgery,”** titled *Star Wars Episode IV A New Hope from the Journal of the Whills*, “revised fourth draft january 15th, 1976.” Real filmed script: *The Adventures of Luke Starkiller… (Saga I) STAR WARS*, Revised Fourth Draft, March 15, 1976. “the script itself wasn’t even the authentic revised fourth draft but more like a transcription of the final film, edited and combined with the real fourth draft.” “a new threshold… the changes made to the story were actively covered up.” Kaminski defends “forgery” and “cover-up” as definitionally appropriate. Pattern of “consistent misleadings.” Special Editions and “The Tragedy of Darth Vader” framed as a later extension of the same process.

Official story shifted: twelve stories, then nine, then after *Jedi* always six; then in the prequel era “never wrote any stories… merely a vague, ten-page outline of the prequels and that the sequels never even existed.” Alan Arnold interview (n.430): “I have story treatments on all nine… Originally, when I wrote Star Wars, it developed into an epic on the scale of War and Peace… I cut it in half, but it was still too big, so I cut each half into three parts. I then had material for six movies. After the success of Star Wars I added another trilogy.” “nothing even remotely close to this history is true,” except adding the third trilogy after the hit. Also (n.431): “The first [script] was about Luke’s father, and the second one took place at a later time and was mainly about Luke” — Kaminski: the rough draft is an early version of the same film, same time period. *American Cinematographer*, June 1980 (n.432): “I chose the chapter I felt most secure with and which I liked the most.”

Lucas on not telling Brackett (n.433): “I didn’t discuss the notion of Vader being Luke’s father with Leigh Brackett. At that point I wasn’t sure if I was going to include it in that script or reveal it in the third episode… rather than confuse things for Leigh, I decided to keep the whole issue out of the mix.” **Kaminski rejects this at length:** he discussed the twin sister, Lando’s Clone Wars past, and the Nixonian Emperor with her; the father’s ghost appears in her draft; the twin-sister plot “rules out such a Father Vader concept at that time.” “April 1978 was a 180-degree turn from everything else before it.” Fuller treatment in Appendix C. Recent Lucas (n.434): “I wanted to film one movie: the tragic story of Darth Vader. In the beginning he should have appeared as a monster; in the middle part the monster should reveal itself as the father of the hero—and at the end of the movie as the true hero himself.” “so far removed from what the truth actually is.”

Post-hit pressure: $200 million, office maniac, pilgrimages to his parents’ house “claiming to be sent by god” (n.435); religious readings; intelligentsia / Campbell hype. Bettelheim *Uses of Enchantment* (1976) published after *Star Wars* was filmed, so Lucas’s claim in Rinzler that he read it beforehand is “probably not being accurate” (n.437). Lucas later (n.438): “simply by following my own inspiration… it was very close to the model.” Didn’t meet Campbell or hear a lecture until after the original trilogy (n.439). Mutual status exchange: *Power of Myth* filmed at the Ranch; Skywalker on a new *Hero With a Thousand Faces* cover; Smithsonian exhibit “acquiescence of inaccuracies… for the sake of educational purposes.”

**Steven Hart** (n.440–441): *Empire* “marks the beginning of Lucas' unheroic journey from honest entertainer to galactic gasbag”; *Time* May 1980 “In the Footsteps of Ulysses.” **Michael Pye and Lynda Myles**, 1979 (n.442–445): the film “lacks true narrative drive… It is a void, into which any mystic idea can be projected”; cine-literacy / Ford / saloon-brawl conventions; Nazi allusions both good and bad; Force as “building a theology out of staying cool”; “the single strongest impression it leaves is… pinball, on a cosmic scale… It is a holiday from thought.” **Hamill**, 1980 (n.446): “like a Hula-Hoop or a Frisbee, a summertime fun thing… Star Wars was a celebration.” Lucas, 1977 (n.447): “a straight forward, wholesome, fun adventure.” Lucas, 2004 (n.448): success attributed to “psychological underpinnings which had been around for thousands of years.” 1974 Flash Gordon quote already locked in Ch. II (n.449). Cultural timing: *Jaws*, *Rocky*, *Superman*; collapse of the American New Wave. Lucas (n.450): “there’s a reason this film is so popular.” Kurtz Flash Gordon dinner already locked in Ch. II (n.451). Lucas (n.452): “I’m so ordinary that a lot of people can relate to me.” Mid-70s SF/fantasy underground (D&D, Tolkien, Conan, *Metal Hurlant*, *Star Trek* and *Flash Gordon* features already in production). **Hampton Fancher** on *Blade Runner* (n.453): “There was this smell of science fiction in Hollywood.” **Dale Pollock** (n.454): “George Lucas was the farm kid on Tatooine.” Lucas, 1981 (n.455): “It’s just a movie… like a sunset.” 1983 (n.456): “Most directors are insecure, and I’m no exception to that.”

**Final revelation, typography locked, p. 214:** “There is no master plan—and there never was. It was all made up as he went, and any plans laid would prove only temporary.”

“That is the way it always was” as “security blanket.” **Hamill**, *Starlog* October 1980 (n.457), on the “Other”: “It didn't sit so well with me at first… I told George that people would think I was pulling a $5 million holdout… I thought it made me look bad. But George insisted it had always been part of the storyline.” On *Revenge* (n.458): “that’s the way the film was originally designed… I’m stuck with the way it was originally planned, and I can’t suddenly go off on some tangent.” On unmasking Vader as “this funny little man” (n.459): “that was the original story, that’s the way it should be, and if the public can’t deal with then what can I do about it?” Closing (n.460): “Right or wrong this is my movie, this is my decision, and this is my creative vision, and if people don’t like it, they don’t have to see it.” Marcia, via Pollock: Lucas is “especially sensitive to criticism.”
