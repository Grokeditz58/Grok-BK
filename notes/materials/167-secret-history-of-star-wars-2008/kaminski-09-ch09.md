# Kaminski, *The Secret History of Star Wars* (e-book v3.0, 2008): notes — Chapter IX: The Beginning…Again (pp. 288–308)

Fully read. Citations: Kaminski p. N; Kaminski Notes n. Y (Ch. IX starts at n.643). Kaminski glosses flagged. Staff/crew/cast labeled by speaker. The 1991–93 renaissance, *Young Indy* and *Jurassic Park* as the conditions for returning; the prequel “outline” locked as seven-or-eight pages of unpinned notes, not a master plan; Anakin displaces Obi-Wan as protagonist; Palpatine/Sidious, the Rule of Two, childhood Anakin, and Jedi-as-monks are 1990s inventions.

## Bantam, Zahn, and the Sequel Trilogy that “never was” (pp. 288–291)

1989: Bantam proposed a new *Star Wars* novel (n.643). Earlier beyond-the-films books (*Splinter*, Han Solo Adventures 1979–80, Lando trilogy 1983) had stopped; Marvel comics ended 1986. Lucasfilm took over a year to reply. **Timothy Zahn**, 1992 (n.644): **Lou Aronica** of Bantam Spectra contacted Lucasfilm “about three years ago”; a year later “Yes, this sounds interesting; let’s talk.” Zahn was “the first one they asked.”

Kaminski: in that waiting period Lucas “finally decided that the Sequel Trilogy was definitely a no-go—the closest he could ever come was to continue the saga as novels,” a concept first implemented with **Alan Dean Foster** in 1975. Lucas, 1999 (n.645), typography locked: “The sequels were never really going to get made anyway, unlike 1, 2, and 3, where the stories have existed for 20 years. The idea of 7, 8, and 9 actually came from people asking me about sequels, and I said, ‘I don't know. Maybe someday.’ Then when the licensing people came and asked, ‘Can we do novels?’ I said do sequels, because I'll probably never do sequels.”

Zahn’s trilogy framed as “the Sequel Trilogy that never was”: **Mara Jade** “similar to the abandoned Jedi-sister concept”; “dark Jedi” **Joruus C’Baoth** and **Grand Admiral Thrawn** as “ample substitutes for Vader and the Emperor”; Had Abbadon “resurrected… now renamed Coruscant” (n.646); story revolves around rebuilding the Republic. Lucas more involved than on later novels: read and approved treatments, veto power. First rule: set shortly after *Jedi*. Zahn (n.647): “My original instructions from LucasArts consisted of exactly two rules: the books were to start 3-5 years after Return of the Jedi, and I couldn't use anyone who'd been explicitly killed off in the movies… A couple of other rules came in later after I'd submitted my outline… Lucas did read the original outline.” Negotiating (n.648): “They cut out my favorite character. And originally, Joruus C'baoth was going to be a clone of Obi-wan Kenobi, but they said no. They didn't want me to refer in detail to the clone wars… I guess by that time Lucas had decided he was going to go back and make the prequel movies, and they didn't want me treading on his turf.” Mid-writing: “they suddenly decided they wanted me to coordinate with the West End Games role-playing materials.”

Zahn on whether anyone still liked *Star Wars* (n.649): “You bet I wondered… certainly the initial book stores’ reaction and orders indicated it would be only a mild success.” *Heir to the Empire*, June 4th, 1991: first printing of 60,000 sold out in its first week; *New York Times* bestsellers list for 29 weeks (n.650). *Dark Force Rising* 1992, *The Last Command* 1993. **Dark Horse** *Dark Empire* 1991: “Luke’s fall to the darkside and subsequent redemption in a story meant to parallel his father’s, which Lucas also had some level of involvement with” (n.651). Footnote: *Dark Empire* in development since 1989 with Marvel; Dark Horse picked it up after management changes.

Renaissance: *Jedi Academy Trilogy*, *Truce at Bakura*, *Tales of the Jedi*, *Rogue Squadron*; Micro Machines; republished *Splinter* and Han Solo Adventures; Marvel reprints; *Lucasfilm Magazine* revamped 1995 as *Star Wars Insider*; Topps *Star Wars Galaxy Magazine* and *Star Wars Galaxy Collector*; LucasArts *Super Star Wars*, *X-Wing*, *Rebel Assault II* (1995) with **David Prowse** as Vader, photographed by **Gil Taylor**. Kenner relaunched 1995; Vader vs. Energizer Bunny; Galoob “making $120 million a year from Star Wars toys” (n.652).

Lucas (n.653): “When I went and decided that I would tell the backstory, it was a very difficult decision at the time… I figured I was done with Star Wars. I didn’t want to do Star Wars any more.” Audience + ILM digital + renaissance wealth: “It technically became possible to do it and I had this backstory. The backstory intrigued me because it kind of turns the whole series on its head.” (n.654) Adopted third child, **Jett**, around this time (n.655): “They wanted a brother… I wanted another daughter because the girls were so wonderful...but it’s great to have someone that understands explosions.”

## *Young Indy*, *Jurassic Park*, the 1993 announcement (pp. 292–295)

*The Young Indiana Jones Chronicles*, 1992: testing ground for crowd replication, set extension, wire removal, digital matte painting and compositing, “at an inexpensive cost.” Lucas (n.656): “I said 'I want to be able to do a couple hundred shots in every hour-long episode and still have a budget that's under $50,000.'… In the end, we made 22 'feature films' in the space of five years… that's what I'm using now.” “Beginning of the second stage of Lucas’ career,” a new entourage: **Gary Kurtz** “would morph into **Rick McCallum**,” Huyck and Katz into **Frank Darabont** and **Jonathan Hales**, **Haskell Wexler** into **David Tattersall**, **Walter Murch** into **Ben Burtt** (now picture cutting). Also **Trisha Biggar**, **Gavin Bocquet**, **Peter Walpole**. McCallum stayed for *Young Indy*, *Radioland Murders*, Special Edition, the prequels, and “the two subsequent Star Wars TV series.”

**Jonathan Hales** (n.657): “We all had enormous fun doing The Young Indiana Jones Chronicles and I think that re-energized George to a degree.” McCallum: “We could go anywhere with this, do anything!” “It was the best job I’ve ever had.” Lucas came up with the stories himself. Assistant **Jane Bay** (n.658): “They would come to the office every day and George would tell them these stories… It was the first time he had collaborated with a group of people in this way, and I have never seen him happier in a creative environment.” Series 1992–1993, ten Emmys; cancelled after a changing ABC timeslot; remaining episodes became TV movies. Lucas (n.659): “I didn’t take seriously the idea of doing prequels to Star Wars until I had the digital technology to do it.”

*Jurassic Park*. Early CG tests, 1992 (n.660): “We did a test for Steven Spielberg; and when we put them up on the screen I had tears in my eyes. It was like one of those moments in history, like the invention of the lightbulb or the first telephone call.” Then (n.661): “That was the watershed of being able to create realistic characters using digital technology… I could do cities like Coruscant, I could do a pod race, I could do other things that up to that point had been impossible.” Soul-searching (n.662): “And now my kids are old enough that I can go back and direct movies—which is what I wanted to do. And I opted for the Star Wars thing because again it was one of these ‘well, the opportunity is there and I think I’d be foolish not to do it.’” Also (n.663): “[I realized the] Star Wars audience was still alive… I decided that if I didn’t do the backstory then, I never would. So I committed to it.” Dryly (n.664): “Part of the reason for doing it is that it’s the first question I get asked. Not ‘This is who I am’ or anything, but ‘When are you going to do the next Star Wars?’” Financial (n.665): “I got a divorce and that sort of set me back quite a ways… one of the reasons to go back to Star Wars was that it would hopefully make me financially secure enough to where I wouldn’t have to go to a studio and beg for money.” 1992, prepping *Young Indy* season two (n.666): “I’ve said that I would always try to at least do the next trilogy, which is the first trilogy… I’ll probably start writing it in the next couple of years. Somewhere between 1995 and the year 2000 there should be three more Star Wars movies that will come out.” **October 4th, 1993 *Variety***: official announcement that the prequels were going into production. Kaminski: he had talked about “the young days of Ben Kenobi” since 1977 and “technically officially announced them in May 1980 in Bantha Tracks,” but this was the first confirmation of actual production.

Twentieth-anniversary re-release planned for 1997. Lucas (n.667): “I had a two-year-old son… I thought, ‘I’m not going to show him the film on video, I’m going to wait and let him see it on the big screen the way it was meant to be.’” Also (n.668): “If you're going to put that much money into reissuing the movie, I want to get it right this time.” **Mark Cotta Vaz**, *ILM: Into the Digital Realm*: late 1993 brainstorming, **Dennis Muren** and art director **TyRuben Ellingson**; Lucas wanted expanded Mos Eisley and the discarded Jabba scene. **Tom Kennedy**: “The initial scope of it involved just two dozen shots.” Muren (n.669): “I suggested to George that we expand the vision and he was open to it. Motion issues, particularly in the space battle scenes, were my concerns.” Special Edition also “an experiment in learning new technology” for the prequels (n.670); “hoped that the theatrical release would pay for the work we had done.”

## The “outline,” writing alone, Anakin becomes the protagonist (pp. 296–300)

He had to outline all three films first. Kaminski: the original trilogy “was mostly a patchwork of improvisations. Now Lucas would finally have the chance to design a true ‘master plan’ from the beginning—and stick with it.” Then: “even this would be upheaved, as we will see throughout the coming chapters.” Dusted off “his old binder”: Mother Skywalker, Clone Wars, Boba Fett, Obi-Wan and Yoda origins; some notes “written out of history” by *Empire* and *Jedi*.

**The pre-1983 “outline,” typography locked.** “Lucas’ description of it wavers from seven pages to twelve pages (back in the days of the original trilogy he claimed each film was twelve pages long, including the three sequels). The actual length of it is almost certainly the lower estimate, roughly seven pages, and that also appears to include the background information, miscellaneous notes and character sketches… It seems as though it is not an actual seven-page narrative summary of the prequel story the way many might assume, but rather it is merely seven pages of notes, perhaps many of the very ones excerpted in chapters II and III of this book.” 2004 DVD commentary: plot points, character background, environment. Lucas (n.671): “It’s mostly historical background that’s not pinned to any particular story.” Also (n.672): “I have a little story treatment, a little outline that says this happened here, this happened here… It’s only about seven or eight pages.”

Juggling companies, children, remaining *Young Indy*, *Radioland Murders* (with Huyck and Katz, another digital test), Special Edition, and prequel writing. First planning “as early as 1992, but mostly in 1993” (n.673). McCallum recruited a writing partner: **Frank Darabont**, in the *Shawshank* cutting room, late 1993 (n.674): “Rick McCallum called me… ‘George would like to know if you're interested in performing screenwriting services for him down the road.’ I said, ‘Sure—what are you—kidding?’” The need “never arose—Lucas simply kept writing on his own. This was a significant departure”: no Marcia, no Coppola/Barwood/Ritchie/De Palma/Spielberg/Kaufman circle, no Huyck/Katz dialog rewrite, no Brackett/Kershner/Kasdan/Marquand/Kurtz conferences. To **Kerry O’Quinn**, 1981 (n.675): would he ever turn prequel scripting over? “I don’t know… I’d love to. But I don’t think its going to be possible.”

Lucas (n.676): “I became fascinated by the idea of making a new trilogy that would forever change the way we see the original movies.” Originally the prequels “would not follow Anakin but Obi Wan, with Anakin Skywalker’s fall seen through his eyes; the true pathos… would hence be the failure of Kenobi.” Pre-early-to-mid-90s descriptions: “the young days of Ben Kenobi.” “As Lucas set out to write… a realization obviously dawned on him. Far more tragic and powerful than Obi Wan’s story was the story of Anakin himself.” Anakin becomes primary, Obi-Wan secondary. *Jedi* had already shifted the original trilogy toward father and son; the six films could be “the story of Anakin Skywalker—beginning with his discovery and ending with his death.” Previously two “books” (Denise Worrell, 1983, n.677); “Now those two books were being combined into one.” Footnote: possible *Godfather II* influence, “We can only speculate,” plus the later chronological recut of that saga. Stylistic disconnect remains: prequels as “melodramatic and tragic costume dramas,” originals as “light-hearted adventures”—fine under the three-trilogy plan, harder once merged into one Saga. Milestone: prequels had been “a tangent, a ‘filling-in’ of backstory… as a separate series.” Timing “up for debate”; Kaminski places the switch around the 1993 outline, “though Lucas’ comments in 1992 may perhaps indicate it had occurred around this time, though his description is inconclusive” (n.678). Still “not as acute as it would be in the trilogy’s final form”—see Chapter XIII.

Original outline “had only detailed the character’s final hours as a Jedi… even his initial discovery by Obi Wan seemed to have occurred when Anakin was a young man.” Not a “demon-seed.” Lucas (n.679): “I wanted him to be a very earnest, very honest, very good-natured person; just the opposite of the way you think of Darth Vader. Because the larger story is really ‘How does a sweet kid like this turn into something as evil as Darth Vader?’… Not, ‘Well he was bad to begin with…’” So “he set the story much earlier than he anticipated… he went back to the character’s childhood.” Footnote: already enchanted by kid protagonists via *Young Indy*’s ten-year-old Indy. Significant deviation: mid-70s, Anakin and Obi-Wan “grew up together on Tatooine” until Vader slays Anakin; after *Empire*, Anakin “ceased to be Obi Wan’s equal and thus became his student”; *Jedi* provided the “discovery” of a young man strong in the Force. “This ‘discovery’ was carried over to the prequels, and the original plot point that Anakin grew up on Tatooine residually remained—only now the events occurred in his childhood.” Flag as Kaminski’s mixing reconstruction.

Writing consistency (n.680): “In writing Episode I, I spent a lot of the time doing research. I had to develop an entire world… Everything had to be laid out in this script so that the next two scripts would follow as they should. I also had to play this script against the three movies that had already been made… There was a tremendous amount of minutiae in these movies that I had to consider.”

## Palpatine/Sidious, Rule of Two, Jedi as monks, childhood padding (pp. 300–308)

1970s Palpatine: “an unscrupulous politician who seized power through bribery and manipulation.” *Star Wars* novelization hinted “a long line of Emperors that were really just puppets of the bureaucracy.” *Empire* made him “more evil and sinister”; *Jedi* “a satanic sorcerer.” “Lucas now decided that Palpatine was a Sith, a fact that had never been officially confirmed—the Emperor was a master of the darkside in the original films, but not necessarily part of any official order, and the term Sith had never even been used in the films.” Dilemma: “how does an evil Sith Lord get into politics?” Solution: **“the dual persona of Palpatine/ Sidious.”** Always “manipulating the state and the people into handing over power.” Lucas (n.681): “This was written during the Vietnam War and Nixon era, when the issue was how a democracy turns itself over to a dictator — not how a dictator takes over a democracy.” Clone Wars linked to that rise. EU version (Mandalorian army, Boba Fett a member), based on Summer 1979 *Bantha Tracks* Shocktroopers and the *Empire* novelization, “Lucas decided to disregard.” Separatists realize the second-draft idea that “war and terrorism persuaded people to accept a police state.” Clone Wars “in Lucas’ original conception (vague as it was), occurred much earlier than what we would be shown in the films, as will be explained in the next chapter.” “The macro event of the Clone War was thus now shifted to a micro event, an event personally connected to Palpatine… the scope of the Star Wars galaxy was becoming considerably smaller.”

Sith history rewritten. Lucas (n.682), typography locked: “One of the themes throughout the films is that the Sith Lords, when they started out thousands of years ago, embraced the dark side… they killed each other. Eventually, there was only one left, and that one took on an apprentice… there could never be any more than two of them, because if there were, they would try to get rid of the leader, which is exactly what Vader was trying to do, and that's exactly what the Emperor was trying to do… And that is the antithesis of a symbiotic relationship, in which if you do that, you become cancer, and you eventually kill the host, and everything dies.” **“Thus was born the so-called ‘Rule of Two,’ a complete turnaround from the previous incarnation in which they were a warrior clan with many members.”** More detail in **Terry Brooks**’ *Phantom Menace* novelization. “Darth,” once Vader’s first name, then Anakin’s Sith alias from 1978, “now… became a Sith designation (see appendix ‘Dark Father’).” Trade Federation from the second-draft “greedy trade barons.” Naboo (Utapau in the initial draft). Kaminski’s outline of the three-film Palpatine plot (flag as Kaminski’s reconstruction of Lucas’s 1990s plan, not a locked 1970s document): blockade and invasion; no-confidence vote; Maul slain; clone army secretly commissioned from **Jango Fett** (father of Boba) for use ten years later; Sidious fosters Separatists; emergency powers; Jedi try to assassinate him, fail, framed as traitors; clones turn; volcano duel; Palpatine resurrects him as Vader. “Palpatine’s schemes however, are plagued with set-backs”; he improvises (Queen herself votes the Chancellor out; notices young Anakin).

Jedi evolution (Kaminski history, then 1990s change): *Journal of the Whills* “intergalactic super-police”; rough draft more samurai; later Lensmen/sci-fi mysticism; Guinness/Ben “wizard-like spin.” 1977 film: “a mysterious group of warriors which anyone could be recruited for… a power which anyone could learn to harness if only they believed in themselves.” Footnote, 1977 (Rinzler, p. 353): “Anyone who studied and worked hard could learn [the Force].” Sequels brought back “superpowers.” Prequels: “outright religious monks,” born of Yoda’s Zen overtones—**Kasdan** and **Kershner** both Buddhist. Lucas (n.683): “I was raised Methodist. Now let’s say I’m spiritual. It’s Marin County. We’re all Buddhists up here.” Early Episode I design still police/Templar/samurai: production art of Obi-Wan in black body armor; rough draft “explicitly describes him as being dressed in black” (n.684); 1983 *Classic Creatures*, Lucas to Hamill that the sleek militaristic black costume was “Jedi-like.” **Ian McCaig** (n.685): “At one point during the Episode I design, we were thinking of the Jedi as lone samurai, then as teams of samurai… They were going to be like a police force, dressed in black and a lot more militaristic. But they evolved into the peacekeeping force they are in the current film.” **Laurent Bouzereau** / McCaig (n.686): “George wanted to make sure that when the audience saw these characters for the first time, it would immediately register that these were Jedi knights… I realized that those designers were very medieval, so we kept to that.” **Kaminski: this was “based on a major oversight—the ‘Jedi garb’ of the original trilogy was not Jedi garb at all.”** Obi-Wan wears Tatooine desert robes; “Uncle Owen is dressed in almost the exact same costume.” Yoda in “hand-crafted rags.” *Jedi* spirit Anakin “simply dressed identical to Obi Wan, perhaps creating the confusion.” In-universe hedge: Anakin is from Tatooine. Consequence: Kenobi “supposedly hiding… strolls around the stormtrooper and bounty hunter infested Mos Eisley streets in his Jedi dress.” Jedi now “indoctrinate potential members from infanthood, and forbade any attachment… like Buddhist monks,” “ivory tower” temple, “sowing the seeds of their own demise with their arrogance.” Yoda moved from Dagobah shaman “designed and written as if a part of Dagobah itself” into “an urban metropolis as part of the political scene.” “Lucas had not come up with a personal history to Yoda in the original films” (n.687).

Tragic flaw: Obi-Wan’s hubris “would shift from Obi Wan into Anakin.” Main Anakin flaw: “attachment to loved ones, his possessiveness and his fear of loss, an influence Lucas obviously took from his Buddhist beliefs.” Mother separation “the primary reason Lucas set the first act of the trilogy to occur during Anakin’s childhood” (n.688); slavery; later reunion as she dies; slaughter of captors; vow; wife premonitions; Palpatine offers the dark side as power to save her. “The details in between would be mostly invented on the fly as he wrote the actual scripts, but he had a clear plan for the character’s arc.” Cannes (n.689): “most bad people think they are good people, they are doing it for the right reasons.” Faust / Marlowe / Theophilus of Adana flagged as Kaminski’s literary parallel, not a Lucas citation.

Mother developed as “a person of politics, similar to Leia’s role,” elected teenage Queen of Naboo, “in order to involve her prominently in the events of Episode I,” now set in Anakin’s childhood. Innocent crush, then secret marriage as adults. Fourteen “was also an idea from the 1974 rough draft… Princess Leia was fourteen (being based on Princess Yuki from The Hidden Fortress).” Episode I rough draft, however, “would state only that her age is ‘hard to determine, but she is a young woman’” (n.690).

To **Leonard Maltin**, 2005 (n.691): “The one we worried about the most was ‘Episode I.’ The backstory wasn’t meant to be a movie.” Childhood setting stretched a few-year outline “well over a decade”; first episode “contained very little of the original plot”; II and especially III condensed. Lucas (n.692): “[Episodes I and II] were character pieces with lots of exposition… The problem is that 60 percent — maybe 80 percent of the backstory — is contained in Episode III.” *Empire Magazine*, 2005 (n.693): “The Phantom Menace contained just 20 percent of this original outline’s first three episodes, ditto Clones; the picnics and pod races, George confessed, were ‘padding.’” Lucas (n.694): “I did run into the reality of the first film… he is a slave kid. He gets found by the Jedi and he becomes part of the Jedi order and that he loves his mother. You know, that’s maybe a half hour movie. And so I did a kind of jazz riff on the rest of it… I have this giant world to play in and I’m going to just move around and have fun with this because, you know, I have to get to the second part.” 1976 (n.695): “Someday I’ll do another movie like this, maybe, which will be much closer to my original plan. I didn’t get the illusion that I’d seen in my mind.” Now “he would finally realise that far-out vision.”

Production plan: shoot back to back like *Young Indy*; release 1997, 1998, 1999; write all three scripts at once. 1992 (n.696): “Shooting the new Star Wars films back to back has always been the plan.” Summer 1994: McCallum and Bocquet begin location recces (n.697); Lucas “finally began preparing to write Episode I’s screenplay.” Close: “It had been eleven years since a Star Wars film had played on silver screens… was Lucas ready to go back?”
