# *Dark Forces Official Player's Guide* (1995): part 2, Chapter 3 "The Making of Dark Forces" (printed pp. 201–212, PDF pp. 223–234)

**Source:** Jeff Hoff, *Dark Forces Official Player's Guide*, Infotainment World, 1995. MEGA `QC1G0ByK`. I read this from OCR and checked it against the page images. Citations give the PDF page; for this chapter, printed page = PDF page − 22. The chapter is a feature on the LucasArts team built from interviews. I read every page of it, including the photo captions and the full credits block.

## Scale and setting (PDF p. 224)
- The scale of the job: "20 people worked 20 months to complete the game, many of them putting in 60-hour weeks without vacations." (The OCR reads "6O-hour".)
- The team photo caption (L to R) lists "Dan Connors, Patrick Sirk, Chris Ross," Jim Current, "Brett Tosti, Aaron Muszalski, Chris Hockabout, Justin Chin," "Ingar Shu, Tom Payne, Daron Stinnett and his dog Tanner," Matt Tateishi, Ray Gresko, and Winston Wolff.
- The office: the core team "works in an unremarkable office building that has no identifying signs. Like many of the doors in Dark Forces, the building's entrance requires a code key to enter." It is dark, with the fluorescent lights left off, and "the place looks and feels more like a college dorm than a place of business. The average age of the inhabitants? Young. The average attire? Rumpled."

## Building the team (PDF pp. 225–226)
- **Daron Stinnett**, aged 29 and the project leader, "looks like a project leader mainly because he’s about the only one on the team who shaves daily and wears button-down shirts with his jeans." He was also one of the lead programmers.
- His origins: "A self-taught programmer from Seattle who has developed video games since the tenth grade, Stinnett was hired by LucasArts in July, 1993". "Originally he wanted it to be a flight simulator for video game systems but he quickly turned his attention to a first-person Star Wars computer game, akin to Wolfenstein 3-D from Id Software." He had run "the high-profile flight simulator division at Spectrum HoloByte (Falcon, et al)", but "knew little about first-person walk-about shooters."
- The team was "pulled together by quirk more than by top-down strategy". "The average age was 27. At its peak, the team had 20 people, with a core group of about 10". Programmers Ray Gresko and Winston Wolff and Stinnett "set out together in late August ‘93 to build a 3-D game engine worthy of the Star Wars name."
- **How the level designers were found (PDF p. 226):** "One day, Stinnett was in a movie theater describing these complexities to a friend. Someone in the next row heard the conversation and recommended that he contact the University of California at Berkeley because it has a program that combines architecture and computers." The result was "Ingar Shu, a Berkeley architectural undergrad, was the team’s first level designer." Jim Current, "a former LucasArts tester", followed, then Matt Tateishi, "another Berkeley undergrad".
- **Justin Chin** "came to the Dark Forces project in December, 1993, as lead artist. An artist by trade and story writer by love", he had been art director at Accolade.
- Stinnett looking back: "With Dark Forces, we winged it at the beginning, but then things started falling into place."

## Building the engine (PDF pp. 226–227)
- The pitch: "This would be the first time anyone could actually walk around in a Star Wars universe. It had to have the same rich look and feel and sound and detail of the movies, plus compelling gameplay."
- Code: it was "originally written in a programming language called Assembly, but when 486 and Pentium chips suddenly and surprisingly became the market standard, the code was translated to a more prevalent language called C."
- Speed versus detail: it takes "an awful lot of eye candy" and "an awful lot of speed." Stinnett: "In order to be real and fast, we had to break the limits of Doom-style engines". The example given is Nar Shaddaa's stacked levels, where you can "look down at a floor below you or above you".
- Stinnett only played it through after it was finished: "he sat down one Saturday and played it from start to finish. “Before then I thought it was good,” Stinnett said. “After, I knew it was good — incredible."

## Building the story (PDF pp. 227–228), the most important part for lore
- **Luke came first, then Kyle:** "Dark Forces’ early story ideas revolved around Luke Skywalker and the original Star Wars plot, but the designers quickly felt the limitations of that idea. Luke did what he did in two hours, and LucasArts needed a 40-hour playing experience full of surprises." After that: "The next idea was a new character called Kyle Katarn, but he was still moving around in the existing Star Wars chain of events, which was still too constraining."
- **Where the dark troopers came from:** Chin saw Gresko's engine: "“It was just a really cool world to walk around in,” he recalls. “It sparked some ideas.” His favorite spark revolved around a special force of stormtroopers."
- **A design document written in five days:** "Chin started writing on a Thursday and produced the first design document the following Tuesday. It contained background sketches of major characters, including Katarn and General Mohc (the bad-guy creator of the dark troopers), as well as descriptions for 32 missions." The OCR reads "Mohe". The 32 missions were later "cut from the original 32 to 14 because of time and space constraints."
- **Influences:** "The only way I thought this game would work was if it were like an action movie," Chin said; "the Star Wars trilogy of course, but also the Die Hard films, James Bond".

## Building the look and feel (PDF pp. 228–229)
- Five artists made "texture" art and a couple more built 3-D models. **Aaron Muszalski**, formerly an art technician on Rebel Assault, created the file-naming convention and printed art catalogs. Stinnett: "The benefit of Aaron's work cannot be overestimated".
- Chin on the hardest part: "It was the mystery of how everything was going to work together. We had new engine technology, new kinds of art, new kinds of animation, and new people. Nobody on the team had ever done this before."
- **Jan Ors was created by an engine change:** "Suddenly, when the game was about 60 percent finished, we figured things out". Cut scenes were taken "out of the game engine and putting them into their own engine". "This last change led to the creation of the Jan Ors character, who serves as the story mouthpiece for players as well as Kyle Katarn." (PDF p. 229)

## Building the missions (PDF pp. 229–231)
- The levels were built in AutoCAD, partly "because architectural students Ingar Shu and Matt Tateishi were well-versed in it." Current: "You start with a blank screen, and pretty soon you're walking around inside your own world."
- Shu: "If you violate the look and feel of the Star Wars universe, people know it". The goal was "a game that would take a good gamer at least 40 hours to finish." (PDF p. 230)
- Same-door elevators (Detention Center) and the rotating walkway on the Executor in Mission XIII are the showpieces. Stinnett: "You just don't see a box like this in a game, the outside of it , and then be able to walk inside of it." (PDF p. 231)

## Building the gameplay (PDF pp. 231–232)
- **Lead tester Brett Tosti** was aged 30. His testers pushed for lives found inside missions, and restart points replaced the original rule: "Originally, you had to start the mission all over again; now you are bumped back just a short distance to restart points."
- **Photo joke (PDF p. 231):** the testers' caption, back row, lists "William W. Burns, Darth Vader, Al Chen, Chris Purvis, Brett Tosti, Reed Derleth, Chris Snyder, Dan Connors."
- Placement by theme: "So on Ramsees Hed, for example, you encounter Ree-Yees on a ledge above you tossing grenades. It's a tough kill for you. But Tosti discovered that the mortar gun is extremely effective in that situation, so he placed that weapon and extra shells nearby." (PDF p. 232)

## Voices, sound effects and music (PDF pp. 232–234)
- **Clint Bajakian**, aged 32, is "a classically trained musician" who has "a masters in composition from the University of Michigan". Voices were "produced by Khris Brown and Tamlynn Barra" and recorded "in a Los Angeles studio".
- The game used "basic 8-bit, mono multimedia sound standards" (PDF p. 232). It has "more than 300 sound effects culled from three sources", among them "actual Star Wars movie sound effects taken from Skywalker Sound" (PDF p. 233).
- **Trooper-pod launch:** he started with "a similar sound from The Empire Strikes Back — the launching of probe droid" pods, and added a "really fast snowspeeder from Star Wars and my voice shifted for frequency". The **Phase Two death** is "his pained, ring-modulated voice mixed with the metallic falling sound of an Imperial walker from Star Wars." (PDF p. 233; I checked the wording on the page image.)
- **Themes:** "Bajakian wrote two new musical themes for the game. The first is called The General Mohc theme." Kyle's theme "is almost a mirror opposite. “It’s a heroic, upbeat slant to Mohc’s badness,”" (PDF p. 233)
- **Dynamic music:** "The image I came up with is a feather that is first blown vigorously into the air but which then calmly floats down". "I call it fight music and stalk music." (PDF p. 234)

## Sequel tease (PDF p. 234)
- "Justin Chin is project leader for the sequel and is working on a story script and preliminary design. In addition to a new story line, the sequel will be built around an entirely new engine, Chin said. Stay tuned." This became *Jedi Knight* (item 16 on this reading list).
