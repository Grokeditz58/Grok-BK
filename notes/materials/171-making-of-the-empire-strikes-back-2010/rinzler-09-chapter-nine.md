# 171 — Chapter Nine: Film Dance (September to December 1979)

Print pp. 248–276 (EPUB anchors `page248`–`page276`; print TOC opening p. 248). Rinzler ESB pp. 248–276. Staff/crew/cast same treatment as Lucas. Rinzler glosses flagged. The October 17, 1979, first cut and Lucas’s October 15/25/31 editorial notes are kept distinct from the finished film and from earlier numbered drafts. Conflicting shot counts, light counts, and typographical variants are recorded, not collapsed.

## FILM DANCE (pp. 248–249)

Starkey, offered either first-apprentice editorial or running a Sprocket Systems sound facility at Kerner, takes a drive with Hal Barwood: “Just ask yourself a simple question: *Do you wanna work on movies or do you wanna work for a company that supplies stuff to people who make movies?*” He chooses movies. Lucas: “One of the things Lucasfilm does ultimately is mentor a lot of young people and move them into the film business in all aspects.” Jim Kessler starts in September as head of Sprocket Systems; “mom-and-pop shop” atmosphere; benches set up “in the big basement of Park House.” Hirsch: “I would say within two weeks of the end of shooting, we will be able to have a rough-cut screening.” Lucas “draws a parallel between making a film and building a house: The screenplay is the blueprint; the shooting is the assembling of the raw materials; but the editing is the actual construction of the house.” Kershner transfers his pattern notes to Hirsch: “sometimes we do it exactly the way I want, though most of the time it changes.” Starkey (source typo “Paul Hirsh”): transition from an English system to an American one; he assists both Hirsch and Lucas/Dunham. Hirsch: “Editing is perhaps the only one of the film arts that has no historical antecedents… Only film and music require that an audience comprehend the details of a work of art over a given period of time.” Kurtz: “Paul was on from the very beginning,” so a rough cut of the England material existed “about a week after we had finished shooting.” Starkey’s first rough-cut screening at Park House: “It’s a mess!”—space fights are “little black-and-white scratchy things.” Burtt: “I was there on *Star Wars* and it’s probably feeling really rough to you, but I promise you this has got all the goods. This movie is working.”

## LOS ANGELES EXPLOSION (pp. 249–250)

Neighbors petition; the city throws editorial out of Park House. Relocation to 321 San Anselmo Avenue (Lucas’s office; Dunham, Starkey, Burtt, Summers). Burtt: downstairs sound, upstairs picture. A reporter describes the converted apartment: “white and buff with a dark green carpet,” a coffee-table-sized clock, fireplace, Betamax; “In the bathroom were white towels imprinted with Artoo-Detoo.” Lucas: “We spend a couple of million dollars a year in Marin County.” The Egg Company building opens in Los Angeles that fall; Tong: remodel budgeted around $200,000, “ultimate bill came to over $2 million.” Weber: creative group in Northern California; merchandising, publishing, and some marketing in LA. Alsup: “The Egg Company was the beginning of bringing in, unfortunately, unfriendly authoritarian nonfilmmaking executives. Our original, delightful, casual Lucasfilm was fading into a big-deal corporation.” Bloom on *us* and *them*: production vs. corporate. Lucy Wilson loses day-to-day access to Lucas (“I couldn’t talk to him unless I had an appointment”). Burtt: “George became more and more isolated from the day-to-day interaction with people.”

## FOCUS ON ILM (pp. 250–252)

Cost-breakdown report: “upward of 600 shots to complete, 265 for the snow battle.” Edlund’s count is lower, “roughly 440 shots.” Record both, do not collapse. About 66 shots already in the can—versus *Star Wars* at the same post point, “1 or 2 shots of its 360.” After *Star Wars* wrap, about 138 scenes remained; *Empire* has 204. Edlund: simple shot programmed “in, say, 15 minutes”; a complex one “may take two hours”; in-house black-and-white test, then proceed. Warren Franklin: the production plan of “what would be the most difficult stuff… all turned out to be totally backward.” September 4 notes: high-speed camera going out for anodizing; new Rebel cruiser design approved; body for big walker being built; wait for slug to determine asteroid-tunnel look; “snow chipmunk puppet for snow planet sequence has been drawn up.” Plant layout: VistaVision/four-perf projection room, camera room, art, editing, L-shaped stage with three motion-control tracks, stills lab, optical, model shop, engineering; reception plaque of a figure in top hat and tails with a wand. Air-conditioning down; Herman: “music is too loud in various departments.” Tom Smith hired to help run the facility: “People wearing dirty clothes and a wildness everywhere.” Peterson had thought *Empire* would be a one-shot deal then a move back to LA; “within six months or so… we’re gonna be here for a while.” Gawley to Muren: “Should we top ourselves after *Star Wars?*… Well, let’s just have fun.” Deadline: film completed by March 31, 1980; editorial needs “bulk of effects material mid-January.” Two roadblocks: “Empire camera still has bugs, but nothing fatal”; Quad optical printer stalled. Franklin: sitting around waiting for the printer “over in the corner of the room.” Lucas: “I don’t care if you have to use a black curtain and put ships on sticks. I’m not interested in getting a technical award. All I really want to do is get the movie made and I don’t care how we do it as long as it looks good.” Herman notes: “Decisions should be run through George. George will be here at four o’clock daily.” Kershner: “The pact was: We would never congratulate ourselves on having done a great special effect. In *Star Trek*, they do a 10-minute sequence in which they’re looking around going, ‘Ooh, aaah, isn’t the ship beautiful?’ We’ll literally throw away those shots. As a result, the story becomes paramount.” Lucas: “A special effect without a story is a pretty boring thing.” Kershner’s contract puts director’s credit “in first position at the end of the Picture, subject to waiver by the Directors Guild of America”; “the official request… seems to have been neglected as the months ticked by.” *Daily Variety*, September 26: Kasdan to “direct his own original screenplay, *Body Heat* [1981], for 20th Century–Fox.” First ILM logo by Pangrazio (Jon Berg posed); later modified by Drew Struzan after being sent to Black Falcon.

## MECHANICAL HANNIBAL (pp. 252–255)

Franklin: “The shots in the snow were very ambitious; no one had really tried to matte things over snow before.” Walker-shot preparation took 10 months. Berg: “A thing that’s 50 feet tall is going to move awful slow… It is very anthropomorphic in its design.” After disappointing bluescreen tests, “about 15 different background paintings as snowscapes.” McQuarrie paints the first landscape for a key shot of five walkers; Pangrazio, mentored by McQuarrie (started late 1978, just after his 21st birthday), takes on snowscape duty; largest panel 35 feet wide. Beswick: for the five-walker shot, “the two background ones were just mock-ups, photo cutouts that move infinitesimally on a track.” Muren: retouch a photo in 20 minutes or take four days to build a model; aversion to painted backgrounds because of *Green Acres* / *The Beverly Hillbillies*; Pangrazio “insisted on working from transparencies or photographs. He didn’t want to use his imagination.” Peterson on microballoons (hollow glass spheres, 26 microns) as scale snow, previously encountered on Mount Hekla for *Galactica*; “We had bought up the United States’ supply for that three-month period.” Beswick: a full day for each shot, four to six hours, for four to six seconds on screen—“That’s one second an hour.” Johnson: “If somebody said that I was going to be doing stop-motion animation, I’d probably commit suicide!” Muren isolates the animator from camera and lights. Lineage noted (Méliès, O’Brien, Harryhausen, Danforth, Starevich/Starewicz; Jim and David Allen; Cascade / Phil Kellison / Pal *Puppetoons*). Edlund: field-usable motion-control with reflex VistaVision, special Mitchell gear head, tape data storage. Muren’s two methods: instant in-house black-and-white, or Lyon-Lamb Video Animation System at 24 fps (earlier Cascade Sony system ran at 30 fps). From October 3: camera crews work alternating Saturdays through Thanksgiving. Shot M-137 (Muren, September 14): a few seconds, walker and set backdrop, later to include Wedge in his snowspeeder; three takes, 30 feet, plus later 50 and 46 feet of background; set torn down September 27. Original plan to photograph walkers against 4×5 Ektachrome transparencies from Norway failed; Pangrazio copied select Ektachromes onto large scenic backings. Some walkers “not much larger than a quarter.”

## COSMIC CUT (pp. 255–258)

October 15 internal memo: planned promotions with Coca-Cola, Kenner, General Mills, Topps, and Nestlé, May–December 1980. Marin County Planning Commissioners approve the ranch as “a creative retreat where filmmakers can meet, study, collaborate, write, edit, and experiment with new filmmaking ideas.” *San Francisco Chronicle*: “last stage in a complex series of Marin governmental approvals that have taken more than two years [sic]”; Lucas hopes to start construction spring 1980; “will require five years or longer to finish.” Medical frigate added to the model-shop agenda October 1 because Lucas, locking the film, “decided a new ship was needed.” Hirsch: French *montage* means “to build.” Kershner trying to keep Lucas’s convention: “you stay with no scene very long… the editorial rhythm is in a way more important than the camera moves or the actors saying their lines.” Kershner screens the cave sequence for his 12-year-old son: “Luke’s being tested… He finds what he fears the most… ‘Well, it’s Darth Vader.’… ‘Is Darth Vader really there?’ ‘No, not really. It’s in his own mind.’… ‘Should he have taken the weapons in?’ ‘If he takes the weapons in, he’s going to have to use them.’ ” Hirsch learns Kershner’s “pi factor”: never cut two images of the same size together. Lucas on Kershner’s interlocking masters: “You stage the first half and the next day you stage the second half—but then you realize the two halves don’t go together… But I’ve worked in documentary films and so has Kersh, so we both knew how to make film work when you don’t have the right material.” Kurtz: Kershner’s planned-on-set masters “did prohibit some of the editing that George would have liked to have done”; “In the end, the editing, I thought, worked out pretty well as a compromise between the two styles.” Kershner: “Anything will connect up in film, with the right sound and music. But the emotion won’t be there.”

First cut completed by October 17. Of its 12 reels, “the last half very closely resembles the finished film, while the first half has many small and some key differences.” Do not collapse these into the finished film: it opens with Luke on the tauntaun (in animatics); “the probot, after landing, zaps a snow squirrel or chipmunk”; extended bacta-tank coverage of Han, Leia, and C-3PO watching; “During the Battle of Hoth, Vader enters the base *before* Luke crashes his snowspeeder, and, just as General Veers reaches the Rebel generator, he is killed in Hobbie’s snowspeeder death run. When the *Falcon* attempts to take off from Hoth, snowtroopers succeed in blasting its hyperdrive.” Most of the asteroid sequence and simpler walker shots already in; multi-element walker-and-snowspeeder material not yet complete. Three screenings of the first cut, October 15, 25, and 31; Lucas dictates 31 pages of editorial notes to Dunham. Among the notes: “Remove probe zaps snow squirrel”; “Perhaps Luke cuts wampa arm off”; “Search outtakes of Yoda throwing can”; “Double-print entire sequence [of dark side tree]”; “Play some of Yoda line, ‘You will destroy all that they have worked for’ over Luke”; “Add new wide shot Cloud City before Leia in VistaVision plate”; “Remove Vader line, ‘You have less power than you think’ ”; “CS Vader with fist, ‘If you only knew the power of the dark side’ ”; “Vader hollers, ‘Luke!’ when Luke falls”; “Extend head of Chewie growl to play over off-screen Luke line, ‘May the Force be with you.’ ” Lucas: “*Empire* is a completely cinematic, editorial way of making a movie… using Eisenstein’s philosophies to actually move people.” “To help the editors, Kershner performed for Burtt a guide-track of Vader’s dialogue with several new and amended lines.” By October 29 Lucas: “Now that we’ve finished the fine cut on *Empire*, I’m leaving for Japan. I’m executive producer of a film being directed there, *Kagemusha* [1980], by Kurosawa.” Johnston, two days later, on going through the cutting copy for live-action-plus-blue-backing sequences, then storyboards for camera and model builders. Caption: new opening storyboards by Johnston, October 15, 1979, “in which a Star Destroyer unleashes a barrage of probe droids”; revised again by Lucas and Johnston on October 26 and November 12, 1979 (“To be done much later”). EE: early black-and-white dupe cut with animatics “as well as a moment that won’t make the final cut—the death of Commander Veers (Julian Glover)” (1:19).

## OPTIMAL OPTICAL (pp. 259–262)

John May, preparing the *Empire Collector’s Edition* booklet, is toured by Brian Johnson: workshop, computer-program room, matte-painting studio (Ellenshaw and team; Ellenshaw: “I finished working on *The Black Hole* [1979] on a Friday and Monday I was here”), model shop “creative chaos,” original *Millennium Falcon* model under dust, snowspeeder “that we’re going to crush.” Peterson: 7 of 12 people are full-range model makers. ILM cost sheet (record separately from the Chapter Eight “about 150,000 lights” figure; do not collapse): “*Executor*, 250,000 lights, total cost $42,000; 10-foot Star Destroyer, $55,000; walker, $80,000 to $100,000; Boba Fett ship, around $15,000. Small *Falcon*, 18 inches diameter, about 13 pounds, $10,000.” Quad printer finally up: designed by Edlund; camera and projector movements by George Randle; special optics by David Grafton; electronics by Jerry Jeffress and Michael MacKenzie. Four-headed optical beam-splitter printer: two pairs of VistaVision projectors, distortion-free relay, anamorph to two-to-one for intercut with Panavision live action. Edlund: “In about 30 to 40 percent of the shots, we have actually had to degrade the image in order to make it look real.” Nicholson: George found some Quad composites “too sharp,” so they “de-saturate.” Franklin: ultraprecision alignment; “cumbersome.” Nicholson: “composite 415 shots in less than one year”; Apple computer streamlines lineup (Franklin and Mark Vargo). Edlund: some asteroid shots “as many as 25 separately filmed elements”; with separation positives, 75 passes, halved by the beam-splitter. In-house Treise Processor for black-and-white intermediates. Stocks: 5302 and 5235; Muren on high-contrast hard edge vs. transparency trade-off. Optical operators: Dave Berry, Ken Smith, Don Clark. Kurtz, late October: first composites from the new printer “look very, very good”; work “should be done by the end of February,” allowing slop-over into March “if it goes like it did last time on *Star Wars*—which it probably will.” Lucas shows Michael Powell around ILM.

## YES, WE CAN (pp. 262–264)

October into November: “a locked film, more or less.” Johnson on teamwork. Ralston and Johnston illegally shoot stuff / helium zeppelins with rockets in the vacant lot next door. Lucas: last time, one person on mechanical effects and one on special effects; “Brian Johnson is really overseeing both of them.” Edlund and Muren “are the ones who are actually making the creative decisions” daily on set. Muren on discipline: stop, analyze, “a nice degree of reality.” Johnston: “The people at ILM had all worked with George first; George was the big boss and Kershner was second in command… I think he probably grumbled about some stuff. I know he did, ’cause I heard him.” Kershner: “I feel that George Lucas is a conceptual artist in a very profound way. That doesn’t necessarily mean that his work is profound. I wouldn’t attempt to judge it. But he is an artist; he is unique in what he does.” Johnson: Kershner’s contribution was “enormous”; “even George Lucas says that he did a better job with the actors. But, ultimately, it’s Lucas’s vision. He designed and wrote the film, and directed the special effects in California… Kersh made some changes, but it was George’s picture.” Patricia Blau: in visual-effects post, “the director was much less involved”; “George loves to play in the editing room.” Ralston on an 11-frame cut: “You get the basic bold move and a big lighting gag… I don’t care if there are some flaws in a shot—it’s the energy I want.” Lucas: animatics gave a precise frame count; “probably 10 times more shots than anybody had ever done before,” so they had to “cost 10 times less”; “this will be on the screen 12 frames, half a second, or 18 frames and that’s it.” Johnson: “The whole production concept is just doing what George wants… He is basically a storyteller and has an innate sense of timing.” Tippett: best directors (Lucas, Spielberg) say, “Here’s what I need editorially and dramatically. Now, you go do it.” Ralston cuts “little crummy models on sticks” with sound “just for laughs”; “George really stuck to how long the cuts were on it.” Muren: spaceship motions “made by hand with a joystick, instinctively, by the cameraman himself.” Ralston wanted a small *Falcon* “instead of that big monster that you had to move with a forklift”; escape from the Star Destroyers: “*Falcon*’s doing big acrobatic jumps and falls, and the TIE ships are like bees or flies.” Edlund: “not a computer system… a solid core memory system… all done by humans and not by computers”; a mathematician’s “perfectly mathematical parabola” is “so perfect that it’s not interesting”; the human element gives “the look of a guy out there with a handheld Arriflex.” Muren: “The thing that I’ve always been weak on and that George is good at, is coming up with the ideas.” Lucas: “When I’m making a movie, I’m just like any other crazy artist. My father said, ‘Never make your hobby your profession.’ But I’m willing to do stupid things to make it great, which most people won’t do.” Ralston: “George never micromanaged anything.” Gloss (source typo “Utimately”): Muren’s team initially found it hard to create the Star Destroyers’ enormity; Muren “made use of a star as a light source.”

## LIBERATING THE MIND (pp. 264–266)

Kurtz: picture to be shown to “Johnny Williams” so composition can begin November 1; record just after Christmas; sound effects already started, robot dialogue being cut; re-recording January into February, then lab and negative cutting. Friday, November 2: Arnold watches Kershner rummage LPs (Stravinsky, Shostakovich, Hindemith, Copland, Prokofiev, Bliss) for a temp track before Williams views the film. Lucas, Kershner, and Williams then sit down to view the fine cut and spot the music at the Carriage House theater at Park House (projectionist Howie Hammerman). Williams: “I’m beginning a little further ahead of myself than is usual, because the score will reprise some music from *Star Wars*… sound like part of an organic whole.” Saturday, November 17: Arnold interviews Williams at home in Los Angeles. Williams: “*Empire* will require 107 minutes of underscore… the equivalent of several Lisztian tone poems. There is a new theme for Yoda, which begins in a kind of piquant way and develops into a more profound, nobler piece. There will be a Love Theme developing from the love interest between Princess Leia and Han. There will be a new piece of music for Darth Vader, who plays a more important role in this film. In *Star Wars*, he had what you could call a musical fragment, but in the new picture there will be a Grand Imperial March.” Influences acknowledged: Walton, Bliss, Vaughan Williams, Britten. On composing: “Sometimes themes come very painfully after hours of holding my head in my hands at the piano… Then I’ll sit down at the piano and it sort of pops into my mind… It was like exhuming another part of myself in a way, to have to go back and continue a score that was done that long ago.” Caption typography: music editor Ken Wannberg, music supervisor (uncredited) Lionel Newman, Williams, and Kershner, early November 1979; Dunham-to-Wannberg notes November 5; revised opening music notes December 4; Herbert W. Spencer orchestration, “Yoda’s Entrance,” reel 5. EE: spotting session including a look at the carbon-freeze scene (1:25). Ralston: “The Warner Bros. cartoons really taught me a lot about timing and energy… Chuck Jones said about how one frame can make a scene funny—or not.”

## SHOOTING THE TAUNTAUN (pp. 267–270)

Mid-November: Tippett and Muren start stop-motion photography for the (few) tauntaun shots; weeks, then months. Motorized brace clamps the model to the table. Tippett: moves of “a tenth of an inch to a quarter of an inch”; “A shot that’s two or three seconds long could take us an entire day or half a day.” Muren sometimes needs up to five hours to match Finse lighting. Tippett to *Cinefex*: “That was probably the most grueling animation on the whole show, having to be under the tables.” By December, after rotoscope by Peter Kuran and Sam Comstock, Lucas approves the first tauntaun stop-motion shots. One sequence does not make the cut: Tippett animated two shots of the Han puppet tossing a sentry marker, turning the steed, and cantering out of frame. Muren: “there was something about it that didn’t look real… the camera was too close on the taun. What George really wanted was a long shot, so we quickly did a replacement shot of the animal running way off in the distance.” Motion-blur via the motorized brace moving the model slightly during each exposure. Tippett: “What we’re trying to do is simulate that effect by getting blurs into the stop-motion.” The shot of Han’s tauntaun riding up as Obi-Wan’s image fades “was actually the first motion-control taun shot where we introduced blurs on the puppet,” Muren says; “I don’t think it was entirely satisfactory.” A similar shot of the tauntaun running up to camera, not bluescreen: “It’s the best taun shot in the film, I think. So does George.” They hook a stop-motion puppet to motion-control on a pylon. Then Lucas wants a tauntaun run into a hangar plate already shot on the Star Wars Stage. Muren: haze, lens flare, no place for a split, two pools of light; “to get rid of the matte lines, we settled for a bit of transparency. In the original negative, you could see the lights ghosting through the tauntaun as it runs.” Kershner of the live-action portion: “Somebody had to run with a cutout of a tauntaun from the door through the hangar.” Tippett: George “initially would have preferred to have the character be a lot more free-acting,” but saw the advantages of blur. EE: early cut, without sound, “a wampa sneaking out from under the snow and attacking Luke, in a scene that won’t make the final film” (0:23). Second EE cut: “The probot emerges—and blasts to smithereens a ‘snow squirrel,’ as represented by squirrelly animatics. Also seen here is the original wampa, which ILM will replace, who is cut across the chest rather than losing an arm” (0:55). Flag: Lucas’s October note is “Perhaps Luke cuts wampa arm off”; the EE early cut shows a chest cut. Do not collapse the early-cut wampa wound with the later arm cut or with the finished film.

## MATTE-LINE MADNESS (pp. 270–271)

Arnold at dailies: *Falcon* through the asteroid belt; technicians not satisfied with the maneuvering. MacKenzie: the Hoth-evacuation transport “kept showing up. They called it at one point, the ‘space salmon’ ”—meant to be gray against white snow, kept coming out “slight salmon-pink.” Dailies comments usually from the second row (Lucas, Edlund, Johnson, Kurtz, Johnston, Bloom) and from Muren standing in the back. Ellenshaw: “George had a great regard for Dennis.” Kuran: harder to hide flaws against white. Lucas, “even decades later”: “I still remember sitting at dailies and everyone would yell out, ‘Matte lines!’ ” Tom Smith: early snow-planet comps “discouraging… fake and pasted on.” Nicholson’s solution: double-expose a ship with a section of the same Finse scene; “The trick worked. It desaturated the ship.” When Lucas asked how, Nicholson said he’d used a “Norway Filter.” Comstock: tauntaun dailies “would come up and everyone would get knocked out of their seats!”—bags and packs bobbing, Luke bobbing as the taun breathes and gallops. December 29, 1979, “laser list” for the snow battle. Caption: early 1979 Johnston Emperor concepts (nos. 346 and 347) informed storyboards of October 15 and November 27, 1979; a production sketch of scene 95 showed “the relative sizes of the ‘Galactic’ Emperor versus Vader: 12′ to 6′.” A Ralston doodle “bore an uncanny resemblance to the Emperor concepts.” EE: Williams with Wannberg and Herb Spencer, concentrating on the mynock scene (1:42).

## MUD AND METAL (pp. 272–273)

Post moves again, to 165 Tunstead Avenue, bought for $175,000 (Burtt, Summers, Dunham, Starkey, Hammerman, Laurel Ladevich). Ladevich: building still under construction, freezing concrete slab, coats until noon, only cold water for dishes. Burtt: “On *Empire*, I was technically much better equipped… a much more vigorous and extensive laser swordfight at the end of *Empire*… elevate it to a higher level in terms of the environment, the sound, the Carbon Freezing Chamber, and all that.” Interim work on *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* and *Alien*. “The innocence was gone. We felt great pressure to outdo ourselves.” Randy Thom, after *Apocalypse Now*, collects field sounds for several months. Burtt: 1,000-plus sounds; coasting down mountain roads, engine off, for Hoth wind; foxholes at White Sands Missile Range; Ken Strickfaden’s *Frankenstein* (1931) laboratory machines placed “here and there.” Dagobah footsteps: “recordings in the mud at the ranch”; bog animals from gulls, terns, shorebirds, sea lions, dolphins, and a water-treatment-plant pump. Nagra quarter-inch machines. Burtt: “The *Empire* sound image is more organized, more martial… Volume for the sake of sheer volume is nonsense. Sound is a coloring element. It should be used with subtlety.” Walkers: Thom records metal shears / “big metallic stamping machines” (Eden National Steel and Midland Ross Metal, Oakland, October 24, 1979; also “torture room doors”); Burtt makes a rhythmic walk cycle and gets knee-joint squeaks “by playing with a dumpster lid, which had been dropped off in front of my house.” Thom: real-world sounds processed—backward, speed-altered, or through electronic circuits. Burtt: Cadillac Eldorado door-window motors become Threepio’s walk; tauntaun voice “concocted from the recording of an Asian sea otter named Mota, which we pitched down a little”; San Francisco Bay foghorns “in the background of Cloud City’s audio track.” Caption: a week at Oshkosh; a 707 becomes different vehicles including snowspeeders; steel guy-wire twang as the basis of blaster sounds (caption typo “Miki Hermann”). EE: Burtt with Bonnie Koehler on the *Falcon* failing “one last time” to jump to hyperspace (1:27).

## CHRISTMAS STAGS (pp. 273–276)

December: following the June announcement, Alan Ladd Jr. leaves Fox; stock falls from $40.63 to $38.87 the day the news reaches the public. Kasdan, writing *Body Heat*: “Alan Ladd got fired… Sherry Lansing was the head of Fox and she put me in turnaround.” He takes it to Ladd’s new company; Ladd will make it if Kasdan gets a director to sponsor him. “So I went to George and asked him and he said, ‘I’ll sponsor you, but I can’t put the Lucasfilm name on it because your film is too sexual, too adult; I’m trying to establish a family entertainment company.’… He told Ladd, ‘I’ll be the executive producer’—and this was the amazing part—‘and if Larry goes over budget, you can use my fee to defray the costs.’ ” Black Falcon is merged into Lucasfilm. Kershner travels to Tokyo to promote *Empire*, stays at the Imperial Hotel, visits Kurosawa on the *Kagemusha* set. ILM: Muren, Tippett, and Berg continue walker sequences; Edlund focuses on pyrotechnic walker shots with larger models and the HSE (high-speed Empire) camera. Johnston has the model shop build a prototype two-legged walker, designed by himself and Jon Berg—“the ‘chicken walker’ (so nicknamed due to its strut)”—based on the earliest walker concepts. Lucas likes it and says “if there was time for an animator to build a stop-motion model, then he would find a place for it in the film.” Tom Smith: “there were practically no women, which had a bad cultural effect on the building… people got really raunchy”; special effort to hire more women in coordinator positions; “if you look at the man–woman ratio, it was terrible.” Patricia Blau, one of the few women on staff: “We were ready to throw ourselves in front of the train.” Paging system: running into the back and screaming, “Dennis Muren, line two!” Smith: some put newspaper ads, “Have a date, go to Lucas’s Christmas party?” Tippett: “30-something-year-old boss who was noncorporate… away from all the adults in Los Angeles”; come in at 12 or 1 because the lab was in Washington and dailies arrived late; “when things really started getting hot, we were working around the clock.” Ralston: nine months shooting nights, “which I hated”; notes and pictures left for the day crew; “we didn’t want people taking them over—we were egomaniacal about it: ‘This is *my* sequence. You can’t have it!’ ” Storyboards added November 8, 1979: *Falcon* pursued by TIEs toward the *Executor* (end of the film). John Van Vliet joke “Star Wars Screening Room Game”; “*Slave I*’s resemblance to an iron”; “Roto suicide.” Printer’s proof of a final airbrushed marketing frame: “the color of the Rebel cruiser had finally been worked out.”
