## VOYAGE TO VICTORY (pp. 328–330)

Sneak preview at the Dominion Theater, May 6; world premiere at the Odeon, London, May 20; “the US preview having taken place three days earlier.” Kershner: Lucas called and said, “Next week, you’re going to fly over to London for the premiere.” Kershner asked when *we* were going; Lucas: “No, no, no. *You’re* going. It’s your film.” Prowse on the paternity revelation: “I felt ridiculous. I thought I was saying one thing and here they have me saying another.” Kershner: “When the picture was shown at the Odeon, Darth Vader was sitting in back of me. And when he saw ‘I am your father,’ he tapped me on the shoulder and said, ‘Why didn’t you tell me? I would’ve done it differently!’ ” Keep distinct from the on-set briefing (Prowse given a dummy line; Hamill and selected others told the real line) and from Jones’s late-1979/early-1980 first reading.

DC premiere at the Kennedy Center, a Special Olympics benefit. Eunice Kennedy Shriver: Special Olympians “give others the same precious prize I believe George Lucas is trying to express in the *Star Wars* films: faith in the unlimited possibilities of the human spirit.” Pre-film luncheon of hot dogs, popcorn, and cotton candy; Fisher, Hamill, Ford, Billy Dee Williams, Oz, Baker, Prowse, Mayhew (Daniels “bedridden at Sibley Hospital with what he called ‘some kind of blood poisoning’ ”). Cast signed autographs for some 600 children, including 300 Special Olympians; 1,100 people at the premiere. Also on hand: Kershner, Kasdan, Kurtz, Ted Kennedy, Amy Carter, Ethel Kennedy, Arnold Schwarzenegger. *Tiger Beat* fans Lisa Timchalk and Teresa Perry: Hamill received their note and found them; standing room. A reporter: “nearly half the dialogue was drowned out with squeals of joy and welcoming applause for each character.” Tony Kornheiser, *The Washington Post*: “wild, unbridled cheers.” Loew’s State, New York, preview May 9 (Terry Lawson, Dayton *Journal Herald*, May 19): Peter Yates called it a “remarkable film”; James Woods “thought it was better than the original” and “It’s going to make … loads of money.” Next morning at the Essex House, Kershner in “ill-disguised anger”: “What you’re asking is, *Was I a technician on this picture or was I a director, right?* Well, dammit, I was the director, ’cause I worked on this picture for two years and two months. I followed through … It’s my damn picture.”

## THE HOUSE OF *STAR WARS* (pp. 330–332)

May 21: 70mm run begins. *Variety* reported first day as only 41 theaters in 16 cities; the initial week had the film in 127 theaters (Northpoint San Francisco premiere benefited the Pacific Film Archive; Astor Plaza, Murray Hill, Orpheum 1 in New York; Avco in Westwood). Midnight opening at the Egyptian in Los Angeles, then “kept running it without stopping for one full day.” Little competition except Kubrick’s *The Shining* (limited, two days later) and *Friday the 13th* (since May 9). Wider 35mm planned for June 18, then even wider later in summer. Usual 90–10 split after house costs, with theater share rising over weeks. *The Washington Post*: 750 in line for the 11 AM show; two parents wrote notes to excuse kids from school; Amy Carter’s second time, “in designer jeans.” Tom Finn: “The print’s usually better if you go early.” Maureen Garrett, Lucasfilm Fan Club: “hundreds upon hundreds… wearing costumes, countdown signs.” David Seltzer, manager of the K-B Cerberus, midnight show: “Everyone started to applaud for each name [on the roll-up]. When Darth Vader’s name first came up on the screen, a hiss started in the front of the theater and went through the whole place. The hair on my arms just went electric.” Peter Myers, Fox senior VP of domestic distribution: “People were yelling and screaming!” Number one its first week, beating *Friday the 13th* and *The Shining*; broke 125 of 127 house records for opening day—“a new industry record for the highest single-day per-theater gross.” First week, Lucasfilm internal notes: $9,601,374. Unidentified Fox executive: “We never thought it would beat *Star Wars*.” Most of the gross from the Egyptian, $269,720; Chicago $123,000. *The New York Times*: *Empire* $77,000 average per theater; *The Shining* even better at $100,000. Ticket prices up: same NYC theater that had *Star Wars* at $4 now $5; Grauman’s $5.50, “a new high”; Delaware still $2. May 26: President Jimmy Carter invited Chinese Vice Premier Geng Biao to see “the Son of Star Wars.” Kurtz on 70mm: “The big advantage of 70mm was there were no pirated copies floating about until the 35mm came out.”

## IT WAS PRINTED HERE (pp. 332–333)

Major reviews mixed; with Guinness “gone as a major player, many critics began to focus on the film’s technical side,” though Yoda received attention. Arthur Knight, *The Hollywood Reporter*, May 12 (mistakenly reports Yoda as played by Bulloch): “While *Empire* doesn’t quite measure up to *Star Wars* in the freshness and originality of its script …, this 20th Century–Fox release remains a rattling good entertainment… On the story side as well, Lucas has strengthened his hand by providing a plot motivation for Darth Vader … Suffice it to say that it’s a twist straight out of Greek mythology, and should serve Lucas well in the episodes that lie ahead.” James Harwood, *Daily Variety*: “like walking through the front gate of Disneyland, where good and evil are never confused and the righteous will always win.” David Ansen, *Newsweek*: “The ‘gee-whiz’ spirit lives on… generosity that surpasses even the original,” but “nothing really at stake—‘Halfway through *Empire*, I began to feel a strong sense of diminishing wonder.’ ” Charles Champlin, LA *Times*: “the quality of parable… a moral imperative in the film’s view of hard work, determination, self-improvement, concentration, and idealism.” Vincent Canby, *The New York Times*, disliked it: “It looks like a movie that was directed at a distance.” Gary Arnold, *The Washington Post*: “This transitional, eerie, deliberately unresolved sequel activates a climactic psychological bombshell, aligning the story in a powerful, sinister new direction, full of dreadful implications for the original movie and the sequels ahead… *Empire* pulls the carpet out from under you while simultaneously soaring along.” Roger Angell, *The New Yorker*: “a pseudo-Sophoclean, outer-Freudian turn of events involving Luke and Darth Vader… Yoda astounds us… the joyful extra touch of life… Yoda has perched himself up on Luke Skywalker’s back, where he clings and talks and gestures like a tiny Sinbad or a sentient backpack. This is wit.” Lucas: “I don’t pay attention to critics anymore. I try to read the good reviews and ignore the bad ones.”

## A TWIST ENDING (pp. 333–334)

After the 70mm opening, with about 30 people left at ILM, Lucas phones Tom Smith: “We need some more shots for *Empire.*… The film’s in the theater!… ‘No, no, no—it’s not in *all* the theaters.’ ” Ralston is on vacation when Blau calls: “George wants to add some more shots.” Smith: “George had gone to see the movie with an audience and realized that the end of the film was unclear. So he needed three new shots and he couldn’t do them without special effects.” Ralston meets Johnston at the Egg Company with George to design three new shots. “It took about three weeks and the new shots were cut into the 35mm prints going out to all the theaters. George saw the new shots and he said, ‘Wait a minute. If you guys did this so fast, why did it take so long to do all the other ones?’ ” Smith: “building a couple quick models and recycling stock shots for the smaller moving spaceships… The editing room had a tough time extending the music and putting in new lines of dialogue, but it was all done in time.” Caption: original three-page Johnston-to-Smith fax, circa late May 1980, “with contemporary coffee-cup stain on No. 1.”

## LASHING OUT (p. 335)

May 28: the Directors Guild of America sends a letter to Lucasfilm asking $250,000 in penalties because the director’s credit had been placed at the end; because of a lack of unit production manager and “first and second assistant directors on a separate card in a more prominent place”; and, in the guild’s view, other DGA Basic Agreement violations. (Chapter Nine recorded that the official DGA-waiver request “seems to have been neglected”; do not collapse that earlier note into this fine.) The film had been made by Lucas’s English production company and was subject only to that country’s union rules, which had been followed; unable to sue Lucasfilm, the DGA “turned around and fined their own member, Kershner, $25,000.” Kazanjian: “Lucas was so angry that he resigned from the Directors Guild.” Ganis: Lucas “paid Kershner’s fine of $25,000… He said that where the credits are placed is a creative decision and shouldn’t be dictated by anybody, including the guild.” Ganis in 2003: “every time I look at a movie today… it has credits in exactly the form that George had on *Empire*.” June 5, *The Atlanta Journal*: 19 moviegoers “striking back at an Atlanta theater to the tune of more than $500,000” in a class-action over misleading showtimes (a canceled 10 AM show after a 75-mile trip).

## A BIGGER BANG (pp. 335–336)

June 18: *Empire* goes out in 35mm “with its additional last-second shots,” expanding onto 115 more screens; 116 added two days later; eventually more than 1,400 theaters; Fox marketing research, audience male–female ratio 60–40. *Commentary*, largely negative: “Lucas, with his particularly intimate feeling for the joys and longings of the juvenile mind, is still engaged in the vast enterprise of proving that the world of the child is superior to the world of the adult.” Live 6 o’clock news at Loew’s Astor Plaza: a man in line would sell his ticket for “something in the order of 500.” *The Maui News*: film broke “15 minutes before the ending,” jeers and boos until refunds. United States officially in a recession; *Variety* headlines *Empire* “Major Exception to B.O. Slump” after $65 million in its first five weeks. *The Blues Brothers* (June 16, co-starring Fisher) had less than half of *Empire*’s fifth-week grosses. “Financially, within three months, Lucas had recouped his risky investment.” August 6 letter from loan officer Robert L. Wallace Jr. to Lucasfilm treasurer Chris Kalabokes: “Now that *Empire* has done exactly what all of you at Lucasfilm said it would do, I guess that it is time for your bankers to shift their focus from Chapter II Co. to ‘Chapter II’ of our relationship [that is, the third film].” *Billboard*, July 26: Freddie Mercury closed a Queen concert “riding on the shoulders of a stagehand dressed in a Darth Vader costume.” June 4, *Variety*: Leicester Square 2,000-seat run, $213,562 in 32 performances, “an all-time record for any picture in the UK.” Venice Film Festival; August opening in Brisbane; number one in Tokyo for at least four weeks. Denmark ruled it off-limits to children under 12 (as were *Star Wars*, *Close Encounters*, and others). Dr. Joergen Bruun Petersen: “Children are not allowed to see a film that desensitizes them to violence, to suffering… They must not see a film if we feel they will get [from it] less ability to feel pity.” Sex on screen was allowed “only if they express love for each other, do what they do with feeling.”

## THE GREATER GALLERY (pp. 336–339)

Kershner: “I’ve had two of the best and most creative years of my entire life on *Empire*… A good dream because I worked with interesting people and it was nice being in London. A bad dream because of the frustration and things not working; running out of time… But there has to be a limit as to how much a picture costs.” Lucas: “The first film was like graduating from high school, the second film was like graduating from college.” Ford: “I asked Irvin if I had to read this section or that… He said there was no need to. So when I finally saw the finished movie, I learned for the first time all the things that happened to Luke. It was great.” Fisher, *Soho Weekly News*: “I love the film … because you can be transported. It’s very childlike so you can be very childlike.” Hamill: “I felt curiously detached watching *Empire*… thinking, *Yes, you are a terrible actor and it was only the special effects that made it all memorable*.” Billy Dee Williams: “This is just the introduction of Lando, so I’ll be in the next episode… I gotta go save Han’s life now, me and the Wookiee.” Ganis on the *Time* cover: “quintessential.” Prowse: “Here I am on the cover of *Time*… But nobody knows me. I’m still not recognized when I walk down the street.” Suschitzky: “I haven’t enjoyed myself so much on a film for a very long time.” Kershner: “I didn’t make the film to be successful or unsuccessful… I wanted the time spent in making the film to be a good time for me.” Ford: “two such different men were able to make two films that meld perfectly… George wasn’t on hand for much of the second film—but the idea was. And it’s Kershner’s discipline, talent, and technique that serviced the idea.” Johnston: “there was never any doubt in my mind… that *Empire* was a George Lucas film from beginning to end.” Fisher on merchandising: “Harrison used to get so upset: ‘Mark gets to be a puzzle, why don’t I?!’ ” Hamill: “I’ve been marked down in price… They had sold out Darth Vader, Chewbacca, and See-Threepio, so I was the only one available. There were just boxes and boxes of me.” Lucas: “The surprising thing to me was that we were able to pull off the reality of Yoda… the key to Yoda is that there’s a hugely talented actor behind it.” Oz: “I’ve talked to George about doing Yoda again… If I can work it into my schedule, I’d love to do Yoda again.” Hamill: initially disappointed he “didn’t get praise for the Dagobah sequence,” then “the greatest compliment of all… no one notices.” “Whereas Edlund and Lucas had rated the special effects of *Star Wars* as 3.5 on a scale of 10, Edlund gave *Empire* a 6.5.” Lucas: “With *Star Wars*, we were inventing the medium… At least *Empire* looks more like a finished product. The quality was much better on all levels… I was very happy with ILM.” Muren: “*Empire* is the hardest film I’ve ever worked on and probably the most rewarding.” Blau, transcribing dialogue for translation sheets without having seen the movie: “I heard Darth Vader on the tape say, ‘Apology accepted.’ I thought, *Wow, that’s different*. But when I watched the movie, I realized that at that exact same moment Darth was using the Force to choke his lieutenant to death!” (Chapter Five recorded “Apology accepted” as “improvised on set (or later in post)”; do not collapse the two notes.) Burtt: better stereo stability, but “if the theaters don’t also own it, the work all comes to naught.” Hamill at Alice’s Restaurant, Malibu, quoting Lucas: “George tells me that the wisest thing I could tell anyone is that I’m retired.” Kershner in 2009: mail from Finland and Japan; a psychologist keeps “a little statue of Yoda on my desk.” Lucas: “To me, film is a historical document… People 500 years from now will look at our films and be able to figure out what we were like.” Caption: *Dynamite* “asked readers whether Darth Vader was really Luke’s father.”

## MYSTERIOUS MEANINGS (pp. 339–343; EE folio disorder places a `page354` marker in mid-section)

Rinzler gloss: “Darth Vader’s avowal constituted one of the greatest plot twists in cinema history. Its only rival is the revelation at the end of *Planet of the Apes* (1968).” “Audiences were stunned by Vader’s claim, but many were divided over whether he was telling the truth.” “Only Lucas knew for sure what was going to happen, and he wasn’t telling anyone.” People also wondered who the Emperor was “beneath his hooded cloak—and who would be revealed as the other mentioned by Yoda.” “Quite a few guessed that the Emperor was a clone, gone bad, of Obi-Wan; Jeremy Bulloch received a letter asking him if Boba Fett was the ‘other.’ ” Kershner’s survey: “I found that children up to the age of about seven didn’t believe that Darth Vader was Luke’s father. They think he’s lying. Above the age of seven, they accept it—and it sends a chill up their spine.” Lucas: “I was very concerned about the ending, that it might be too intense for small children. That is a pretty intense moment and basically a castration scene. But I spoke to a number of psychologists who basically said that most kids, if it’s too intense, will simply deny that Vader is Luke’s father. But I was also concerned about leaving kids hanging.” Hamill: “Darth Vader is a good example of changing a character to please the people. I think, originally, if you follow classic drama, I would have to kill him in the third episode. But now he’s a cult figure and, in a way, George may not want to do away with him. Ultimately, the Emperor should be the main bad guy, someone you try to get through the nine movies—and in the ninth, you succeed. I don’t know who the Emperor is, but I think it is fascinating the way they put him together.” *Presbyterian Journal* (Asheville): “The whole theological and ethical foundation is more Zoroastrian and Buddhist than it is Judeo-Christian… Worse than that, the movie has strong overtones of Eastern religion where God is merely an impersonal force.” *Christianity Today*: Lucas “less threatening” than Kubrick’s *2001*, but still lacking “the *why* behind all the drama—the main problem in both cases being an absence of Jesus and a righteous God.” Rinzler gloss: “To sum up, *Empire* was somehow problematic because its ethics were simultaneously too personal and yet too impersonal.” Lucas: “Whenever you have a teacher in a movie, they often carry more weight than the hero does… taking old truths and reiterating them.” Kasdan: “The Jedi Knights are enlightened warrior-priests… The idea of having an evil father and a good father is very common in mythology… we have within us the dark side and the light, good and evil, the devil and the angel.” Kershner: “The film *may* have religious qualities, but I wasn’t working toward that. I wanted to stay away from magic and concentrate on people’s untapped powers… Someone told me it was Jungian. Someone else told me it was pure Christianity… He said Darth Vader is the fallen angel, the closest thing to Lucifer you can find. Actually, Vader wants to bring order to the universe*—his* order, *his* universe. He’s the ultimate dictator.” Lucas: “The Force evolved out of various developments of character and plot. I wanted a concept of religion based on the premise that there is a God and there is good and evil. I began to distill the essence of all religions into what I thought was a basic idea common to all religions and common to primitive thinking. I wanted to develop something that was nondenominational but still had a kind of religious reality. I believe in God… ‘Thou shalt not kill.’ ‘Do unto others …’ is the philosophy that permeates my work.”

## FANTASTIC STATISTICS (pp. 344–346)

Though Fox was “merely a distributor,” after release the company’s stock “jumped $13, to a healthy $53 a share.” By September, *Variety*: $160 million (70mm prints around $69 million), “16 percent of the total summer revenue,” almost triple *Airplane!* Weber: Stigwood on the Williams score, “You only gave me one new song! Everything else is still the old *Star Wars*”; record still sold one million copies by the end of August, but “huge piracy problems” softened the RSO relationship. Novelization by Donald F. Glut, Ballantine: two million copies “purchased by the end of the film’s first week.” Phipps Plaza, Atlanta, more than seven months, $500,000; two girls saw it “between 40 and 50 times.” Finished 1980 as box-office champ, “earning nearly twice as much as the number two film for the year, *Kramer vs. Kramer*.” “Empire‘s Achilles’ heel was that it didn’t get the kind of repeat viewing that *Star Wars* enjoyed—although it did become the third-largest-grossing movie of all time, after *Star Wars* and *Jaws* (1975).” Statement of participation, Fox to the Chapter II Company, July 14, 1981: *Empire* rentals “had exceeded $250 million.” October 1981 reissue: another $26.3 million; “took second place on *Variety*‘s list of all-time domestic box-office champs, overtaking *Jaws* to sit behind *Star Wars*.” (*Raiders of the Lost Ark* was then number four; “Lucas had three out of the top four films of all time.”) Lucas: “I’m just as used to having things fail as I am to having them succeed… I usually have to bet the store in order to make it work.” *Battlestar Galactica* lawsuit (source typography “Battlestar Galatica”): October 2, 1980, US District Judge Irving Hill threw out Fox’s case; May 8, 1981, MCA/Universal countersuit thrown out; early 1983, US Court of Appeals ruled Fox’s case “should be settled in a courtroom… ‘After reviewing the *Star Wars* and *Battlestar* motion pictures, we conclude that the films do in fact raise genuine issues of material fact as to whether only the *Star Wars* idea or the expression of that idea was copied.’ ” November 18, 1983, Agreement for Settlement; March 5, 1984, a $225,000 settlement “was paid to Twentieth Century–Fox.” Tom Jung “Style B” poster, more action than the romance-oriented first, appearing about a month after release.

## ACADEMIC ABYSS (pp. 346–347)

NATO named Kershner director of the year, October 1980. March, LA *Times* critics’ top-10 summary: *Ordinary People* on 42 lists; *Empire* fourth, on 24. People’s Choice Award for Best Motion Picture, accepted by Kershner, Hamill, and Billy Dee Williams. Academy of Science Fiction Fantasy and Horror Films: best picture, director, and actor (Hamill). Williams: BAFTA, Grammy, Oscar nomination; *Empire* also nominated for Best Art Direction and Best Sound. Yoda Oscar speculation: Peter H. Brown, LA *Times*, on whether a “nonhuman” could win; Fox and Lucasfilm “seeking nominations for Darth Vader (with body by Prowse and voice by Jones), Yoda the Muppet (voice and movements by Frank Oz), and See-Threepio (Anthony Daniels).” Art Sarno’s reading of the rules: “It looks like Darth Vader would be out since Prowse supplies the body and actions and Jones supplies the entire voice. Yoda and See-Threepio might be eligible.” None nominated. Lucas: “We tried to get Frank Oz a nomination. But the Screen Actors Guild said that puppeteers aren’t actors, which I thought was outrageous… It was a brilliant performance and it *is* acting.” March 31, after the ceremony was delayed 24 hours because of an assassination attempt on President Ronald Reagan: Best Sound to re-recording mixers Bill Varney, Steve Maslow, Gregg Landaker, and production soundman Peter Sutton; Special Achievement Academy Award for Visual Effects to Richard Edlund, Brian Johnson, Dennis Muren, and Bruce Nicholson. Burtt’s name had been submitted with the sound group, “but Academy rules wouldn’t admit him.” Lucas: “I was disappointed because Ben did most of the work. Down there, they don’t recognize *Sound Designer* as a credit.” Franklin on the optical printer’s Academy Award: “the printer never got used the way it was planned to be used… it has a little statuette that goes with it now.” (Within three years, split into two conventional printers.) German theatrical poster “one of the few to have Yoda in it.” Jung painted a 1982 re-release poster featuring Yoda and “a prominent Han Solo (Harrison Ford being a megastar at that point thanks to *Raiders of the Lost Ark*, 1981).”

## YOU CAN’T GO HOME (pp. 347–348)

*Empire* is “only Episode V”; Episode VI already begun in 1980, scheduled for 1983. Isaac Asimov on David Letterman, October 1980: “I enjoyed *The Empire Strikes Back* so much that when they finished it, I jumped up in my seat and yelled, ‘*Start the third part!*’ I figure at the rate they’re going, they’ll do the last few after I’m dead, which doesn’t strike me as fair.” (Rinzler parenthetical: “Asimov passed away in 1992.”) Gary Arnold, *The Washington Post*: waiting years for the next chapter “may be a ‘landmark in the history of popular culture.’ ” Angell: “I’m not sure that I’m up to seven more *Star Wars* adventures (I’m pretty sure that my son is), but I can hardly wait for the next one.” “One big question about the third film had already been answered for the public in August 1980: Harrison Ford had agreed to play Han Solo one more time.” Lucas: “The next chapter is called *Revenge of the Jedi*. It’s the end of this particular trilogy, the conclusion of the conflict begun in *Star Wars* between Luke and Darth Vader. It resolves that situation once and for all. I won’t say who survives and who doesn’t, but if we are ever able to link together all three, you’d find the story progresses in a very logical fashion.” Ford: people disappointed with the ending “contradict themselves every time, because their next comment is always: ‘I can’t wait for the third one.’ ” Lucas: “I always knew I’d have a problem with *Empire* because it was the second act, a down movie, and didn’t have an ending. I had to get from number one to number three. I knew if I could just get through number two, I’d be okay. In the second film, once we introduce the ‘other,’ it creates tension over whether Luke’s going to die or not. There is also the question, *Is he going to become like his father?* That’s what the real conflict is.” Rinzler gloss: “While Lucas did succeed in creating perhaps the single most memorable cliffhanger in modern times, *Empire* is, as he says, an incomplete work. It would find its final form only when linked with Episode VI. *Star Wars*, on the other hand, was a complete story… the first film was innocent of success and expectations, and it had not been entirely possible to recapture that mystical quality.”

## CORPORATE DREAMING (pp. 348–349)

Weber: “*Empire* put the company on the map as a self-financing business entity controlling all its own rights… This put us in an equal position with any big studio, from a business standpoint, which was always George’s goal.” Howard Roffman, hired as legal counsel one month before release: “Once we knew that *Empire* was going to be successful, the company went from a state of some anxiety to something more relaxed… we were flush and we still had a lot of money from *Star Wars*.” Parenthetical: “Lucasfilm had made $101,801,838 out of that first $250 million in *Empire* rentals.” Lucas: “I certainly never got into this business wanting to be rich and famous… I just wanted to make movies… And my hobby is building.” The “one–two punch of *Star Wars–Empire*” enabled Skywalker Ranch Farm Group construction and allowed Ed Catmull to hire three Computer Division experts: Ralph Guggenheim, picture editing; Andy Moorer, sound editing; Alvy Ray Smith, “the graphics project, which would become the Pixar Image Computer.” Lucas: “I want to update film into the 1980s… Eventually, you’ll be able to take a machine the size of a Betamax with a little camera and make a movie.” Hirsch: computers “still adolescents.” Smith: “when I first came to ILM, there wasn’t even a computer in the building, except inside some of the machines.” Lucas: “I want to make a machine that will ensure my ability to make the kind of movies I want without any concern about their commercial potential… I learned it and I beat it, and I’m going to use it to make the system that I want to have happen.”

## DARING DIGITAL (pp. 348–350)

Lucas to Tom Smith: “what he really wanted was that ILM just be functioning three years later when he did the next *Star Wars*… ‘Keep it in business, don’t go bankrupt.’ ” *Dragonslayer* (1981): Muren and Tippett develop a motion-control rig for a stop-motion puppet; “This enhanced technique and the accompanying blur would be called go-motion.” Edlund: new VistaCruiser camera, 80-foot track versus the present 42-foot; fantasy of an electronic control system so equipment can “*talk* to each other.” Lucas: “It was struggling with Yoda that took me to the next level. I thought, *Gosh, I wish I could get that character to walk more than a few feet*. That was what really started me on the way to digital characters that could move freely on the set, without having to block everything around the puppeteer.” Brian Johnson leaves: “I’ve never been permanently employed by anybody… I would really like to work with as many different people as possible.” Richard Tong: George asked Charlie Weber to come up in December 1980 “to discuss the closing, perhaps, of the Southern California facility”; Weber “began to renegotiate a much more lucrative compensation for himself. At first I think George agreed, but then when Charlie left the meeting, George had some second thoughts… So Charlie was called back from the airport, actually, and then he was let go.” “In addition, Kurtz was not asked back to produce the last film of the trilogy.” Kurtz: “When we finally wrapped up *Empire* and shipped it off, George and I had a long talk about it and it was clear he was unhappy with what had happened. The cost, and then the other things. And I was weighing the option of joining Jim Henson to do *The Dark Crystal*… So it was mutual. George didn’t really want me to do the next film and I didn’t think I really wanted to do it.” Caption: McQuarrie “The Lucasfilm Family We’re Moving” announcement, September 1979, for the Egg Company move, “has often, mistakenly, been used to show the move north” (the northward move is 1981).

## APOSTLES OF CINEMA (p. 351)

Rinzler gloss: “While *Empire* was at one time part of an ephemeral 12-film plan, by the time it was released, the number had been reduced to 9—though it sometimes resurfaced as 12. Depending on who was being interviewed when, the details and time periods would subtly shift.” Lucas: “People were always asking, ‘Are you going to do *more* sequels?’ Sometimes I got carried away.” Hamill: “Now George is talking about three more films, a fourth trilogy that would have hardly anything to do with space, but would be these esoteric, philosophic, inner-directed films. Can you believe it? George has all this in his head, all figured out.” Lucas: “The prequel stories exist—where Darth Vader came from, the whole story about Darth and Ben Kenobi—and it all takes place before Luke is born. The other one—what happens to Luke afterward—is much more ethereal. I have a tiny notebook full of notes on that. If I’m really ambitious, I could proceed to figure out what would have happened to Luke. / There are six original stories that were written—really, seven; one was an odd film. But six original—two trilogies—and they’re complete and they were written really during the first one.
