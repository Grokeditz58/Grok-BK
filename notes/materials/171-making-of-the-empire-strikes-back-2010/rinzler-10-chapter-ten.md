## WONDERLAND EMPIRE (pp. 278–279)

Not long after the holidays, Williams and the London Symphony Orchestra tape the score at Anvil Studios, Denham, and EMI Studios, Abbey Road. Seventy-two of the musicians had played on the first film. Hamill: “The main titles will be the same… The main titles are *my* theme… Carrie pointed to the *Star Wars* album and said, ‘Look, Mark, here’s ‘Princess Leia’s Theme,’ and stuck out her tongue at me… I pouted for three days thinking, *Where’s* my *theme?*” Williams, who had recently replaced the late Arthur Fiedler as conductor of the Boston Pops, “had l08 minutes of original music to record” (source typography “l08”; Chapter Nine quoted Williams as “*Empire* will require 107 minutes of underscore”—record both). Since *Star Wars* he had worked with the LSO on *The Fury*, *Dracula* (1979), and *Superman* (two Grammys). Attending: Lucas, Kershner, Kurtz; recording engineer Eric Tomlinson, orchestrator Herbert Spencer, music editor Kenneth Wannberg, supervisor Lionel Newman (source typo “Willliams’s”). Williams: fifth score with the LSO in three years; 80 players, other days over 90, “for the most elaborate passages and the finale we had the full complement of 104.” Hamill: “Williams has written a terrific Darth Vader Theme and it’s so villainous… I can already hear the audience booing and hissing.” Williams: “The new material is a Darth Vader Theme or Imperial March, a dramatically scored piece of martial music that personifies not only Vader, but also the Imperial forces themselves. There is a theme for Yoda; it’s very lyrical and, hopefully, there’s a sense of wisdom and age in there. I tried to make the Love Theme a direct outgrowth of the original Princess Leia theme… And there are new themes that relate to the robots, Lando’s palace, and Cloud City.” Lucas: “Music is the most fun part of making a movie, especially with John, because the music turns out perfect… *Gee, I didn’t think he was going to be able to top the first one, but I think he did*.” Williams on “Battle in the Snow”: “an unusual orchestration calling for five piccolos, five oboes, a battery of eight percussion, two grand pianos, and two or three harps, in addition to the normal orchestral complement… a mechanical, brutal sound for the sequence showing Imperial walkers.” Eighteen sessions of three hours each over two weeks; cues generally rehearsed one or two times as sight-read, then a final recording in sync with the projected film. Williams: “How much do we hear when we see?” EE: Williams with the LSO and Newman in the control booth, circa January 1980 (1:31).

## THROUGH THE MATTING GLASS WINDOW (pp. 279–281)

January is midpoint for the matte department (started October; full-gear until March). Ellenshaw: “The day I walked into ILM, Neil Krepela was still testing equipment and not a single matte painting had been started.” McQuarrie: “I did some matte paintings that blended with the live action pretty good. But I was a pinch hitter.” Ellenshaw: McQuarrie, “previously a stranger to matte paintings,” works each matte through from beginning to end; Ellenshaw usually works several at once. “Without him, I don’t think we could have made the deadline.” McQuarrie: 10 or 11 hours a day and Saturdays; “I’m almost happier working on the matte paintings… this is the stuff that goes into the film.” Ultimately 70 paintings, every one generated by Ellenshaw, Pangrazio, or McQuarrie; many use a front-projection matte system (Scotchlite behind holes in the glass, live action projected through a beam-splitter). Kershner on the sunset *Falcon* landing on Cloud City: “I shot it at 64 feet on the stage. The buildings of course were paintings. That shot is one of the biggest in the whole film and it was all done inside a studio.” Ralston: the “gigantic” first-film *Falcon* model is resurrected; part of the platform built as a model; John Van Vliet animates retro rockets; combined in optical with a McQuarrie painting. One bog establishing shot is an Ellenshaw painting, with birds built by Tippett and animated by Ralston. Ralston: “George hadn’t planned on putting anything in those shots… they were going to go with something even *more* bizarre, a creature sitting in the water, but there was simply no time.” Edlund: “It’s all a painting except for a little foreground water with some fog. All we added was the smoke coming out of the X-wing and the birds.” Kershner: a shot he thought “probably the worst shot in the movie” becomes “the best” once married to its matte.

## ODDS AND ENDS (pp. 281–299, with EE matte galleries on pp. 282–297)

Doug Beswick, called January 2 for more armature work: the space slug, aluminum from a wooden mock-up, “pretty heavy. It worked like a hand puppet; a return spring mechanism would close the jaws.” Tippett designed an exterior; Berg puppeteered the first version; the vertically mounted “sock puppet” needed “more than 50 takes over a period of a week.” Howie Hammerman: “Ben came to me one day and said, ‘I need to record your burps’ ”; Hansen’s juice; “Ben was working on *Alien*. Later, those burps were used for the space slug.” Early January: Edlund shoots walker foot and snowspeeder with the HSE; his own Mitchell high-speed camera at 175 fps (VistaVision only 96 fps) for “Walker’s Ass,” or M-131; falling walker rigged with “hidden wires and solenoids.” Beswick: legs “underbuilt”; ratchets stripped once the body was on. “Originally, there were three scenes where the walker falls; the film used two of them.” January 15: Edlund shoots the walker’s head blowing up at the San Francisco Armory, Mission District; ceiling “a good 80 feet high”; five high-speed cameras; a couple of weeks, about 400 explosion shots. January 25, Muren, shot M-141: walker firing its cannon. Muren: “The walker sequence ends with Luke climbing up a rope and blowing up a walker… That’s not the way it used to end. Originally, that sequence came *toward* the end. But the shots of Luke running were done under a heavy overcast, so George rearranged that for the end, but it worked better dramatically, because I had come up with the idea of a progressive snowstorm… we were very rushed to shoot the high-speed walker footage and some of the backgrounds we used in those instances did not conform to the continuity.” Pickups in “George’s swimming pool,” “a big pit in the ground that was full of muddy water,” for the Dagobah monster swallowing Artoo; MacKenzie: LEDs in divers’ goggles. Johnson redesigns the Artoo spit-out rig because the Star Wars Stage could be flooded only three or four feet. Ralston on the wampa: “We saw the rough cut of the Abominable Snowman scene—and whatever they built in England was so crappy… What they had looked like a big owl, kind of nice and cuddly, but not scary… So Phil built a miniature head and we just went into a vacant lot and shot up at a cloudy sky. Luckily, George used it.” Keep distinct from the Chapter Nine EE early cut (original wampa “cut across the chest rather than losing an arm”) and from Lucas’s October note “Perhaps Luke cuts wampa arm off.”

Matte-gallery notes (do not collapse typographical variants): paints in acrylic or oil (accelerator for oil drying); “Pangrazio used oil paints; McQuarrie, acrylic and sometimes tempura [source typography]; Ellenshaw used primarily acrylics.” Caption typo “Pangrazzio.” Caption typo “cllimactic duel” on McQuarrie’s gantry matte. Cloud City window painting includes “a tower in the background from one of McQuarrie’s early concept sketches for Darth Vader’s castle.” McQuarrie to Ellenshaw: “I don’t want to know anything about how things work around here, I just want to paint.” Matte crews ultimately work 24 hours a day; completed shots off the printer every day or two in February–March 1980. Second-floor vibration: Krepela uses the PA to tell the building to be still. Burtt on Dagobah: birds in an echo-y aviary slowed to “spooky howls,” mixed with “raccoons in a bathtub.” Muren on Cloud City aerial plates: “the same problem they had on Superman,” light backgrounds and bluescreen, “multiplied the difficulties 10-fold”; heavy diffusion, blacks knocked out, image degraded. Ellenshaw: a *Falcon* takeoff composite comes back with “the lasers… upside down and coming out of the top of the building”; fixed “with just *days* to go.” Lucas typically arrives 45 minutes before afternoon dailies. Ellenshaw: “Typical of George was his very clever concept of showing the passage of time in Cloud City, by going from sunrise to daylight (Luke’s arrival) to sunset for the *Falcon*’s departure.” *Slave I* East Landing Platform: most of Fett’s ship is a photograph of the painted miniature; a “moving split” hides the live-action forklift carrying Han in carbonite. Space-slug tunnel storyboard December 3, 1979; *Falcon* escape “recalls Pinocchio’s from the belly of Monstro the whale.” Caption typo “Samuel Zolltheis.” Barron fibbed about being only 18 to get the job. Early 1980 hires: Robert Elswit, Michael Lawler.

## MYSTERY KID (pp. 299–301)

Ganis: Kenner and other licensees “doing *great* in 1979”; Lucas “said out loud that it was the reason he was able to create plans to build the ranch and to fund ILM.” Kenner had made about $100 million the previous year, but that success was based on the first film; “historically, sequels never lived up to the original movie; merchandise expectations were therefore pretty low.” Ganis’s Cincinnati shoe-department slide-projector pitch. Lucas: “I’ve said that I do a lot of *research* and people immediately think of marketing research… But it wasn’t market research; it was research into fairy tales, folklore, and organized religion. It was really my anthropological studies that I put together to make the film.” Ganis: “we were very shadowy when it came to discussions about George, creating a mystique. People didn’t know George. They knew of the success, but they didn’t know the kid from Northern California.”

## MIND-OVER-MATTER MAGIC (pp. 302–303)

January 22 optical-continuity list: Lucas “had officially changed the film’s opening shot from the tauntaun to one out in space featuring a Star Destroyer that launches several probe droids.” Muren: the tauntaun “was intended to be the ‘grabber shot,’ as dynamic as the opening shot in *Star Wars*. After some thought, though, George decided to start the film off in space… to remind you of the first film and to establish the source of those brilliant probes.” Edlund: “The opening shot in this picture isn’t as dynamic as the one in the original—but then it didn’t need to be.” Lucas: “I was starting a style… ‘Each one is going to start in space. Each one is going to start with a Star Destroyer or some kind of a ship,’ because the titles go into the stars, we pan down to wherever, and then we start the movie.” The tauntaun plate remains “most challenging”: helicopter Wesscam into a huge ice field, ending straight down. Internal note on OP-9: move plotted by Comstock on an Oxberry; Muren shot the taun bluescreen, full-frame VistaVision, alternate frames, then a shadow card; Nicholson separated taun and shadow; frames cut up and placed on a registration block; “Plotting the move took three weeks. Shooting time was 10 to 15 hours straight. Never been done. Conceived by Dennis.” Tippett performs the taun running in place, suspended on a rod. Muren: “it breaks every rule of special effects… We broke it up into components—and that idea of breaking up the shots, so the mind can tackle one problem at a time, is a methodology we’ve always used at ILM.” Newly designed wampa filmed against a white backdrop, then a cloudy sky. Caption: on the way to Dagobah, R2 “was actually repainted black instead of blue to avoid becoming transparent… ‘the only way to make the shot work,’ says Ralston.” January 11, 1980: letter from the son of Ub Iwerks to Edlund thanking him for a tour. Kasdan: “It’s wonderful being able to put words in the mouths of characters that have the same kind of place for young children that Mickey Mouse had for me.”

## BLAZING ON (pp. 303–304)

February 6, 1980, editing-room notes: Lucas improving dozens of effects shots—“Fix matte lines, lose diffusion, correct color, make cable thinner, soften lasers, remove shadow at bottom of ship, crater should be darker and browner …” Edlund: a “could-be-better” list of shots they could “probably get by with if we had to.” Johnson: six-day weeks, long 12-hour days since the end of August; optical working 24 hours a day; “Some of the more than 400 effects have required 100 pieces of film going through the optical printer!” Model-shop inventory: 108 miniatures, including seven Cloud City buildings, a small R2-D2, Lando and Luke puppets; Edlund “counted at least 50 major pieces”; “The medical frigate was added late in the picture.” Huston: “a big additional sequence added” in the last two or three months before release. Muren: six, seven days a week “for months at the end”; “the final four or five months of that show was just a really incredible push.” Johnson: sometimes “we’ve hung them on wires” instead of motion control. Accidents: the Dykstraflex crashed through a bluescreen; Jerry Jeffress dropped the high-speed Empire camera while a French documentary crew was filming. Tippett: “We’re just burned out… My vacation at the end.” Bloom: “about 100 people… 24 hours a day… we were beginning to turn ILM into a working special effects house.”

## THE DARK LORD’S TRICKS (pp. 304–308)

Pre-mix of dialogue and sound effects had begun in LA the previous month; Ladevich: “full-bore mixing by the beginning of February… until probably the end of April.” February 21, 1980: Kershner interviewed at Samuel Goldwyn Studios, Looping Room D, supervising audio layers and ADR. Regulars at the mix: Burtt, Lucas, assistant sound editor John Benson, Ladevich. One night they listen to reel six at the Academy theater without picture. Burtt: more pre-mixing than on the first film, but “we didn’t have our own mix room, so I had to go to Los Angeles and stay there for three months.” Kershner on Ford in the meteor cockpit: Harrison “was talking very fast”; in Hollywood ADR “he asked me to go check the projector. He said, ‘I can’t talk that fast, the projector’s off.’… So we kept running the ADR over and over, and finally he got into it and of course he *could* talk that fast.” Burtt on contrast: “busy, noisy snow battles to very quiet jungle… Those hills and valleys, I think, made it a balanced film.”

James Earl Jones, “the voice of Darth Vader,” recorded “in late 1979/early 1980”: “When I first saw the dialogue that said, ‘Luke, I am your father,’ I said to myself, ‘He’s lying, I wonder how they’re gonna play that lie out.’ ” Keep this first-reading reaction distinct from the April 1, 1978, handwritten reveal, from the typed Ladd copy that omitted it, from the on-set dummy line given to Prowse, and from later commentary that treats the line as known truth. February 19, 1980: Lucas directs Jim Bloom in pickups of Luke hanging in the wampa cave (boots in ice, hand extending for the saber). January 18, 1980: five or six partial sets at Elstree; Watts: “Kersh and Mark Hamill came over and it was bang, bang, bang.” Lucas also directs effects cameraman Jim Veilleux as an Imperial officer killed when an asteroid smashes his vessel; death via hologram added to Elstree footage and composited into Vader-and-extras material.

February 5: Jim Bloom letter to Marjorie Eaton, Palo Alto, enclosing “five lines of dialogue you will need to know for lip synching,” photography intended February 18–22 (originally scheduled for November). “Eaton was filmed at ILM as the Emperor, though her test didn’t prove satisfactory; she was replaced by Elaine Baker in makeup, with the Emperor’s voice later supplied by actor Clive Revill.” Hamill: “It’s interesting because the Emperor was an actress, dubbed with a male voice, and monkey eyes superimposed… There is certainly something strange about him. This has got to be due to the fact that he looks and sounds a bit like Obi-Wan.” Ralston: “To make the hologram of the Emperor, we shot an actor in makeup with the eyes blacked out… We wound up shooting the eyes of a chimpanzee, then matchmoving the eyes of the actor and rephotographing it from a TV screen. Actually, that was true of all the hologram shots.” Caption: candidates for the Emperor’s eyes included “a cat, a chimpanzee, and those of assistant accountant Laura Crockett (the chimp won out)”; appliance/mask “crafted by Rick Baker,” worn by Elaine Baker; split-beam with a mirror combined chimp eyes and Baker in camera. Lucas chose among voice-over auditions. Jason Wingreen dubbed Boba Fett. Frank Oz returns as Yoda; contract finalized November 1979. Lucas: “I didn’t want Yoda to sound like Miss Piggy… I’d wanted to use a different actor. But I’ve discovered over the years that, in terms of puppetry, the person who is actually acting the role is really into it… It was really a matter of me not being able to find another actor who could have performed it as well as Frank could—the same thing had happened with Tony Daniels.” March 9: additional dialogue changes in reels one through three, from “First transport is clear …” to “The first transport is away!”; from Luke’s “Artoo, are you okay?” after the swamp monster spit-out, to “It’s lucky you don’t taste very good.” Caption: September 1979 notes revise the Vader–Fett exchange in the carbon-freezing chamber; November 1, 1979, “Revised Secret Pages” “clarified the words for reel 11’s climactic reversal scene. A note on the second page modified Luke’s rescue by the *Falcon* (ultimately, he would collapse, falling off the antenna into Lando’s arms).” November 2, 1979, cue sheet lists the Emperor’s lines (original ADR date, then postponed). January 25, 1980, page of Yoda lines for Oz. Droid motor sounds get a six-week tune-up by Terry Eckton; they “had been rushed in the first film.”

## ALL FOR ONE (pp. 308–314)

March 12, 1980, *Daily Variety*: deal for Fox to distribute Lucasfilm’s *Star Wars III*, terms “significantly improved” over the *Empire* deal; the article “couldn’t mention… the harrowing financial straits that had led to the agreement.” Color timing at Deluxe. “Pops” in walker stop-motion concealed by animated laser blasts. Muren: bluescreen for three shots at the end of the walker sequence. Luke’s snowspeeder crash, shot M-111, combines a “luge rig” camera plunge with stop-motion. Late in post, Lucas wants a scene just before the Rebel fleet leaves Hoth; ILM staff audition. Johnston gets the speaking part: “Groups 7 and 10 will stay behind to fly the speeders. As soon as each transport is loaded, evacuation control will give clearance for immediate launch.” Ten takes, never blew the line. Edlund: “Ralph did that particular painting, so it was kind of neat to have the guy who actually did the painting moving around in front of his own artwork.” Also in the shot: Pangrazio, model builders, effects editor Michael Kelly. Probe-droid pickup: “anyone who wasn’t doing something in the building was called in,” including Blau on a Variac; baking soda in a fan; Pangrazio on smoke. Chicken walker added “practically out of time”: Johnston glues prototype parts to Berg’s new armature; St. Amand works the joints; “basically the display model that ended up in the film.” Muren: “just in two background out-of-focus shots, marching along. It’s a throwaway thing.” Ralston: “another ‘element of the strange.’” Lightsaber technique changed because highly reflective tape from principal photography “tended to disappear in a blur” on mighty swings (Edlund). Starkey sent to Los Angeles to oversee out-of-house laser, swordfighting, dissolves, wipes, and titles.

Lucas rewrites the roll-up “to accommodate the film’s new beginning, locking it for final release”: “It is a dark time for the Rebellion. Although the Death Star has been destroyed, Imperial troops have driven the Rebel forces from their hidden base and pursued them across the galaxy. / Evading the dreaded Imperial Starfleet, a group of freedom fighters led by Luke Skywalker have established a new secret base on the remote ice world of Hoth. / The evil lord Darth Vader, obsessed with finding young Skywalker, has dispatched thousands of remote probes into the far reaches of space …” Caption on the typed final version: “contains one last edit from Lucas, who changed ‘have’ to ‘has.’ ” Record both: the body-text crawl as printed uses “have established”; the caption states the locked typography is “has.” Abandoned poster tag line: “The war isn’t over.” 1979 teaser one-sheet by Tony Seiniger and David Reneric; Vader helmet photographed by Bob Peak, Jr. Unused Tom Jung Vader poster concepts.

## TERROR AT THE SCREENING (pp. 314–318)

Hamill, early 1980: “I don’t think *Empire* can become a phenomenon. You can’t take people by surprise like we did the first time… But I think it’s going to be a smashing success.” March 13, Lucas: “I think it’s a better film than the first one… I think it stands just as much chance of being a hit as not being one. I guess I’m the biggest pessimist around here—after all, I said the very same thing about the first one.” Ladevich: “a very apprehensive atmosphere… George had, obviously, a tremendous amount riding on this financially, emotionally—it was going to define the rest of his career.” Arnold to Hirsch: “you want this man to succeed.” Johnson: “There’s no doubt in my mind that *Empire* will be every bit as good as *Star Wars* and probably even better.” Ellenshaw: “I’m scared… afraid many of the matte shots will *look* like matte shots.” *Starlog* asks Prowse for plot specifics; he laughs: “You must be joking. I haven’t got a clue.” Billy Dee Williams, on the *Nighthawks* (1981) set: “I don’t even know what happens in the movie!” Fox executives get their sneak on March 22; Starkey, cutting in very late visual effects and a new mix Burtt drops “within hours of the screening,” sits “in this numb-eared state of hearing sync,” terrified of a break in the middle of the studio screening; “It all came off.” Two days later Lucas is back at ILM with more improvement notes. February 9, 1980, pickup of the Rebel hangar scene Lucas found unsatisfactory as shot in England. Johnston redesigned his 1977 two-legged “scout walker” January 28, 1979; revised AT-ST storyboard February 23, 1980. Ellenshaw convinces Lucas that the reactor-shaft matte (into which Luke lets himself fall) “would be more foreboding if dark,” though earlier shots of the core were lighter with a pulsating glow. November 6, 1979, storyboard for Luke’s POV as his lightsaber falls away under Cloud City.

## GOOD SUSPENSE (pp. 320–323)

*Variety*, April 1: Lucas will act as consultant on John Korty’s *Twice Upon a Time* (1983) for The Ladd Company. Kasdan on *Body Heat*: “George never came to visit… He did come and look at the movie when I’d finished cutting it… making a few suggestions, which were very supportive and very smart, ’cause George is a great editor… And then he came to the sneak preview.” Lucas finalizes shots as late as April 11. “The last reel was delivered on April 16, which triggered a $10 million payment from Fox to the Bank of Boston.” April 17: Lucasfilm Employee Bonus Plan, qualified staff to participate in *Empire* profits. Same day, *The Hollywood Reporter* prematurely calls the film “completed” because it is being shown in seven anti-blind-bidding states (Ohio, Virginia, Georgia, Alabama, South Carolina, Louisiana, Tennessee). Fox president Norman Levy: minimum play time “of up to 28 weeks and more” (usual upper limit for a “big” film was 12 weeks).

April 19: Lucas previews the film, as he had *Star Wars*, at the Northpoint Theatre in San Francisco. Interoffice memo of Preview Questionnaire comments, typography as printed (including “[sic]”): Males under 10: “Thought it was great … It was like a dream … Movie too long. Luke sometimes gross (hand). Tan Tan [sic] animation could be better… It was kind of sad when Han Solo turned into ice. Why are you making me phil [sic] this out?” Females under 10: “You should have less violence, or if you have violence, make it funny violence… I did not like it as much as *Star Wars*… I didn’t like the ending. Overall good suspense.” Males 16 to 20: “Mattes of Bespin weren’t realistic looking… It’s one of the few sequels as good or better than the original.” Females 16 to 20: “I hope you find Han Solo!” Males 21 to 25: “I was surprised the story was good… the army stuff, not for me.” Males 56 and over: “You will have the kids of America standing on their heads practicing psychokinesis. The Biblical parallels of the story are remarkable…” Muren on tauntaun criticisms: “I thought, *My God, what did we do wrong?*… we had solved 10 percent of the problem, not 100 percent of the problem.” Kershner: “George was worried about Solo’s line, ‘I know.’ So we sneak-previewed the film and when the line came, the audience roared. George turns to me and says, ‘You see, it’s a mistake.’ Now the picture is over and they’re all talking about that line and how great it was. They all noticed it, so we kept it in the film. George knows what he wants, but he is very flexible and that’s why I like him so much.” Keep this April 19 preview-and-keep decision distinct from the February 20, 1979, script lines, the June 21, 1979, “I know” rewrite, and Ford’s later memory that “the original line was, ‘I love you, too.’ ”

April 21: “Action Negative” delivered to Deluxe. April 22 Kurtz-to-Robert Greber memo: Dolby stereo optical soundtrack negative on target for April 29; mono optical still three weeks; 35mm answer print the following Monday or Tuesday; Deluxe around the clock on 70mm color timing for the May 21 release; MPAA PG certificate forwarded to Fox. Kurtz: 70mm magnetic soundtracks had to be check-run in real time; “the reject rate on the reels was something like 22 percent.” Shot-status board: someone writes the Han Solo quote, “Never tell me the odds!” Medical frigate design by Rodis-Jamero (“Joe, I’ve got it!”); Lucas inspects the model with Edlund, Johnson, and Gawley. March 25, 1980, Rodis-Jamero drawings of the last shot; Muren’s nebula from flour, over-exposures, and a mirror (one day setup, half a day to shoot), drawing on his 1972 film *The Solar System: Islands in Space*. McQuarrie “Luke and Leia (Star Cruiser)” production painting, October 3, 1978.

## EXCITED PERCEPTIONS (pp. 323–327)

By May the financials are clear: to make a profit *Empire* would have to earn more than $57,158,514, the sum of its “Before Release Producer fee,” including the final cost of the film itself—$30,478,433, about $8 million of which was ILM—plus the distributor fee of $20,456,661 and more than $6 million in interest. “The guarantees didn’t come close to this figure.” Ganis: “the guiding light for the campaign was George… ‘I want to do a poster of Han Solo and Princess Leia like the *Gone with the Wind* poster.’ ” First advertising would feature Han and Leia, followed by posters featuring Darth Vader—“a ‘conscious’ strategy devised by Lucas.” Lucas: “I’ve always had a very strong interest in the way my films are promoted and the way they’re released.” Kenner: $10 million advertising push. Internal name “E-Day.” *Star Wars* Hotline 800–521–1980 (the number is the release date); recorded messages from cast members. Principals hustled LA–New York–Washington–London–Japan–Australia. Ford at the Plaza, exhausted: press conference at nine, then “about 150 interviews in four hours,” fifteen television interviews, “Then we went back to our rooms and passed out.” Next day, 27 television interviews; that morning, the *Today* show and four or five print interviews. Hamill: “The last time we were on tour together, Harrison was the publicity sheriff… He would give us report cards: ‘Humility—*B*. I like what you said about not being in the business for money—*A* for that.’ ” Selfridges, Oxford Street: signing with Fisher, Hamill, Ford, Williams, Mayhew, Prowse, and Daniels. Luke’s rescue “finalized late in post, on April 23, 1980, and shot using one of the miniature Lukes”; Muren holds two pilot Lukes, “the smaller of which is a Kenner toy.” First theatrical one-sheet inspired by the 1967 *Gone with the Wind* re-release poster (designed by Tom Jung, painted by Howard Terpning); six artists re-imagine Han and Leia as Rhett and Scarlett; Roger Kastel’s painting is chosen. “To keep him a secret, Yoda was not featured on any early posters in the United States.”
