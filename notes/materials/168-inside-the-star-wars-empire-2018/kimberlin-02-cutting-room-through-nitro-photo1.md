## The Cutting Room Floor

“We were nominated for an Academy Award for visual effects nearly every year and we won our share.”[fn 2] “One of our supervisors, Dennis Muren, has won nine Oscars alone for visual effects. Dennis holds more Oscars than anyone alive.” Bake-off reels cut from theatrical prints (optical-sound sync problem). “My favorite to show people was the one from *The Empire Strikes Back*. That one always impressed people.” *Pearl Harbor* (2000) reel also “breathtaking” with “the lousy acting and the poor storyline on the cutting room floor.”

Phil the barber (“connector”) leads to Desmond Clark, then Nick Toth and Kathy Schick (Stone Age Institute; Gordon Getty funding). Lecture at Indiana University uses a *Saving Private Ryan* Omaha Beach crane shot: “What we were given to work with was a huge crane shot that slowly revealed a beach with absolutely nothing in the frame other than the ocean and the beach itself. We added everything else.” History professors compare it to *Triumph of the Will*; Kimberlin: “It’s a movie, fellows… This is entertainment; we make stuff up for a living.” After *Jurassic Park*, Dennis Muren: “I have studied them and studied them, and I cannot find a flaw.” Audience exit interviews: “I liked it when the dinosaur ate that guy.” Kimberlin floated a fake UFO home-movie plant; “I never could get anyone to go along with me.”

## Film School

San Francisco State, 1968, vs “Hollywood film schools.” Contemporaries: “Lucas, Coppola, Spielberg, Scorsese, etc., all went to film school in either New York or Los Angeles.” Teachers include Lester Cole (Hollywood Ten). Strike / Jack Johnson documentary. A classmate’s draft-protest film won the National Student Film Award; “George Lucas had also won the same award but for a very different kind of film. His was called *THX 1138* and was about a time in the future where technology was used to dehumanize people. This was the difference between my film school and Hollywood’s. Our films were about hard-core reality and theirs were fables. Fables are successful.” “George expanded his student film into a feature motion picture released by a major studio.” DiMaggio/Monroe house anecdote; DiMaggio in the studio: “*I hate coffee.*”

## “Francis Coppola Wishes to Speak with You”

Early screenplays “are not always blueprints for great films—they became great films while they were being made.” “*Annie Hall*” example. Then, typography locked: “The original screenplay versions of *Star Wars* were so long and shaggy that they had to be made into three films.” Flag as Kimberlin; do not collapse with Lucas’s later “always six / always nine” statements, or with Kaminski’s account of how the saga grew after 1977–78.

1970: job at Bill Palmer’s (Ampex / captured German tape recorder). Phone call: “Francis Coppola saw your film and wants to talk to you.” “Coppola and several of his filmmaking buddies, including George Lucas, had founded a movie production company in San Francisco called American Zoetrope.” Friday student screenings; Kimberlin leaves a silent work print of the unfinished Jack Johnson film. “Soon I was meeting with Francis and getting a tour of his studio. George was somewhere there finishing his film. This was before *The Godfather* and before the release of George’s first feature, *THX 1138,* which was also to be the first release of a multiple-studio film deal that had financed the Zoetrope studio.”

Coppola offers a job and help; Kimberlin turns down the job (won’t walk out on Palmer’s) but edits nights at Zoetrope. Martin Ritt too far along on *The Great White Hope* to attach the short. Zoetrope impressive but “no tape at all, no splicers… no supplies.” “Within months, *THX 1138* was released and it dropped dead. The studio hated the film and all the other projects Coppola had lined up. They cut off all money and even made Francis repay what had been spent. Zoetrope vanished from the landscape (at least until *The Godfather*)… Now I knew why there were no supplies: They were broke and counting on the release of *THX* to save them.” Coppola later sneaks out of the official festival to play tuba at Kimberlin’s alternative screening. *Jeffries-Johnson 1910* gets McGraw-Hill distribution.

## Mike Medavoy; *Chan Is Missing*

1974 Willie Sutton treatment; Kimberlin bluffs onto a studio lot to see Medavoy, is later chewed out; Spielberg “had bragged about doing much the same thing… except he had even moved into an empty studio office.” Almost an AFI internship with Richard Fleischer on *The New Centurions*; money question blows it. A *Show* magazine photo of Kimberlin with Coppola under a blow-up “that would later wind up in my office at ILM.” Don Sanders hired then fired on the *Dallas* pilot. “In 1973… I decided that if I couldn’t get a start in Hollywood movies, I’d make my own damn movie.”

*Chan Is Missing* (~$20,000; Wayne Wang; Rick Schmidt editing): San Francisco festival doesn’t even open the cans; MoMA New Directors and Ebert/Canby launch Wang. Kimberlin: the “art film” game is “somewhat rigged.”

## *American Nitro*

Long production-and-distribution memoir of Kimberlin’s 16mm drag-racing feature (cinema vérité; Tom “The Mongoose” McEwen; Cannon / Golan and Globus; ~$1.2 million ticket sales; rights recovered). He calls his small-town high-school years “my *American Graffiti* years.” Theater chains “refused to replace films like *Star Wars* even after a year because they had reached the 50 percent split.”

Lucas lunch on *Jedi*, typography locked: “Years later, on *Jedi,* I was sitting next to George Lucas during an ILM lunch break and was able to engage him in a movie conversation. I mentioned that I thought that Arthur Penn’s *Bonnie and Clyde* was a great film. George said, ‘Arthur called my office the other day, looking for work. His problem is that he is an “A” director and an “A” director commands an “A” salary, and there aren’t that many “A” projects around these days.’ He also suggested that Penn was more of a ‘theatrical’ director than a filmmaker, implying that that was a little passé… It also reminds me now of something George said years later, when I was nearing the end of my Lucasfilm/ILM career. He was quoted as saying, ‘I am at a point financially where no one can tell me I am too old or too un-cool to make movies.’” (Quote-within-quote typography locked from the XHTML; the second Lucas line is “quoted as saying,” not a conversation Kimberlin heard.)

By 1987 ILM clients “from Steven Spielberg on suddenly changed their movie release standard from wide-screen Panavision back to the historic almost square, television-screen-friendly format” because home video was now the major profit generator. Close: “Right now my job was still at Lucasland, but I didn’t want to see myself totally swallowed up in it any more.”
