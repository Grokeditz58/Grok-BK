## The Screamer; The Players

ILM business model, typography locked: “George had moved his company away from Hollywood, but we still had to work with them. The idea was to work on other directors’ movies in between movies that George wanted to make. In this way a large staff of talented people could be kept together until another Lucas production came along. Otherwise, all the talent would have to be laid off and it would disperse back to Los Angeles… I used to say that working for Lucasfilm was like a Boy Scout camp compared to Hollywood or New York filmmaking.”

*Die Hard 2* (1990) and Joel Silver (“a screamer”). Reel shown is forest-fire footage shot for Spielberg’s *Always* (quarter-scale trees in a Point Richmond plant). Silver: “I will look at your reel, but just to be clear, I don’t fuck my friends.” He then awards ILM the work. Budget tirade over ~$300,000 more; ILM producer **Chrissie England** “had started at Lucasfilm in 1977 as a receptionist, and by 1978 was George Lucas’s secretary… it was rumored that George had granted her a job for life.” Flag the rumor as rumor. *Die Hard 2* budgeted $42 million, ballooned to $67 million, grossed $240 million.

*The Golden Child* (1986; “staring Eddie Murphy” typo locked) and *Star Trek III* overlapping under Ken Ralston; Michael Ritchie furious. Eddie Murphy ill at ease at “lily-white” ILM. *Cocoon*: Ron Howard “first worked with Lucas” on *American Graffiti*. “Apparently, George’s direction of actors was limited to instructions like ‘faster,’ ‘slower,’ or ‘that was great.’ Ron went up to him and said, ‘Just tell us what you want. We’re actors, we can do it.’” Howard bursting into editorial: “Where’s George?”

## What Do You Do?; The Oracle

Editors joke that coming to Lucasfilm is being “rescued.” “Veterans guided the new troops and recounted their past victories under George, the supreme commander and benevolent dictator. Success was our narcotic; box office and Oscars our goal.” Flag the “benevolent dictator” phrase as Kimberlin.

Oracle, 1982: “the Lucasfilm database system… Unix-based… from the Los Angeles offices such as the Egg Company (George naming things again) to all the divisions in Marin County.” “You could ask it for all of the X-wing fighter aircraft shots with pilots looking left, for instance, and it would immediately produce a list.” Company e-mail and “allmessages” “a decade” before the general public’s Internet. Computer-controlled motion-control camera systems for spaceship passes. 1984: a Macintosh in optical; computer division’s first entirely CG shot for *Young Sherlock* (stained-glass knight). Kimberlin projects Lasseter’s *Luxo Jr.* in Building D: “a Luxo lamp that had feelings.”

## The Smell of Napalm

Building D dailies workhorse; Building C the elegant former mix theater. Kimberlin wanted to nickname them “Jonestown” and “Graceland” (never approved). Rooms “hot miked.” After-hours classics: *Easy Rider* with László Kovács; Lucas attends an *American Graffiti* screening. Kimberlin asks “if he had any nostalgia for the great low-light cinematography” (Techniscope). “This screening was at a time when George was pushing the Hollywood film world to go digital. His answer was that he wasn’t primarily known for his cinematography and he was fine with the look he could get with digital. It was a disappointing answer for me… George had famously turned down Vilmos Zsigmond as the cinematographer of one of his *Star Wars* movies because ‘the guy wants a million bucks.’”

John Milius on *Red Dawn* (1984): wrote *Apocalypse Now* including “I love the smell of napalm in the morning”; “having inspired the character John Milner in *American Graffiti*.” Spielberg called him “the greatest hood-of-a-car screenwriter I ever met.” Zoetrope blow-ups now at ILM (*Flash Gordon, Citizen Kane, King Kong,* Kurosawa, Eisenstein). Visitors include Michael Jackson, Stallone, Cruise, Susan Sarandon, “Akira Kurosawa (dignified).”

## *Roger Rabbit*; *Back to the Future*

By *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* (1988) Kimberlin is head of ILM editorial. Disney and Spielberg’s Amblin pay for tests RR1/RR2; Ken Ralston designs them to make animation as hard as possible. “I then synced up the soundtrack, and sent it off to Spielberg and Zemeckis. Spielberg called the next day. Everything worked, it was beautiful and funny as hell, so please reserve the next two years of your lives for us. That was more or less the message.” Pipeline book ~30 pages; ~185,000 frames of hand-drawn animation; cut locked before animation. Previews: “When we previewed *The Golden Child* in Sacramento… I’d much rather hear what Marcia Lucas had to say.” Disney leaves Baby Herman’s salty lines intact. Worldwide gross “about $330 million… beginning a turnaround for Disney that continues to this day.”

*Back to the Future III* (1990) train/DeLorean gag shot at a Marin quarry. “We used to find huge stone bowls there that the local Indian tribe, the Miwoks, used for grinding corn eons ago. George named the Ewoks in *Star Wars* after these early Marin County Miwoks, so it seemed fitting that this place be used to shoot a movie.” Flag as Kimberlin’s etymology (not a Lucas quote in this book).

## Pixar

Building C “George and Marcia had designed… from the ground up”: Art Deco mix theater, editing rooms with French doors (Marcia “primarily an editor”), three-story blue-screen stage, computer division on the top floor. Steve Jobs’s Porsche, license plate NEXT: “contrary to what others have written, Jobs’s car did indeed have a license plate.” “Propeller heads”: Lasseter, Ed Catmull, David DiFrancesco, Ralph Guggenheim, Don Conway. Laser film scanner pioneered with Eastman Kodak; “Pixar” as image software.

Lasseter hired 1983 after being fired from Disney. Feature-animation pitch: “George listened carefully, said good-bye, and drove back to the Ranch. The next day the president of Lucasfilm came down to the computer division and said, ‘Don’t ever mention this idea to George again.’” “Money was the reason for the decision to sell what would become Pixar to Jobs—that and the fact that George had stated many times, ‘There is only one filmmaker at this company, and that is George Lucas.’ He didn’t want things going out, essentially under his name, that could bomb and tarnish Lucasfilm’s reputation… But the money was the biggest factor.”

Divorce, typography locked: “After the successful release of *Jedi,* George and Marcia announced that they were divorcing… Marcia was a full partner in building the film empire that was now to be split. The sad thing was that her contributions were no longer going to be felt. It is not easy to find someone who is not only capable of pointing out flaws in dramatic film structure, but also able to tell you how to fix them.” Spielberg friends’ screening of *Raiders*: “At the end of the screening, Marcia said, ‘There is no emotional resolution to the story. You need a scene where we find out what happens to Indy and the girl.’ She pinpointed the problem and offered a solution. Priceless advice. Steven shot an additional scene, released the movie, and the rest is history.” Inventory of the whole company; “they split it right down the middle and George bought back the half of the company he didn’t already own for $50 million.” Later Disney sale “$4.05 billion, not including the Ranch.” Jobs bought what became Pixar “for $10 million, $5 million in cash and $5 million in other guarantees, with Lucas retaining the use of certain software.” Kimberlin also notes later-revealed no-poach collusion “including Apple and Lucasfilm.” “In this new world, George Lucas would have never been hired by his own company, and neither would I.”

After Pixar’s success, typography locked: “I heard George comment on his having sold it. ‘The only thing I regret,’ he said, ‘is having let this successful model out into the world where others could copy it.’” Spielberg in *Wired*: “I always thought that if ILM had run the space agency, we’d have colonized Mars by now.” The “free speech” board outside editorial “was often George’s first stop after leaving dailies each morning.” One controversial posting: “an interview where George had stated that ‘what you are by age thirty is what you will be in life.’”

## A Jolt of Money; *The Mask*; Off the Ground; Defining Myself

Santa Barbara: “George recently purchased a $19 million teardown here just to get the beachfront.” Marcia Lucas: “When you get a big jolt of money, it’s very easy to be in awe of it and lose touch with reality.”

*The Mask* (1994): Jim Carrey still unknown; Cameron Diaz. Steve “Spaz” Williams “would do the original animation test of a dinosaur running through a field that convinced Spielberg to do all of *Jurassic Park* in computer-generated images.” The Pit under C theater. Main-house restaurant “usually where George ate”; second floor “off-limits… George has his office and writing room.” ~1990, after a Timothy Leary talk Scott Ross arranged without authorization, Spaz and Mark Dippé wander upstairs, “took up residence” in George’s office (lore: cigars, boots on the desk). Infrared alarm; they give fake names. “When George learned of this, he initially wanted the office offenders fired… ‘Why are people like this working for my company?’ he reportedly asked. When it was explained to him that there were certain ‘key’ employees that ILM could not do without, he relented. But they were famously ‘banned’ from the Ranch.” A later spin-off company takes the name Banned From The Ranch. “Making a movie is a lot like fighting a battle, so if your generals are fighting each other instead of the enemy, it is usually best to fire them both, and that is what George did” (Scott Ross and Doug Norby). “Spaz and Mark Dippé survived, but they could never go back to the Ranch or work on any *Star Wars* films, *ever*.” (XHTML italicization of “*Star War*s” locked.) Chairs in Kimberlin’s office “that Marcia Lucas had commissioned.”

Paul McCartney “Off the Ground” (1993) video shot at the Ranch. Autographs, typography locked: “George would sign things for children, but when his executives brought a bunch of mementos into to a meeting one time, he set them straight: ‘This is a business, not a fan club.’ On the other hand, I saw him sit down after we finished *The Phantom Menace* and sign things for his employees for hours to thank them for all their hard work.”

Defining Myself: overheard Hollywood hierarchy; “Spielberg and those guys are on top.” “George used to say, ‘There are a lot of pencils and typewriters in the world but not a lot of novels. Why? Because it takes guts to write a book.’” “The biggest guy in visual effects at that time was probably Dennis Muren, a multiple Oscar winner, and even he was destined to be a mere footnote in movie history, if that.” Rest of the chapter is family history (father’s suicide; “The Seed King”; Mason’s Brewery).
