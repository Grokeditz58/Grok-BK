## The Gilded Cage

May 1982, Marin County, typography locked: “I am in George Lucas’s editing room with a small group of production people sitting around a KEM film editing machine… George is explaining to us what he wants to happen in the space battle sequence of *Revenge of the Jedi,* the third in the original Star Wars series. I have been hired as the visual effects editor for this space battle.”

Spielberg phones mid-meeting. “George starts describing how he wants the next Indiana Jones movie to open. ‘It should be a big Busby Berkeley–type dance number,’ he says and then proceeds to describe in detail what is to become the opening to the sequel of the wildly successful *Raiders of the Lost Ark*.” (XHTML italicizes *Raiders…*; extract sometimes collides the closing italic with the preceding word.) Kimberlin: “How can he handle all of this at the same time?” ILM then “about 120 people.” Alumni named: David Fincher, Joe Johnston (*Honey, I Shrunk the Kids*), Steve Starkey, Colin Wilson, John Knoll.

Company ethos: “to hand people buckets of responsibility and trust them to pull it off.” “To paraphrase a famous line from *Star Wars*: ‘Do or do not.’ At ILM, there was no try.”

*American Graffiti*: “Made for just $750,000… it went on to earn $50 million. This picture alone made George and his wife, Marcia Griffin, independently wealthy” because the film “cost so little, and made such huge profits, that there was no place left to hide them.” *Star Wars* as “an ode to the old Flash Gordon movie serials”; “he had tried to secure the rights to Flash Gordon at first, but finding them unobtainable, he wrote and directed his own version.” These two films “changed the Hollywood business model forever. They proved that it was possible to sell just the sizzle.”

Toy rights: “He had shrewdly bargained with the 20th Century Fox studio to retain the toy rights from the movie for himself, a tradeoff in exchange for a lower salary. Toy rights had been traditionally worthless, and the studio gladly gave most of them up.” After that success he “wanted to own his own movies,” following Chaplin and Lloyd (not Keaton); he would “finance his own films from now on… The studio would receive a mere distribution fee.” This “risked everything they had worked for.”

By Kimberlin’s arrival Lucas had made “*The Empire Strikes Back*” — Kimberlin parenthetically “arguably the best of the entire series” (flag as Kimberlin) — and, “with director Stephen Spielberg” (typo locked), *Raiders*. “Spielberg and Lucas offered a record-breakingly bad deal to the studios vying for the rights to distribute the new *Raiders,* and the studios eagerly accepted.”

ILM name, typography locked (Kimberlin’s etymology, not a Lucas quote): “I always thought the name Industrial Light and Magic came from the real estate itself. When searching for a location where the messy end of filmmaking could take place (we blew things up), George must have encountered the term ‘light industrial’… All he had to do was add the ‘magic.’”

Skywalker Ranch: “a 5,000-acre movie ranch, at an eventual cost of at least $100 million”; 25-minute drive from ILM; $3.5 million rec building; technical building with a hundred-piece orchestra stage; George’s office “a 50,000-square-foot building that looked like a Victorian hotel” with “a magnificent two-story library crowned with a stained glass dome. The artist who created all this stained glass would eventually break up George’s marriage and run off with his wife, Marcia.” Record as Kimberlin’s report; do not collapse with Jones 166’s account of the divorce.

ILM “quarantined” from the Ranch. “For many of those who worked there, it came to represent a gilded cage… Those of us at ILM were the barnacled hull, and the Ranch was the part that stuck out of the water. Beautiful Lake Ewok, we renamed ‘Butthead Lake’ among ourselves. Some of us even started calling the whole operation ‘Lucasland.’ But not yet. That was to come.” Closes with Fitzgerald on the back lot as “thirty acres of fairyland.”

## Mrs. Hickman

VFX editorial “layer up shots, like making Dagwood sandwiches,” off storyboards. Footage from “the six large shooting stages at Elstree Studios in Great Britain, where the full-size *Millennium Falcon* was parked.” VistaVision: “Each frame was four times the size of a regular movie film frame.” “George rounded up the old cameras and projectors.” “George differed from the rest of Hollywood in that he not only made movies, but also wanted to advance the technology… George, on the other hand, was developing a completely new electronic editing system and a better sound system for theaters, and was taking the early steps toward digital cinema.”

Union ten-hour days, overtime, Saturday screenings, organic Thanksgiving turkeys. A model maker hospitalized after a flight to England: “bulletins on his condition every morning before dailies, until it was announced that George showed up at the hospital and was ‘on the case.’”

Lucas in “a flannel shirt and jeans”; executives the “green carpet boys.” Secrecy: no signs; “The front door had lettering that said ‘The Kerner Company. Optical Research Lab.’” Cover names “like *Blue Harvest*.” A former Marine to a suspicious fan: “We manufacture and *test* rectal thermometers.” Fans “would go through the garbage and climb over the fences.”

Darth Vader mannequin, typography locked: “One Saturday… I saw an outside telephone repair guy had brought his kids with him… ‘Would you guys like to see Darth Vader?’ Their dad said it was OK, so I took them over to the model shop, which had a fully costumed seven-foot-tall Darth Vader on a hidden mannequin. Also, R2-D2 and C-3PO were there. This was long before any commercial mock-ups of these characters were out in the real world… They were impressed, but I could tell that they were also slightly disappointed… Without the lighting and music, without James Earl Jones’s thundering Darth Vader voice, it was just an empty suit.”

George’s birthday week, Building D dailies; Kimberlin “just behind his right shoulder”; Joe Johnston “his art director.” Bike Chase footage: “several Ewoks suddenly entered screen right holding a huge banner that said ‘Happy Birthday George.’ It got a big laugh and George kept saying, ‘Make sure that is in tonight’s dailies at my house so Marcia can see it.’ George’s wife Marcia was every bit a full partner in both the moviemaking and the empire-building. She was a first-rate film editor in her own right, having cut *Taxi Driver,* the seminal 1976 film directed by Martin Scorsese. In fact, I once saw her fashion an emotional scene out of some almost-scrap footage of two Ewoks talking. When she gave her opinion, people listened.”

Lucas pouring his own coffee. The morning *E.T.* surpassed *Star Wars* as all-time box-office champ: “Someone asked him about it and he said, ‘Good, now maybe they will leave me alone.’”

Staff Tim: “Hello, Mr. Lucas, I’ve worked for you for over two years and I still haven’t met you.” “Tim said that George almost leaped backwards in shock.” Christmas parties “always slightly awkward, because they seemed to have no host. A simple ‘Thanks for coming’ from George would have done the trick, but that never happened.”

Decision-making, typography locked: “Whenever George was around, the whole movie had a direction and could move forward, but when he was off in England or somewhere, the decisions would pile up… I remember him returning to our screening room after a couple of weeks’ absence… he said, ‘You want decisions? Bring the lights down and roll the film, I’ll give you decisions.’”

Harrison Ford double-take at Kimberlin’s felt hat “that looked a little like the famous Indiana Jones hat.” Rest of the chapter is Kimberlin’s childhood (parents’ deaths; Great-Aunt Bobbitt; neighbor Bessie Barriscale / Mrs. Hickman, “the biggest silent-screen star in the country in 1910”).

## The Droid Olympics

Meritocracy “set from the top, and that was George Lucas. He had come from nowhere, and the message to the employees was that you could come from nowhere too.” Chris, hired young to watch the Ranch: interview with “George and Marcia Lucas at their home in San Anselmo”; they had “purchased the oldest house in Marin County after the success of *American Graffiti*.” Chris asked if friends could visit. “ ‘Of course,’ George said. ‘This will be your home.’ ” Jane Bay: “I’m sorry to bother you, George, but I have Sir Laurence Olivier on the phone, and he wishes to speak with you.” George: “I’m talking to Chris right now. Tell him I’ll call back.” Chris: “That doesn’t happen in Hollywood.”

Droid Olympics at Walter Murch’s house in Inverness. Lucas delivers team T-shirts. Kimberlin: “With these shirts, how can we lose?” Lucas: “At my rate, those are the most expensive T-shirts you will ever own.” After *Empire*: “another employee told me, ‘We all noticed that George got new tires for his Camaro.’” Kimberlin wins a timed Moviola event; Lucas, “who at that time barely knew who I was, walked over and said, ‘Nice job.’ … This guy was so competitive that he could rise out of his reticence if he spotted a winner.”

Title change, typography locked — **do not collapse** with Kazanjian / Cocks / Lucas “always Return” / Pollock / Kaminski: “Originally, *Return of the Jedi* was called *Revenge of the Jedi*. George had decided to change the name of the movie reportedly because some little kid wrote and reminded him that Jedis don’t seek revenge. Whether they do or not, I have no idea.” After months in the trenches “the fantasy stuff had rubbed us a little raw.” Asked to name the company’s central computer, editorial suggested Rochester; “Instead they chose Endor… Yikes.” Camp lines adopted: “Many Bothans died to bring you this information.” “I still like the sentiment in ‘Do. Or do not. There is no try,’ because that comes directly out of George’s personality. He had spent his entire early career with people, sometimes important people, telling him, ‘You can’t do that.’”

Line-up room, typography locked: Lucas asks for Ken, then a shot. “I ran the shot for him and he said something like, ‘Take this X-wing and blow him up, right at this frame.’ For some reason I opened my mouth and said, ‘I don’t think we can do that.’ Wrong answer. George turned to me and said, ‘Of course we can,’ with such force that I instantly glimpsed what had got him where he was. Absolute certainty.” “This guy had probably blown up hundreds of them, as well as destroying thousands of stormtroopers. George hadn’t been angry and he didn’t shout, but he woke me up.”

Ken Ralston, 28, “won five Oscars while at ILM”; later president of Sony Pictures Imageworks; “Talent loves him.” SB19, typography locked: “the nineteenth shot in the space battle and is pictured on the cover of this book. This was the shot that George had told Ken that he wanted to be a ‘wow’ shot. Ken had set up four camera crews shooting the battling spaceships for three months just to get this shot. It contained sixty-three separate elements… It would take about ten hours when it came time for the final printing… It took almost a year to get that shot from start to finish… Oh, and look quickly, because it’s only two seconds long.” Kimberlin “borrowed ships from other shots or other movies. We had tons of reusable elements from the two earlier *Star Wars* films.” “I hadn’t designed it or shot it; that was George, Ken, and the art director Joe Johnson’s work.” (typo locked). Temporary composite shown to Lucas: “I ran it for him and he said, ‘Great.’ That was it. His most frequent expression of approval.” BBC later filmed Ken and Kimberlin reenacting the shot.

Opening-strategy gloss (Kimberlin): Lucas “would open a movie with either an action sequence like the chase in the original *Raiders of the Lost Ark* or the seemingly endless underbelly of the Star Destroyer in the original *Star Wars*… Once the audience felt they were in the safe hands of a movie master, George could afford to introduce his characters and story points without fear of losing them.”

## High School Reunion

Ackbar plates, typography locked: “VistaVision foreground plates of Admiral Ackbar… a guy with a rubber head sitting at some kind of spaceship controls… One of the supervisors, probably Richard Edlund, asked George what the window Akbar was looking out of was made of. George said, ‘Well, that hasn’t come up. When it does come up, we will figure out an answer.’” Kimberlin: “We were all sitting here participating in a story that George was making up as we went along.” Flag as Kimberlin’s conclusion; do not collapse with Lucas’s later “always planned” claims or with Kaminski’s thesis, though it is consistent in spirit with the latter.

Monday after Lucas’s Modesto high-school reunion. Kimberlin drives to Modesto, sees the family office-supply store and “George’s father… at the Ranch wandering around with a huge smile on his face.” Kimberlin: “Just as all the male characters in *American Graffiti* were different facets of George, his father’s rock-solid, small-town business acumen loomed over his creative work…” Flag as Kimberlin. “His wife Marcia often said that George was ‘centered,’ meaning he was comfortable with himself and his ability to achieve his goals.” Left Hollywood for Northern California; “He wrote and developed his own projects, hiring screenwriters to help structure the stories… Above all, he avoided debt like the plague, remembering how it had once brought down and almost destroyed the dreams of his friend Francis Coppola.”

Interactivity: “George’s remark was succinct and accurate: ‘Interactive is games. How hard is that to figure out?’ Soon we had a Games Division.” “I would argue that not since Thomas Edison and Henry Ford have we seen a more ambitious creative artist… So far, it seems that Steve Jobs is the one to have received most of the plaudits, but I think the more Lucas’s accomplishments are recognized, the higher his stock will rise.” Flag as Kimberlin. “I’ve seen his student films and they all showed talent.” Union rumor rebutted: “he quickly signed up with the local unions and we were all members.” Footnote n.1: Rebecca Solnit, *River of Shadows* (Viking, 2003).

## What’s It Like?

Invited to screen *American Nitro* at ILM: “about one minute into it, someone in the audience yelled out, ‘Has George seen this?’ I was working there about a month later.”

Work print of the original *Star Wars*, typography locked: “a reel of picture and a reel of sound from one sequence in the first film. It comprised some of the opening scenes inside a spaceship with Princess Leia trying to escape some stormtroopers… This was not the finished movie—it was the final edit but the music, sound mix, visual effects, color grading, etc., were all not present… Watching it was an eye-opener. Without the final polish it looked awful, worse than amateurish. The prop guns made fizzing and popping sounds when fired, with fake-looking sparks spitting out the front of them. The actors were yelling and it was hard to hear what they were saying, and on top of that you could hear George from somewhere behind the camera yelling, ‘Look to your left! Look to your left!’” “The original *Star Wars* crew was no exception, and I could see why it was rumored that they didn’t expect much from it. A similar thing happened to George at a major screening of *American Graffiti*. The film was almost finished but the head of the studio pronounced it ‘unreleasable.’”

ILM name, second account (Kimberlin): “That is partly why George Lucas called the division of Lucasfilm that I worked for ‘Industrial Light and Magic.’ Because moviemaking is a craft at its base, which with the right collaboration of artists, can become art. But it takes a kind of *magic* to make it all happen…”

Filmmaker vs director: “If you gave George Lucas two months and a hundred pounds of film, he could deliver you a movie. He could write, shoot, and edit it himself. Steven Spielberg can direct brilliant movies, but he once said that when he tried to edit something himself, he was all thumbs and chasing the splicing tape under the editing table.” WWII dogfight footage cut into the original *Star Wars* before the spaceships were shot; “Even as late as *The Phantom Menace,* he was having us send him clips from famous movies that we pulled off of videos in a library of classic movies that I had put together. He wanted to scrape them for ideas… Several years earlier he had shot the whole *Young Indiana Jones* TV series in 16mm.”

Cow Palace explosions for *Empire* reused; Learjet belly-mounted VistaVision cloud plates later sold to Disney for $50,000. At the KEM, someone flags a cut that “crosses the arch”; Lucas: “That’s film school stuff—we’re making a movie here.” “Films are made in the editing room, and you can do any damn thing you can get away with.”

## Hollywood

*Jedi* sneak, early 1983, typography locked: unannounced substitution. Announcer apologizes that the scheduled film is unavailable. “However, in its stead we are going to run the new *Star Wars* film.” “The entire audience erupted in a cavalcade of cheering… The only one I could think of was the old Ed Sullivan introduction, ‘Ladies and gentleman, the Beatles.’” (Kimberlin’s “gentleman,” typography locked.)

After *Jedi*, 70mm-print supervision in L.A.; Ken Ralston “picked up the Oscar awarded to our crew for best achievement in visual effects. Rushing through the names to thank, he mentioned mine.” Howie flies to Farmington, Michigan, for the home-video transfer (Andre Blay / Fox). “Home video… also kept franchises like *Star Wars* alive as people actively awaited new episodes.” Computer division “had made a planet transform in *Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan* using computer graphics as early as 1982.”

Digital actors, typography locked: reporters ask, “When will you be able to create digital actors?” “Our answer was always the same: ‘Why bother? Actors come fairly cheap.’ … Besides, as George has said, ‘You can’t replace actors. We’ve created duplicates, clones, but they can’t act. They’re a computer, for God’s sake.’” Company mantra: no abstract R&D; “Give us real problems and we will solve them for any given movie.”
