## *Jurassic Park*; *Schindler’s List*

“The making of *Jurassic Park* (1993) was a major turning point in motion picture history. Spielberg was all set to make it using traditional stop-motion animation. He had hired Phil Tippett, who now had his own special effects company and was the master of stop motion and the genius behind animating the AT-AT (All Terrain Armored Transport) Walkers sequence in *The Empire Strikes Back*…” Stan Winston on practical creatures. “Somewhat undercover,” Steve “Spaz” Williams, Mark Dippé, and Stephen Fangmeier built a walking T. rex wireframe. Spaz looped it when visitors came through. “One important visitor was Kathleen Kennedy, Spielberg’s longtime producer. ‘What’s that?’” Management would normally prevent such conversations; this one mattered. “When Steven saw the tests… he authorized a budget for seeing if this creature could be fleshed out.” Dennis Muren “in charge… While he may have been skeptical initially, he was now on board,” adding real water splashes and so on.

Kimberlin’s internal ILM documentary: Tippett “told the story of how he learned on a Friday afternoon that Steven had decided to dispense with all stop-motion work and replace it with computer graphics… Phil cupped his palms together in front of my camera and then slowly opened them to simulate” his world evaporating. (Exact gesture description typography-locked in the chapter.) “*Jurassic Park* was a massive hit… Spaz was back in business and would go on to do CG work on a scene in the restoration of *Return of the Jedi* that George had had to drop in the original version because it looked so fake. This restoration of Spaz reminded me of a comment Abraham Lincoln had made when told that General Grant was a drinker: ‘I can’t spare this man—he fights.’”

*Schindler’s List*: daily secure video (Image Net) with Spielberg in Poland, later Martha’s Vineyard (“I just had lunch on the most beautiful yacht”). Spielberg “felt strongly” the subject required black-and-white and “had the power to override the studio.” The girl in the red dress: color preserved in otherwise B&W shots, then “cut into every print released… At least 5,000 prints.” “It was crazy, but that was the plan.” Released that way; “a critical and commercial success.” Kimberlin cites William Goldman: “nobody knows anything.”

## The Intern; Acting Out; Earthquake

Lucasfilm intern program across divisions. Business-affairs office: “a list on the wall of all the countries in the world where *Star Wars* was distributed… broken down into areas like states, and then by cities. The number of cities on that list, that produced the ticket and merchandising sales that supported us all, was mind-boggling.” Intern **John Knoll** builds a mechanical butterfly for *The Golden Child* and shows it to Ken Ralston via Kimberlin. “Gradually people began to realize that this was no ordinary, run-of-the-mill smart kid. In fact, John is the only person I ever heard George Lucas label a genius.” “John is now the creative director of ILM and one of its principle project supervisors.” Kimberlin also brings an eleven-year-old mentee, Michael, from West Oakland to a “take your kid to work” day.

Acting Out: “people working on what I will call genre films, like science fiction for instance, may have no interest in that genre. Nilo Rodis was one of the main designers on both *Star Wars* and *Star Trek* and he has told the tale of flunking his original job interview with George Lucas by saying he didn’t read, watch, or like science fiction. George hired him anyway because of his talent.” *Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom* (1984): “Spielberg’s production staff sent Indy’s prop notebook up to us at ILM because Stephen felt he needed a close-up of the signing and they hadn’t shot one originally.” (“Stephen” typo locked.) Mike Gleason keeps a copy “for the next twenty years”; “the other copy of the prop book was in a glass display case in the main house at the Ranch, along with Indy’s famous hat and whip.” *Ghostbusters II* Titanic dock shot.

Earthquake (1989, Loma Prieta): Kimberlin’s only overnight at the Farm Group apartments. Ranch “never seemed to have any discretionary monies”; editorial “held prints of Lucasfilm titles like all the *Star Wars* and *Raiders* movies” that headquarters kept borrowing. “When George wanted to use some facility, he didn’t want to pay for it. He reasoned that it was his company and he could use it whenever he wanted.” Door story, typography locked: “George was walking around the Ranch buildings with a small group of people one day and he casually mentions, ‘There should be a door there.’ Weeks pass and he is again walking with the same group and notices the new door. Frustrated, he exclaims, ‘Just because I happen to mention something, that doesn’t mean everyone should stop everything and do it!’ Sure enough, the next time he passes, the door is gone and it is impossible to tell that there had ever been” one. Mike’s Skywalker Ranch Honey: after years, “a ranch manager told him to take the bees away because George wanted to know, ‘Who is making money off my property?’” A cameraman who shot 16mm *Empire* making-of on short ends “got a severe reprimand and could have lost his job” but later “selling the footage to the production company when they realized they needed ‘making of the movie’ footage.” Patty Blau, head of production, “read me the riot act” for Kimberlin and Mike’s *Jurassic* dinosaur documentary; “Fortunately, I knew George’s editor at the ranch. He showed our film to George and George called the head of Lucas Digital, Jim Morris,” who was Patty’s boss — the project stands.

## The Distraction

Restoration / Special Edition, typography locked. “When it came time to restore the original *Star Wars* and rerelease it (1997), George called a few people together in the Building C theater and we screened his personal print.” “As the film rolled in theater C, George made comments on the scenes he wanted to fix or, in some cases, add. From his perspective, these changes were all based on what he wanted to do in the first place but had either run out of time, technology, or money on the first go-round. Now that he had all three at his disposal, he wanted to get it exactly as he originally envisioned it.” Flag “from his perspective” as Kimberlin. “After George pointed out all of the shots he wanted fixed, he said something I have wondered about ever since. He said that he was going to do a comedy version of *Star Wars.* It’s been years since that day and it hasn’t happened yet, but remember, you heard it here first!”

Kimberlin e-mails Lucas proposing a documentary of still-living original-film crew, citing the *Jurassic* doc Lucas had liked. “The next day my phone rang and it was George’s attorney. He told me that George liked my idea and that he, Howard Rothman, would be in charge of the project. I never heard from him again.” A month later Kimberlin writes again; “In a few days I got a call from Lynn Hale, the head of publicity, informing me that there was going to be a documentary, but that it would be handled by the studio. I have never seen it.” Fellow editor Tom Christopher, hired “to handle all the film and lab details of what he wanted done to the original *Star Wars*”: “Tom said he remembered my letter coming in because George’s longtime producer Rick McCallum wanted to know, ‘Who the hell is this guy?’”

“None of the Lucasfilm executives wanted George fooling around with his old movies. ‘It’s a distraction,’ they said. ‘We are not in the restoration business.’ The new movies were where the big money was… If George would commit to make three more *Star Wars* sequels, there was no limit to the money that could be made.” ~1996: “The ancillary money stream from toy deals that George had so famously bargained for with the original *Star Wars* had dwindled. The bonanza of home video sales had also dissipated… ILM was the bright spot.” Original plan “to only redo the wipes, those famously old-fashioned *Star Wars* transitions.” ILM “opened a project billing account and started charging all our time to it.” Tom asks a committee for three purchase orders; “Game over.” He finds the original negative at Fox and separation masters “in a salt mine somewhere in the Midwest, but he couldn’t find the original negatives for the wipes… Nobody asked me. They were in my ILM editorial warehouse.”

Fox/Technicolor protection masters of *Star Wars* “did it on the cheap… the wet printing method is more expensive”; they used dry. “Soon all was ready to cut into the original *Star Wars* negative. Before doing that, however, Tom wanted to check something with George. He explained to George that everything was ready to cut in, but he wanted to know whether they should order a duplicate negative of the original version… George said, ‘This *is* the original version,’ meaning that what everyone else had described as a restoration, he considered the final version of the movie.” Kimberlin then asks, as himself, who should decide whether a cultural work may be altered even by its maker — flag as Kimberlin, not Lucas. “The original *Star Wars* was rereleased in 1997 as the ‘Special Edition’ and it skyrocketed to the number one position at the box office for three weeks, grossing over $138 million. This surprised a lot of people, even George I suspect. *Empire* and *Jedi* soon followed, all of them together grossing more than $250 million. These unwanted rereleases helped build a cash pile that could fund the new *Star Wars* films that would ultimately generate billions of dollars in new revenue.”

## Digital City; The Stone Age Institute; Dropping Dead; About the Author

~1999, ILM has outgrown Marin; expanding the Ranch would anger neighbors. Pitch to move to the Presidio / old Letterman Hospital site. “This move also was tantamount to admitting that the Ranch was not where all the new young talent wanted” to work. Kimberlin works on Lucas’s short film for the board; director/cinematographer Bob Dalva, “a long history with both Lucas and Coppola,” shouts down an opening-montage idea, accepts Sandra Joy Lee’s antique-projector collection: “She also donated her time working as a projectionist at the local art house movie theater that Marcia Lucas had donated money to restore.” Kimberlin writes a paper for screenwriter Matthew Robbins, “an old friend of George’s.” Letterman Digital Arts Center approved. “I told one of the Presidio neighbors that I didn’t have a dog in this fight, I just worked for the guy, but I thought people would be crazy to turn it down, especially if you consider the quality of workmanship Lucas is known for.”

Stone Age Institute tour of ILM and lunch at the Ranch inspires their Indiana limestone campus. “I remembered something Lucas had said after donating hundreds of millions of dollars to USC for a new film school building: ‘If you want a discipline to be taken seriously, you need a serious building.’” “Phil Tippet” (spelling locked) on living animals as a paleontological record. Gordon Getty’s converted airliner.

Dropping Dead, typography locked: “Mine came on a Friday afternoon in 2002… They had just awarded me with a golden statue of the *Star Wars* character C-3PO to mark my twenty years. The first day of my employment, I had signed in with the receptionist, and when I walked past her, she said just loud enough for me to hear, ‘Day one.’” “It was originally supposed to be about an eight-month gig, but the films just kept coming. I was the chief visual effects editor and was promoted to department head… I lasted nine years, which was a record. I survived four management changes and numerous political battles, but when the digital revolution hit with full force, my age began to cripple” his prospects. Boonville country place. “In 2002 I decided to follow the Mark Twain route and distribute my own movie. Like George Lucas, I felt that now no one could tell that I was too old or uncool to make movies.” (Echo of the earlier “quoted as saying” line in *American Nitro*.) *American Nitro* cult revival; Library of Congress: “your movie is pure Americana.” Lesson: “You have to make a living, but you need to also do your own work.”
