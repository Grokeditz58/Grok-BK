## Chapter Seven: The World Devastators
> "Umak Leth, Chief Engineer to the Emperor, came to his master with a new design for a molecular furnace that could break down the physical structure of matter." — p. 112
> "In funding Leth’s project, Palpatine cleverly exploited his engineer’s ambition to exceed the great Bevel Lemelisk." — p. 112
> "The World Devastators are enormous droids: the crew, technicians and slaves live in special compartments inside." — p. 112

### Hextrophon lecture (p. 114)
> "After the one-in-a-trillion shot that blew the first Death Star to atoms in the Yavin system, Imperial propaganda departments went into overdrive." — p. 114
> "Palpatine had even secretly destroyed the most recent of these, the Outbound Flight project. According to what we have retrieved from Admiral Thrawn’s notes, he led the mission to kill the six Jedi aboard the ship." — p. 114
> "It seems obvious that Palpatine’s ultimate goal was the conquest, not just of our galaxy, but of others." — p. 114

### Master Control Signal and growth (p. 115)
> "The Master Control Signal is the most highly guarded secret in the entire Imperial arsenal because with it one can override all commands from the bridge. ... Luke Skywalker retrieved the codes for the Rebellion, and R2-D2 was able to command the World Devastators to shut down and turn their weapons upon each other." — p. 115
> "The longer a Devastator functions, the more it improves and modifies itself, growing larger and more lethal. ... Theoretically, a single Devastator could eventually become large enough to strip a planet unaided in a few weeks or even days. Neither Umak Leth nor his technicians have any idea what the upper limit to a Devastator’s growth is." — p. 115
> "The sun-hot maw of the molecular furnace at the front, bottom or rear of a World Devastator can break down any structure, from bare mountainside to starship to gleaming city." — p. 116
- Lore: to hide the Silencer-1 build at Thomork, "The Emperor’s agents had to kill over 450 people to make their story hold" (p. 115).
