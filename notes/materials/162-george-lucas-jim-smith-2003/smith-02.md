# *American Graffiti* (pp. 28–47) and *More American Graffiti* (pp. 87–95)

## *American Graffiti* (1973)

Credits (p. 28, footer verified on the Virgin scan): written by George Lucas and Willard Huyck & Gloria Katz; produced by Francis Ford Coppola; co-produced by Gary Kurtz; edited by Verna Fields and Marcia Lucas; directed by George Lucas. Principal cast as listed (Dreyfuss, Ronny Howard, Paul Le Mat, Charlie Martin Smith, Cindy Williams, Candy Clark, Mackenzie Phillips, Wolfman Jack, Harrison Ford as Bob Falfa, Suzanne Somers as the blonde in the T-Bird, etc.).

**Screenplay (pp. 29–32).** Coppola pushes Lucas toward something more ‘warm and fuzzy’ than *THX*. United Artists’ David Picker advances $10,000. Richard Walter’s drafts are rejected as the wrong adolescence (East Coast urban tone, a sex scene, elopement). The Huycks return for a pre-production rewrite, especially the Steve/Laurie strand, which Lucas felt was ‘based far less on his own experiences’.

**Casting and shoot (pp. 32–36).** Fred Roos ‘cattle calls’. Wolfman Jack (Robert Weston Smith) on the soundtrack and in a cameo. Night shooting in San Rafael; permit revoked; move to Petaluma. Industry legend: at the wrap-party reel Harrison Ford shouts ‘This is a fucking hit!’

**Cuts (pp. 36–37).** Universal removes two and a half minutes (Steve shouting at a teacher; Falfa singing ‘Some Enchanted Evening’; a fast-talking car salesman). Lucas reads the trims as ‘a deliberate, entirely cynical display of their power’. A scripted opening of a transparent dream-girl in a deserted drive-in is never shot.

**Music (pp. 38–39).** Forty period recordings; Elvis rights refused; Beach Boys cheaply licensed via Dennis Wilson / Kurtz. Lucas and Murch re-record the source records ‘live’ in concrete spaces so they will not sound too clean.

**Recurring concerns (p. 39–40).** Cars as identity. Smith (analysis): in Lucas’s later directorial features ‘both Luke and later Anakin Skywalker can only escape their mundane home lives when they’ve respectively sold and abandoned their car-type vehicles’. He reads this as a shift away from the ‘adolescent mind-frame’ of *THX* and the student films.

**Release and reception (pp. 43–47).** Huge commercial success after a troubled preview history (the book also recounts the earlier *THX* and preview-theatre lore). Contemporary reviews are quoted at length (*Variety*, *New York Times*, *New Statesman*, *Daily Telegraph*, *Spectator*).

**LUCAS ON AMERICAN GRAFFITI (p. 47 area; Index also points to production notes / Sturhahn):** ‘It all happened to me ... I went through all that stuff, drove the cars, bought liquor, chased girls . . . but I sort of glamorised it.’ Index p. 48 also records ‘The film is .. .’ from Sturhahn, *Filmmaker’s Newsletter*, March 1974, and Charlie Martin Smith on Lucas letting the cast invent.

No Force / Vader / Sith / Chosen One material in this chapter beyond the later-career car/Anakin comparison, which is Smith’s.

## *More American Graffiti* (1979) (pp. 87–95)

Sequel directed by B. W. L. Norton; Lucas as executive producer / story source. Four storylines (New Year’s Eves 1964–1967) in split-screen and other formal experiments; Vietnam, protest, and the end of the *Graffiti* ensemble’s world. Smith quotes Lucas on taking risks, and Norton on the editing and on the film’s ‘white-bread’ characters. Reviews are largely unkind (*Variety* ‘daring but…’; *New York Times* ‘trivialisation’; later *Empire* magazine on coherence).

**LUCAS ON MORE AMERICAN GRAFFITI:** ‘I did *More American Graffiti*, it made 10 cents, it failed miserably.’ (Smith cites the *Phantom Menace* DVD as the source.) The failure is reused later as Lucas’s own caution, during *Episode I* production, that ‘You can destroy these things. It is possible’ (see smith-07.md).
