# *Return of the Jedi* (pp. 128–140); *Temple of Doom* and the Ewok TV movies (pp. 141–161)

## *Star Wars: Episode VI — Return of the Jedi* (1983)

Smith’s plot summary includes the Emperor’s trap, Luke’s refusal, the second ‘death rather than the dark side’ choice, and Vader killing the Emperor, ‘mortally wounded defending his child’. Luke burns Vader’s body; ‘three spectral figures: Obi-Wan Kenobi, Yoda and Anakin Skywalker, redeemed by his final sacrifice and returned to the light.’

**Screenplay, p. 129–131.** Lucas turns to ‘the third act of his 1976 synopsis’, writes a rough draft, then brings Kasdan back (Kasdan calls it ‘a favour’). Spielberg offers to direct; Lucas refuses. Spielberg in 2002: ‘Star Wars is George’s baby … This is George’s . . . fingerprints.’ Richard Marquand is hired after *Eye of the Needle*; Lucas is ‘very, very impressed’. Actor conferences feed the rewrite.

**The ending, p. 131 — important.** Kasdan suggests radical alternatives: Luke succumbing and becoming ‘the Emperor’s new enforcer’; Luke dying of his injuries; Luke using the Death Star to destroy the Imperial capital. ‘All of these were rejected by Lucas who felt very strongly that the ending as planned was the core of the film and should not be discarded.’ ‘Lucas’s handwritten first draft ends, as the film does, with Vader dying saving his son from the Emperor.’ Lucas contemplates directing it himself, then does not: ‘I took one look at the amount of work and thought, “My God, my life is complicated enough”.’

Production: Buttercup Valley, Arizona, under the cover title *Blue Harvest* (‘Horror beyond your imagination’). Reports that Lucas was a ‘back-seat driver’; Marquand ‘always strenuously denied’ that Lucas directed the film in all but name. Special Edition (1997) has the most OT alterations: new Jabba-palace musical number (‘Jedi Rocks’), and a glimpse of Coruscant after Vader’s funeral because Lucas ‘was already aware that the planet would be a major location throughout the prequel trilogy’. A sandstorm departure scene with the full-size Falcon is cut for a moment of calm. A cut beat of Vader Force-choking throne-room guards is dropped because the gag had been ‘perhaps overplayed in *Empire*’.

**Recurring concerns, pp. 137–138 — compassionate life, and Anakin’s return.** If Lucas’s talk of a ‘conscious modern mythology’ is to be given weight, ‘now is the point to look at what the Imperial trilogy seems to say. Lucas himself never made any claims beyond the obvious for the series, saying that it was about living “a compassionate life” — that the characters and situations demonstrated the value of selflessness over selfishness, and that this was the job of a certain kind of story.’ Han’s arc is the working example. Then Luke: he ‘proves that he is the man his father failed to become, and again chooses death rather [than] corruption’ — ‘Never, I’ll never turn to the dark side… I am a Jedi, like my father before me.’ ‘Lucas is repeating the idea that death is preferable to dishonour… This is what Vader’s final sacrifice is about.’ In TITLE TATTLE Smith adds that the film ‘isn’t about the return of the Jedi in the sense that it is about the restoration of the Jedi order; it’s about the return of one particular Jedi, Anakin Skywalker.’ (Smith’s reading of the title, not a Lucas quote.)

Reviews are ‘indulgent but weary’; one *New York Times* line: ‘The force is with them but the magic is gone.’

**LUCAS ON RETURN OF THE JEDI:** ‘This one was grim for me, just as bad as directing . . . *Jedi* almost killed everybody, every department. Everything was very, very hard on everybody.’

## *Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom* (1984) (pp. 141–150)

Lucas’s story, Spielberg directs, Willard Huyck & Gloria Katz write. Lucas on dropping Marion, on the picture as ‘an experiment’, and on not being ‘quite sure’ about tone (home-video interviews, Index pp. 144–145, 149). PG-13 / British cuts are discussed. Smith defends the film as deliberately shaped like a serial: ‘strings of set-pieces’ that are ‘the point’. No Force/Sith material.

## *The Ewok Adventure* (1984) and *Ewoks — The Battle for Endor* (1985) (pp. 151–161)

TV movies; Lucas as executive producer / story. Smith is not an admirer of them as cinema (he later calls out their ‘outright sentimentality and lazy plotting’ in the *Phantom Menace* chapter). Korty and Thomas G. Smith are quoted; Warwick Davis appears.

**LUCAS ON THE EWOK ADVENTURE,** answering a merchandising-cash-in charge: ‘There are lots of teddy bears marketed [already]; you don’t have anything . . . unique. If I were designing something original as a market item, I could probably do a lot better.’ (*Rolling Stone*, 24 July–4 August 1984, per the Index.)
