# *Star Wars* (1977) (Smith pp. 48–86)

Credits and a long principal-cast list open the chapter (p. 48; David Prowse as Darth Vader, James Earl Jones as the voice). Smith’s plot summary (pp. 48–50) is a straight walkthrough of the 1977 film, including Kenobi’s claim that Luke’s father was ‘betrayed and murdered by Darth Vader, a young Jedi who … was a pupil of Kenobi’s before turning to evil’, Kenobi’s death, and Vader’s escape from the Death Star.

## Screenplay and the Force (pp. 50–56)

Origin hard to pin down. During *THX* (1970) Lucas told Lalo Schifrin he wanted to make a Flash Gordon picture ‘how he remembered them as having been’; Di Laurentiis held the rights, so he began to invent his own. After *Graffiti*’s pre-release troubles he wrote an outline whose first sentence Smith quotes: ‘The Story of Mace Windu. Mace Windu, a revered Jedi-Bendu of Opuchi who was related to Usby CJ Thape, Padewaan learner to the [a] famed Jedi’. ‘Jedi’ is, Smith says, a word Lucas coined for ‘a clan of warrior mystics, samurai monks’, named from Japanese *jidai geki*.

Shortly after release Lucas claimed to have written ‘four entirely different screenplays’ and called *Star Wars* ‘a good idea in search of a story’ (*American Cinematographer*, July 1977; Index p. 52). Research backwards from Flash Gordon through Burroughs, Verne, Edwin Arnold; then Joseph Campbell, especially *Myths to Live By* (1972). **Lucas (Index p. 53):** ‘The book began to focus what I had already been doing intuitively.’

Visual aims (Index pp. 52–53): ‘cohesive reality’; a ‘used future’ inspired by a lived-in Apollo capsule; he ‘wanted the seeming contradiction of . . . fantasy combined with the feel of a documentary’. To Lucas the film ‘wasn’t science fiction at all; it was, as *THX* had been, a “documentary fantasy”.’ John Barry is quoted: Lucas wanted it to look ‘like it’s shot on location on your average everyday Death Star or spaceport’.

Ladd Jr at Fox: ten days after the pitch, a development deal. Ladd reportedly: ‘I’m interested, I don’t understand this, but I loved *American Graffiti* and whatever you do is OK with me . . .’ (p. 54, scan-verified). Fox money: $15,000 to develop, $50,000 to write, $100,000 to direct; Kurtz $50,000 as producer.

**p. 55 (scan-verified) — Kiber Crystal, Knights of Sith, trilogy outline, fairy tale.** The ecstatic *Graffiti* preview, Smith says, suggested ‘nobility as well as commercial advantage in making uplifting films’. Early *Star Wars* drafts had had ‘mutilation and torture and far more unpleasant violence’. The film ‘consciously became … fun’. In notes for the novelisation Lucas calls it ‘a movie for the kid in all of us’, hoped to drive away ‘the grimness of everyday life’ and stop it ‘following us into the movie theater’. He would spend the rest of his life describing it as ‘a fairy tale’. Then the draft-history paragraph:

> a ‘Kiber Crystal’ (a physical embodiment of the force) was written in and then written out. … A new character named Darth Vader — sometimes a rogue Jedi, sometimes a member of a rival clan called ‘the Knights of Sith’ — had his role expanded. Some drafts killed him off during the explosion of the Death Star, while others allowed him to survive; across subsequent drafts his role continued to grow. … It is somewhere around this point that he claims to have written an outline which comprised in essence the events of *Star Wars* and its two sequels.

Smith immediately qualifies the outline claim as ‘often dismissed’, possibly never in full-script form, and quickly set aside so that the Death Star climax could end a shorter, more saleable picture. Friends who read drafts include De Palma, the Huycks, and Jay Cocks. De Palma’s sarcasm about ‘the farts of others’ is why ‘the force of others’ ‘lose[s] the second half of its name’ (pp. 55–56).

Deal points (pp. 55–56): merchandising (60%, rising), music, sequel rights (a sequel must be in production within two years), and official production by Lucas’s own ‘Star Wars Corporation’ (40% of net). Shooting draft dated 15 January 1976 is ‘the first version of the script in which Obi-Wan Kenobi dies’ after the Vader fight; Guinness is ‘initially unhappy’, then persuaded that a heroic death plus a spectral voice would be more memorable.

**Lucas on his own writing (Index p. 57):** ‘I’m not a good writer. It’s very, very hard for me. I don’t .. . have a natural talent for it.’ Smith nonetheless calls the *Star Wars* script ‘an excellent piece of work’.

## Casting, production, Special Edition (pp. 57–73)

Joint cattle calls with De Palma’s *Carrie*. Ford is steered back into the room by Roos (he is building Roos a desk). Guinness is first thought of after Toshiro Mifune; he takes 2¼% of Lucas’s 40%. Tunisia; dewbacks and banthas mostly fail in 1976 (later Special Edition fixes). Elstree is chosen because nine stages can be held and because the facility has no imposed staff — ‘This … sealed the deal for Lucas and Kurtz’. The opening rebel-ship boarding is offered as a model of the ‘documentary fantasy’ camera style.

Preview at the Northpoint, LA: the audience stands and claps when the Falcon saves Luke. The original negative is worn out by the number of prints Fox struck; remastering ‘mutated’ into the Special Edition. Lucas: there were always things he ‘wasn’t satisfied with, special-effects shots that were never really finished, scenes that I wanted to include [that weren’t] included for reasons of money and time’ (pp. 69–70). Jabba in the hangar: he had intended a creature, shot Declan Mulholland as a stand-in, and later said, ‘At that point .. . ILM was coping with so much work . . . we were way behind schedule ... to add that sequence would have broken the back of the operation.’ Boba Fett’s later insertion is, Smith says, partly to support ‘planned *Star Wars* prequels’.

Two types of SE change (p. 71): ‘One is restoration ... to make it a cleaner, clearer picture of high quality that will be preserved longer . . . A lot of the ILM changes we made were . .. for aesthetic reasons.’ On further changes: ‘There’s no real emotional things on the creative side [left to] come along.’ **Greedo (p. 71; Index):** ‘In terms of Han’s character I didn’t like the fact that the first thing he does is gun someone down in cold blood.’

## Music, motifs, release, criticism (pp. 74–85)

Temp track from Holst, North, Herrmann; Lucas wanted a Korngold-like orchestral score. Recurring ‘circles’, including Vader’s ‘The circle is now complete’, and the decision to ‘begin the *Star Wars* saga in the middle’ so that ‘form imitates content’. Crew screenings before production: *2001*, *Silent Running*, *Once Upon a Time in the West*, Fellini’s *Satyricon*. ‘Hidden fortress’ is spoken in the film as a nod to Kurosawa.

Release 25 May 1977 to ‘a paltry 32 screens’ (Smith); record opening week, wider release later. Lucas on money (p. 81 area): America needs to stop dwelling on ‘the monetary side’ of movie-making — ‘How much does a film cost? How much does a film make? It’s very depressing to dwell on that. No film is about what it cost.’ Jay Cocks / *Time*: ‘a subliminal history of the movies’. Oscars: the film wins technical awards; Lucas is nominated in three categories and wins none.

**LUCAS ON STAR WARS (p. 85; Index p. 86, *Mail on Sunday*, 28 April 2002):** ‘. . . life sends us along funny paths. We think we know where we are going but we don’t. I was really determined to be a documentary filmmaker and do avant garde things on the side.’
