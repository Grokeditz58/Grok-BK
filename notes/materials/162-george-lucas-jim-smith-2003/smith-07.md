# Prequels, *Episode III* (pre-release), Other Projects, Postscript (Smith pp. 220–end)

## *Star Wars: Episode I — The Phantom Menace* (1999) (pp. 220–243)

Credits list Lucas as writer-director and executive producer, Rick McCallum as producer. The book consistently misspells the Jedi master as **‘Qui-Gon Ginn’**. Tagline: ‘Every generation has a legend. Every journey has a first step. Every story has a beginning.’

**Plot summary, pp. 220–222 — midichlorians and the Chosen One.** This is Smith restating the film, not a new interview. Qui-Gon tells the Council the attacker was ‘well trained in the Jedi arts’, possibly ‘a Sith Lord’; the Council thinks this unlikely because ‘the Sith have been extinct for millennia’. Qui-Gon ‘believes the boy to be “the chosen one”, the child prophesied as the one who will “bring balance to the force”.’ Smith then glosses: ‘The force is an energy field created by life which can be communicated with by listening to midichlorians, microscopic lifeforms living in symbiosis with humans and other sentient beings which communicate the will of the force.’ Anakin’s count is ‘unusually high’. Yoda: ‘Always two there are, a master and an apprentice.’ Yoda to Anakin (QUOTES): ‘Fear leads to anger, anger leads to hate, hate leads to suffering. I sense much fear in you.’

**Screenplay, p. 223.** On 1 November 1994 Lucas, ‘a divorced father of three’, starts the first prequel draft by ‘transcribing his various “backstory” notes, some of them prepared as long ago as 1975’, with Keystone Cops tapes playing and a copy of Julius Caesar’s *The Civil Wars* on the desk, writing ‘in pencil and in longhand’ on pads placed ‘into the same ring-binder’ (a ‘self-consciously myth-making exercise’). Carrie Fisher and Lawrence Kasdan are not used as co-writers; the script is ‘all — or at least primarily — his own work’.

Casting notes include Liam Neeson as ‘Alan Ladd in *Shane*’, and Samuel L. Jackson’s enthusiasm for the line ‘May the force be with you’, after which Lucas ‘promise[s] him that Windu could have an expanded role’ in Episode II. Lucas funds the ‘$100-plus million’ budget himself. A Tunisia sandstorm on 29 July 1997 is dubbed ‘good luck’ because something similar happened in 1976. CGI is the technical centre of the chapter (Jar Jar as a path to later Gollum; ‘If this picture works … then everybody else will want [its technical innovations] for their picture’).

A cut Greedo-as-child scene is, for Smith, an ‘ill-judged’ piece of series-binding. Williams’s ‘Anakin’s Theme’ hides a refrain from the Imperial March and so ‘serves as a constant reminder of Anakin’s destiny’.

**FORWARDS/BACKWARDS, pp. 233–236.** The film has ‘two completely different, incompatible, diametrically opposed meanings’ depending on whether the viewer already knows Palpatine is the Emperor. Smith walks through backwards storytelling: the Obi-Wan/Anakin handshake; Palpatine watching Anakin’s career ‘with great interest’; Kenobi’s later memory that Luke’s father was ‘already a great pilot’ and that he was ‘amazed how strongly the force was with him’, dramatised as the midichlorian test (‘The reading’s off the chart!’); Yoda’s ‘Fear leads to anger…’ as the reason Yoda bristles when Luke says he is ‘not afraid’ in *Empire*; Vader in *Empire* pushing Luke down ‘his own prior path’. Smith leaves open whether Lucas had ‘some unfeasibly complicated pre-existing plan’ or is ‘skilfully working within the margins of what he has already created’, and finds either ‘very clever indeed’.

Lucas before release: ‘We’re never going to beat *Titanic* … it’s not possible’; ‘You can destroy these things. It is possible.’ Smith surveys both raves (Maslin, Ebert) and pans (Lane in the *New Yorker*), and insists on the ‘utter sincerity’ of the *Star Wars* story.

**p. 240 — demystifying the Force (Smith’s analysis, not a Lucas quote).** The film ‘isn’t the story of a wise Jedi called Obi-Wan Kenobi who finds an angry young boy called Anakin and trains him to be a Jedi’. The Empire ‘is the Republic under different leadership’, rotting ‘from the inside’. Then:

> Lucas somehow both aggrandises and demystifies his own universe. He strips the force of its pseudo-religious status whilst making Darth Vader overtly messianic. He shows the Republic of which the older characters mourned the passing, and makes it a far from perfect world (Tatooine seems to be in a worse position under the Republic than the Empire). He subtly raises the idea that Ben and Yoda are manipulating Luke in the Imperial trilogy, and even seems to flat-out contradict the later pictures on a couple of occasions. Seems to, that is, until the viewer looks at them, particularly *Star Wars*, afresh and realises that they dovetail with fearful precision.

The next page adds that it is ‘the audience’s mistaken assumptions that *The Phantom Menace* disregards, never the other films’ own few certainties.’ Title tattle: the ‘phantom menace’ may be Sidious, the unknown plot, the decoy invasion, or ‘Vader, a shade of the future’, whose breathing is heard over the credits.

Jar Jar racism controversy, p. 242: Lucas to Kirsty Wark, 13 July 1999 — ‘How in the world could you take an orange amphibian and say that he’s a Jamaican? It’s completely absurd!’ And: ‘… those criticisms are made by people who’ve obviously never met a Jamaican.’ On Watto / Fagin-type complaints, Smith reports Lucas’s view that the comments ‘reflected more on “the people who are making the comments than it does the movie”.’

**LUCAS ON THE PHANTOM MENACE:** ‘I made this film because I like to make movies [and] I wanted to finish this. I like the whole movie. I’ve taken some chances in storytelling and the way I put it together and by and large I think I’ve been successful.’

## *Star Wars: Episode II — Attack of the Clones* (2002) (pp. 244–260)

Plot summary: assassination attempts, Kamino’s unauthorised clone army, the Tusken massacre, Dooku, Geonosis, the secret marriage. DVD adds Padmé’s ‘To be angry is to be human’ and Anakin’s ‘I’m a Jedi. I know I’m better than this’.

**Smith’s reading of the romance, pp. 250–251 area (analysis, not Lucas):** the Anakin/Padmé scenes are ‘deliberately shot like’ Douglas Sirk and are there ‘to underscore and parody the simple fact that the two characters aren’t falling in love at all’. Anakin ‘never tells her he loves her’. Visually, ‘Anakin often casts Darth Vader’s shadow’. Owen is ‘simply Anakin’s adoptive brother’, not his brother by blood. Palpatine assigns the protection detail ‘presumably because he is aware that the emotional turmoil’ will help push Anakin ‘towards the dark side of the force’. Structural rhyme with *Empire*: a young Jedi loses a right hand to a Sith Lord, confronts the dark side after a parent is involved, downbeat ending — ‘in line with Lucas’s declared desire for drama which “rhymes”’.

Hayden Christensen: ‘The story and the characters are almost in the public domain ... I was playing a character who was already defined by other films.’

**LUCAS ON ATTACK OF THE CLONES:** ‘It’s what I was always aspiring to but never quite got there . . . anything I can imagine I can do.’

## *Star Wars: Episode III* (then due 2005) (pp. 261–264)

This chapter is a **pre-release sketch**, written in 2003. Smith’s SUMMARY is a reconstruction from then-public backstory, and it does **not** match the finished 2005 film in several respects (a group hides Anakin’s ‘pregnant wife and unborn children’; Padmé takes Leia to Alderaan; Ben ‘appeals’ to Vader then duels). Do not use this summary as a plot source for *Revenge of the Sith*.

**FORWARDS/BACKWARDS, pp. 262–263 — ‘This is all pure speculation.’** Smith says Episode III will have to explain ‘the prophecy of one who will bring balance to the force’ — ‘explicitly Anakin’, though ‘the manner in which he will do this (and indeed when) is more of a mystery’. He floats that ‘balance’ might be ‘the extermination of the Jedi’; that Mace and Yoda worry because they know ‘what he has been chosen to do is to [their] detriment’; that the Force is ‘out of balance’ because ‘there are thousands of Jedi and only two Sith’; that the Jedi may once have wronged the Sith (Maul speaks of ‘revenge’). Applied to the OT: two Jedi and two Sith in *Star Wars*; Luke as himself ‘balance’ (black clothes, chain clasp, ‘one hand in the darkness and the other in the light’); Vader’s death leaving ‘only one Jedi left alive, his son’. Then, as a separate paragraph: **‘This is all pure speculation.’**

**Dark side, p. 263 (also prospective):** Episode III will have to show ‘what Lucas sees as succumbing to “the dark side”’. In the OT, Yoda and Obi-Wan call it ‘the quick and easy path’, ‘give in to hate’, essentially using the Force for selfish rather than selfless ends. ‘Yet Vader … speaks more than once of “the power of the dark side”’ and seems ‘regretful’ in *Jedi*. Smith wonders whether the process ‘resembles possession or is akin to becoming schizoid’; whether the dark side ‘is in some sense conscious’; whether Sith second names separate ‘their previous self from their new one’. He cites Vader’s ‘That name no longer has any meaning for me’, and Kenobi agreeing that the Force ‘controls your actions’. Tolkien’s Ring is the offered metaphor. Flag every bit of this as Smith thinking ahead of a film he has not seen.

Lucas is quoted calling the still-unmade story ‘very small and personal’ and ‘too dark to really succeed as mainstream summer entertainment’. The title is to be revealed on starwars.com in summer 2004.

**LUCAS ON EPISODE III (p. 264):** ‘I will not do a 7, 8 and 9. This is it. This is all there is.’

## *Indiana Jones IV* (then dated 2005) (pp. 265–266)

Another 2003 preview, not the 2008 film. Shyamalan is asked, then Frank Darabont, working from a Lucas plot Ford and Spielberg approved. Spielberg hopes for Connery; Kate Capshaw is ‘confirmed’. Set in the 1950s. **Lucas:** ‘Indiana will have a son’; on casting, ‘Yes, I know who. But I won’t tell you yet.’ Intended 4 July 2005 release, ‘a one two punch’ with Episode III. Treat as a superseded production bulletin.

## Other Projects (pp. 267–273)

A chronological personal-involvement list (not games, comics, novels, or every LucasArts title). Smith explicitly will not list ‘the canned fruit factory Lucas once owned, or his brief interest in taking over the bankrupt Delorean car company’. Entries include student ‘help’ and sound work; *Gimme Shelter*; *The Rain People* already covered; *Latino* (Wexler, 1985); *Mishima* (Schrader, 1985) — Lucas ‘executive producing and taking a keen interest’, ‘a hand in the preparation of the final cut’; *Labyrinth*; *Howard the Duck* — Lucas ‘visited the set fewer than half a dozen times’ and later said his participation was ‘far less than people assumed’; *Captain Eo*, ‘written and executive produced by Lucas’, ‘Dated and unfunny now’; *Droids* (set ‘ostensibly … between *Episode III* and the original *Star Wars*’) and *Ewoks* (one episode titled ‘Blue Harvest’); *Tucker: The Man and His Dream*, which Lucas funded ($24 million) and executive-produced, ‘one of the most emotionally satisfying projects of his career’.

## Postscript: “Defining Star Wars” (pp. 275–277)

Printed chapter start is p. 275 (contents page and Virgin-scan footer both confirm; the opening page was opened).

**p. 275 (scan-verified):** *Star Wars* is ‘the vessel into which he’s poured the bulk of his creative endeavours’. ‘He’s called it “my destiny” and his “narrative symphony”.’ Motifs are ‘picked up and reprised’ — Thomas Snyder’s ‘duplication and inversion’, linked by Smith to Arnold van Gennep’s *Rites of Passage*. The 1977 film combines Campbellian archetypes with Lucas’s ‘self-described “documentary fantasy” approach’ and ‘simple, spiritually aware yet ultimately secular values’, including ‘the importance of living a compassionate life, as opposed to a selfish one’, ‘as Lucas himself once put it’ (a slightly tightened reprise of the p. 4 quotation; typography on this page has no mid-phrase ellipses). *Variety*’s ‘concepts of valour, dedication and honour’ is placed beside it. Biskind: Lucas stripped Eisenstein’s Marxist ideology and Bruce Conner’s irony and put the techniques ‘solely to the service of the story’.

**p. 276 (scan-verified):** During and after the first release Lucas would only call it ‘a fairy tale’ and ‘fun’. Later:

> ‘someone has to tell young people what we think is a good person. “This is what a good person is; this is who we aspire to be.” You need that in a society. It’s the basic job of mythology’.

Smith will not yet call the series ‘one of western culture’s mythologies’ or grant it ‘the cultural weight of an *Iliad* or *Odyssey*’; Lucas ‘may very well turn out to be more Charlie Chaplin than Homer’.

**p. 277:** ‘However it absolutely isn’t a religion; Lucas has been both adamant and careful about that, even demystifying and redefining the force and the Jedi within the fiction itself in order to separate himself from those who see it that way.’ He ‘curtly dismissed’ Coppola’s ‘half-serious suggestion that he found a Jedi church (“forget it” was his response …) … and refused to make the force his own dianetics.’ He ‘publicly explained why he felt it would be wrong for him to do so. He’s a filmmaker, or more simply a storyteller, not an ideologue, and one without “any interest in power”.’ Closing: *Star Wars* is, ‘to borrow a phrase from one of Lucas’s cast, “more than just movies” now.’

## Back matter (pp. 278–end)

**Index of Quotations (pp. 278–285)** lists sources for the book’s quotations (periodical, date, and often writer). It is the first place to go when a Lucas line in these notes needs a named interview. Useful confirmations include p. 4 / *Starburst* #125 for the compassionate-life statement; *Show*, April 1970, for the THX ‘cubist’ / cage-door lines; *American Cinematographer*, July 1977, for ‘a good idea’ and ‘cohesive reality’; the 1995 *Star Wars* and *Empire* VHS interviews; *Starburst* #223 (March 1997) for Special Edition comments.

**Select Bibliography (p. 286)** and **Index (pp. 287–end)** follow. The index entries for Anakin Skywalker cluster on the prequel pages (Smith lists 228, 236–237, 241, 243, 247, 249, 252–254, 257, 263, 264); ‘Sith’ is indexed to p. 263.
